Rational Numbers, Irrational Numbers, and Decimal Patterns

Some decimals arrive: they stop, or they settle into a block that repeats forever. Others wander forever without a pattern. Today we sort the two, put irrational numbers in order, and turn a repeating decimal back into a fraction.

I can…
  • describe a decimal as repeating or non-repeating, and terminating or non-terminating
  • use a fraction’s denominator to predict whether its decimal terminates
  • classify a number as rational or irrational and give a reason
  • order irrational numbers on a number line by estimating them
  • convert a repeating decimal to a fraction with a calculator (and with algebra — extension)
AN2 Demonstrate an understanding of irrational numbers by representing, identifying and simplifying irrational numbers, and by ordering irrational numbers.

Terminology

Repeating decimal — a block of digits copies itself forever. Put a bar over the repeating block only.
0.414141\ldots=0.\overline{41}    2.8333\ldots=2.8\overline{3}
Non-repeating — never falls into a repeating block.
Terminating decimal — has a finite number of digits: it stops.
\dfrac14=0.25    \dfrac{17}{4}=4.25
Non-terminating — the digits go on forever.

Every decimal gets one label from each pair — but only three combinations ever happen:

Why does a fraction always terminate or repeat?

Divide one digit at a time and watch the remainders. Dividing by 7 can only leave a remainder of 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6 — so sooner or later we either get 0 (it stops) or a remainder comes back (and everything after it repeats).

Rational and Irrational Numbers

Rational number — can be written as a ratio \dfrac{a}{b} of two integers (b ≠ 0). As a decimal it terminates or repeats.
\dfrac{19}{8}=2.375   \dfrac{5}{11}=0.\overline{45}   -11=-\dfrac{11}{1}
Irrational number — can’t be written as a fraction of integers. Its decimal is non-terminating and non-repeating.
\sqrt{7}=2.6457513\ldots   \pi=3.14159\ldots
Quick test for a fraction in lowest terms: if the denominator’s prime factors are only 2s and 5s, the decimal terminates; otherwise it repeats. Either way, it’s rational.

Does it ever repeat? Wrap the digits and look for stripes

Write the digits in rows. If a block repeats, some row length lines the block up and every column becomes a single digit. Irrational numbers never line up — no matter how long the rows.

!
A pattern isn’t a repeating block. 7.12 112 1112 11112… has a pattern, but the groups keep growing, so no block ever repeats: irrational.
!
The calculator screen ends — the number doesn’t. √7 shows 2.645751311, but its digits go on forever without repeating.

Rational or irrational? Ask one question at a time

!
A root sign doesn’t make a number irrational. \sqrt{121}=11, \sqrt{2.89}=1.7 and \sqrt{0.25}=0.5 are all rational. Only roots of non-perfect squares are irrational.
Think People sometimes use \dfrac{22}{7} for π. Is \dfrac{22}{7} equal to π?
No. \dfrac{22}{7}=3.\overline{142857} repeats, so it’s rational. \pi=3.14159\ldots never repeats. They agree only to 3.14 — \dfrac{22}{7} is just close to π.

Chasing π with fractions

Around 250 BCE, the Greek mathematician Archimedes trapped a circle between two shapes with 96 sides, one inside it and one outside. That proved π is between 3\tfrac{10}{71} and 3\tfrac{1}{7}.

About 700 years later in China, Zu Chongzhi found \tfrac{355}{113}, which matches π to 6 decimal places. Nobody beat his work on π for about 900 years.

Write Archimedes’ two numbers as decimals. Which digits of π did he know for sure?
3\tfrac{10}{71}=3.1408\ldots and 3\tfrac{1}{7}=3.\overline{142857}. π is between them, so π = 3.14…: he knew the first two decimal places.

Ordering irrational numbers on a number line

1. Squeeze
Find the perfect squares on either side: 16<19<25, so 4<\sqrt{19}<5.
2. Estimate
Closer to 16 than 25, so a bit less than 4.5. Check with the calculator: \sqrt{19}\approx4.36.
3. Place
Mark each number between the right whole numbers, then read them left to right: smallest to largest.

Repeating Decimal → Fraction: Algebraic Method

Extension
1Let x equal the repeating decimal.
2Identify the repeating digits.
3Multiply both sides by the power of 10 that shifts the repeating block to the left of the decimal point.
4If non-repeating digits sit right after the point, also multiply Step 1 by the power of 10 that puts the block immediately right of the point.
5Subtract Step 4 from Step 3 (or Step 1 from Step 3 if there’s no Step 4). The repeating tails cancel; solve for x.
!
The tails only cancel if they line up. Both decimals must have the same digits after the point — that’s why 2.8\overline{3} uses 100x and 10x, not 100x and x.
Example 5

Finishing the procedure

A student is converting 3.0\overline{62} to a fraction. Steps 1–4 are done; complete Step 5.

Step 1. Let x=3.0\overline{62}.
Step 2. The repeating digits are 62.
Step 3. Move 62 left of the point: multiply by 10^3=1000
1000x=3062.\overline{62}
Step 4. Move 62 to sit just right of the point: multiply by 10
10x=30.\overline{62}
Step 5. Subtract, and solve for x.
The tails .\overline{62} line up and cancel: 1000x-10x=3062.\overline{62}-30.\overline{62}
990x=3032, so x=\dfrac{3032}{990}=\mathbf{\dfrac{1516}{495}} (divide by 2). Check: 1516 ÷ 495 = 3.0626262… ✓

Key ideas

Every decimal is terminating, repeating, or neither (non-terminating and non-repeating). The bar goes over the repeating block only: 2.8\overline{3}.
Rational = a fraction of integers = a decimal that terminates or repeats. Lowest-terms denominator of only 2s and 5s → it terminates.
Irrational = never ends, never repeats: roots of non-perfect squares, π. Order them by squeezing between perfect squares.
Repeating decimal → fraction: F◂▸D on the calculator (fill the screen), or algebra: multiply by powers of 10 so the tails cancel.
