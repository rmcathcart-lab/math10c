Classifying the Real Number System

Every number on the number line has a home. Today we sort numbers into N, W, I, Q and \overline{Q}, see why the sets sit inside each other, and use the perfect squares and cubes to estimate roots before the calculator does.

I can…
  • list every set a number belongs to (R, Q, \overline{Q}, I, W, N), from largest to smallest
  • explain why a rational number’s decimal ends or repeats, and an irrational number’s never does
  • estimate a square root or cube root from the perfect squares or cubes on either side, then check it on the calculator
  • estimate a square root to one significant digit by grouping digits in pairs
  • Extension find an absolute value as a distance, and graph an absolute value inequality
AN2 Demonstrate an understanding of irrational numbers by representing, identifying and simplifying irrational numbers, and by ordering irrational numbers.

1

The Real Number System

Divide one integer by another and only two things can happen: the decimal terminates, or it settles into a block of digits that repeats forever. Either way it’s a rational number — built from a ratio of integers. Decimals that never terminate and never repeat, like \pi and \sqrt{2}, are irrational numbers. Together they fill every point on the number line: the real numbers.

Natural numbers
N = \{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}
the counting numbers
Whole numbers
W = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\}
the naturals and 0
Integers
I = \{\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\}
wholes and their opposites
Rational numbers
Q = \left\{ \dfrac{a}{b},\ a, b \in I,\ b \neq 0 \right\}
fractions, and decimals that terminate or repeat
Irrational numbers
\overline{Q} = \{\text{non-terminating, non-repeating decimals}\}
\pi,\ \sqrt{2},\ \sqrt{11}
Real numbers
R = \{Q \text{ and } \overline{Q}\}
every point on the number line

Rational or irrational? Look at the decimal

Why can a fraction’s decimal only end or repeat? Watch the remainders in the long division.

!
A pattern isn’t a repeat. 1.010\,010\,001\ldots has a pattern, but no block of digits repeats, so it’s irrational.
!
Close to π isn’t π. \tfrac{22}{7} = 3.\overline{142857} repeats, so it’s rational. \pi never repeats.

The sorting machine

Each set sits inside the next: every natural number is whole, every whole number is an integer, every integer is rational. The irrationals sit beside the rationals — no number is both. To sort a number, simplify it first, then test from the outside in.

!
A root sign doesn’t make a number irrational. \sqrt{9} = 3, \sqrt{\tfrac14} = \tfrac12 and \sqrt[3]{-8} = -2 are all rational. Only roots that don’t come out exactly, like \sqrt{10}, are irrational.
Think There is exactly one number in W that is not in N. Which one? And why is no number in both Q and \overline{Q}?
0 is whole but not natural. A decimal can’t both repeat (or end) and never repeat, so Q and \overline{Q} never overlap.
Example 1

Naming every set a number belongs to

(a) For each number below, list every set (from N, W, I, Q, \overline{Q}, R) that it belongs to, ordered from the largest set to the smallest set.

(i) 12
A positive whole count, and 12 = \tfrac{12}{1}.
\boldsymbol{R,\ Q,\ I,\ W,\ N}
(ii) \dfrac{5}{9}
A ratio of two integers, but not a whole count.
\boldsymbol{R,\ Q}
(iii) \sqrt{11}
11 isn’t a perfect square (3^2 = 9,\ 4^2 = 16): never ends, never repeats.
\boldsymbol{R,\ \overline{Q}}
(iv) -8
Negative, so not natural or whole — but still an integer.
\boldsymbol{R,\ Q,\ I}
(b) Explain why 12 belongs to five different number sets, while -12 belongs to only three.
12 is positive, so it passes the natural and whole tests as well as being an integer, rational and real: five sets. −12 is negative, so it fails the natural and whole tests; it is still an integer, rational and real: three sets (R, Q, I).

Square Roots

Every positive real number has two square roots, one positive and one negative. The positive one is the principal square root, and it’s what the symbol \sqrt{\ \ } always means.
The square roots of 49 are 7 and −7, but \sqrt{49} = 7 only.
Perfect square → rational root
\sqrt{36} = 6 \qquad \sqrt{0.25} = 0.5
Not a perfect square → irrational root
\sqrt{23} = 4.795\,831\,5\ldots \qquad -\sqrt{23}
Know your perfect squares — they’re how we estimate. Tap a blank to fill it.
Check it on the calculator. 2ndx²53ENTER gives \sqrt{53} = 7.280\ldots ≈ 7.3. Estimate first, so you’ll notice a typing mistake.
!
\sqrt{49} = 7, not ±7. The symbol means the principal (positive) root. “The square roots of 49” are 7 and −7.
!
-\sqrt{7} isn’t \sqrt{-7}. -\sqrt{7} \approx -2.6 is real. No real number squares to −7, so \sqrt{-7} isn’t real.

Estimating to one significant digit

For a very large (or very small) number, group its digits in pairs, starting from the decimal point and going both ways. Each pair gives one digit of the square root.

√40000=200
√0.0004=0.02
each pair → one digit of the root · the decimal point stays put
Example 3

Estimating to one significant digit

Estimate each square root to one significant digit.

(a) \sqrt{7\,340}
Pairs 73\ 40: two digits. 73 is near 81 = 9^2.
\sqrt{7\,340} = 85.67\ldots \approx \mathbf{90}
(b) \sqrt{51\,234.6}
Groups 5\ 12\ 34\,.6: three digits. 5 is near 4 = 2^2.
\sqrt{51\,234.6} = 226.35\ldots \approx \mathbf{200}
(c) \sqrt{0.005\,12}
0.005\,12 = 51.2 \times 10^{-4}, \sqrt{51.2} \approx 7
\approx 7 \times 10^{-2} = \mathbf{0.07} (calc: 0.0716)
(d) \sqrt{0.051\,2}
0.051\,2 = 5.12 \times 10^{-2}, \sqrt{5.12} \approx 2
\approx 2 \times 10^{-1} = \mathbf{0.2} (calc: 0.2263)
!
Pair from the decimal point, not from the left. 51 234.6 groups as 5 | 12 | 34 . 6, so its root starts with √5 ≈ 2, not √51 ≈ 7.

Cube Roots

Every real number — positive or negative — has exactly one cube root, written \sqrt[3]{\ \ }.
The cube root of a perfect cube is rational: \sqrt[3]{8\,000} = 20,\ \ \sqrt[3]{-64} = -4. Otherwise it’s irrational, like \sqrt[3]{15}.
Know your perfect cubes — tap a blank to fill it.
Cube roots on the calculator. Type the index first: 32nd^30ENTER gives \sqrt[3]{30} = 3.107\ldots
Negatives work too. Use the (−) key, not −: 32nd^(−)64ENTER gives -4.
!
Square root vs cube root. \sqrt{-64} isn’t real, but \sqrt[3]{-64} = -4 because (-4)^3 = -64.
!
Use the cube table. \sqrt[3]{50} is between 3 and 4 (27 and 64) — not 7! That’s \sqrt{50}.

Estimating expressions with cube roots

Same plan as before: estimate each root from the perfect squares or cubes on either side, do the arithmetic, then check. Read the index carefully — \sqrt{50} and \sqrt[3]{50} are very different numbers.

Extension — Absolute Value

The absolute value of a real number is the principal square root of its square. Geometrically, \lvert a \rvert is the distance of a from 0.
\lvert 8 \rvert = \sqrt{(8)^2} = \sqrt{64} = 8 \qquad \lvert -8 \rvert = \sqrt{(-8)^2} = \sqrt{64} = 8
!
The bars only act on what’s inside them. \lvert -10 \rvert = 10, but -\lvert -10 \rvert = -10: the minus sign outside still applies.

Graphing absolute value inequalities

To graph \lvert a \rvert \lt 3,\ a \in R, we need every point less than 3 units from 0. The endpoints themselves aren’t included, so they’re open.

Between the points → \lvert x \rvert \lt k or \lvert x \rvert \le k
Outside the points → \lvert x \rvert \gt k or \lvert x \rvert \ge k
Open circle → \lt or \gt   Solid dot → \le or \ge
Example 5

Absolute value and its graphs

Evaluate each of the following.

(a) \lvert -9 \rvert + \lvert 9 \rvert
\lvert -9 \rvert = 9 and \lvert 9 \rvert = 9
9 + 9 = \mathbf{18}
(b) \lvert 2 - 9 \rvert
Inside the bars first: 2 - 9 = -7
\lvert -7 \rvert = \mathbf{7}
(c) -\lvert -\sqrt{100} \rvert
-\sqrt{100} = -10 and \lvert -10 \rvert = 10
-\lvert -\sqrt{100} \rvert = \mathbf{-10}

(d)

\boldsymbol{\lvert x \rvert \le 5}

Key ideas

The sets nest: N inside W inside I inside Q inside R. \overline{Q} sits beside Q; together they make R.
Rational decimals terminate or repeat. Irrational decimals never end and never repeat. Simplify first: \sqrt{9} = 3 is rational.
Estimate a root from the perfect squares or cubes on either side, then check on the calculator. \sqrt{\ \ } means the positive root; every number has one cube root.
One significant digit: pair digits from the decimal point. Absolute value is distance from 0, so it’s never negative.
