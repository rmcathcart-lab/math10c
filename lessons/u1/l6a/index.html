Entire and Mixed Radicals

\sqrt{50} and 5\sqrt{2} are the same number written two ways. Today we run the product rule backwards to pull the largest perfect square out in front of the root, push numbers back under it, compare radicals without a calculator, and keep Pythagorean answers exact.

I can…
  • split a square root into a product or a quotient of two square roots
  • tell an entire radical from a mixed radical
  • convert an entire radical to a mixed radical in simplest form, using the largest perfect square
  • convert a mixed radical to an entire radical
  • order radicals without a calculator, and give exact answers with the Pythagorean theorem
AN2 Demonstrate an understanding of irrational numbers by representing, identifying and simplifying irrational numbers, and by ordering irrational numbers.

1

Splitting a Radical Apart

Product rule
\sqrt{ab}=\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{b}
for a,b\ge0
Quotient rule
\sqrt{\dfrac{a}{b}}=\dfrac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}}
for a\ge0,\ b>0

Any square root can be rewritten as a product or quotient of two smaller square roots, once we choose a sensible split.

Example 1

Splitting one root into two

Rewrite each as a product or quotient of two square roots.

(a) \sqrt{63}=\sqrt{9\times\underline{\phantom{000}}}
=\sqrt{\underline{\phantom{000}}}\times\sqrt{\underline{\phantom{000}}}
63=9\times\mathbf{7}, and 9 is a perfect square.
\sqrt{63}=\mathbf{\sqrt{9}\times\sqrt{7}}
(b) \sqrt{32}=\sqrt{16\times\underline{\phantom{000}}}
=\sqrt{\underline{\phantom{000}}}\times\sqrt{\underline{\phantom{000}}}
32=16\times\mathbf{2}, and 16 is a perfect square.
\sqrt{32}=\mathbf{\sqrt{16}\times\sqrt{2}}
(c) \sqrt{\dfrac{17}{9}}=\dfrac{\sqrt{\underline{\phantom{000}}}}{\sqrt{\underline{\phantom{000}}}}
Quotient rule, straight off:
\sqrt{\tfrac{17}{9}}=\dfrac{\sqrt{17}}{\sqrt{9}}=\mathbf{\dfrac{\sqrt{17}}{3}}

Fill in the blanks to see why all three are the same number.

Entire (pure) radical: the number sits alone under the root sign.
\sqrt{31}\qquad\sqrt{150}\qquad\sqrt[3]{19}
Mixed radical: a coefficient sits outside, multiplying the root.
\tfrac{5}{6}\sqrt{3}\qquad7\sqrt{11}\qquad-4\sqrt[3]{19}
Every mixed radical with a positive coefficient can be rewritten as an entire radical (a negative stays outside: -7\sqrt{5}=-\sqrt{245}). An entire square root can be rewritten as a mixed radical exactly when its radicand has a perfect-square factor: \sqrt{30} can’t, because 30=2\times3\times5.

What does a mixed radical like 5\sqrt{2} actually mean?

A mixed radical is copies of a smaller root: 5\sqrt{2} is five \sqrt{2}’s laid end to end, and 6\sqrt{5} is six \sqrt{5}’s. Cut a square into equal smaller squares and you can see it.

Can’t spot the largest square? Use prime factors.

Break the radicand into primes. A pair of matching primes is a perfect square (3\times3=9), so each pair leaves the root as one number.

Example 2

Entire → mixed

Convert each radical to a mixed radical in simplest form.

(a) \sqrt{72}
Largest square factor of 72 is 36.
\sqrt{36\times2}=\sqrt{36}\times\sqrt{2}=\mathbf{6\sqrt{2}}
(b) \sqrt{63}
Largest square factor of 63 is 9.
\sqrt{9\times7}=\sqrt{9}\times\sqrt{7}=\mathbf{3\sqrt{7}}
(c) \sqrt{288}
Largest square factor of 288 is 144.
\sqrt{144\times2}=\sqrt{144}\times\sqrt{2}=\mathbf{12\sqrt{2}}
Think Ali wrote \sqrt{72}=2\sqrt{18}. Is he right? Is he finished?
It’s true (\sqrt{4}\times\sqrt{18}=\sqrt{72}) but not finished: 18 = 9 × 2 still has a perfect square. 2\sqrt{18}=2\times3\sqrt{2}=\mathbf{6\sqrt{2}}. Using 36 first does it in one step.

Mixed → entire

Convert each mixed radical to an entire radical.

(a) 3\sqrt{8}
3^2=9;\ 9\times8=72
=\mathbf{\sqrt{72}}
(b) 5\sqrt{6}
5^2=25;\ 25\times6=150
=\mathbf{\sqrt{150}}
(c) 9\sqrt{5}
9^2=81;\ 81\times5=405
=\mathbf{\sqrt{405}}
(d) \tfrac{5}{2}\sqrt{8}
\left(\tfrac{5}{2}\right)^2=\tfrac{25}{4};\ \tfrac{25}{4}\times8=50
=\mathbf{\sqrt{50}}
(e) 0.6\sqrt{75}
0.6^2=0.36;\ 0.36\times75=27
=\mathbf{\sqrt{27}}
(f) -7\sqrt{5}
The − stays out front. 7^2\times5=245
=\mathbf{-\sqrt{245}}
5

Comparing Radicals

To compare mixed radicals without a calculator, convert them all to entire radicals. Then compare the numbers under the root signs: bigger radicand, bigger value.
!
Don’t rank by the number in front. Is 3\sqrt{2} bigger than 2\sqrt{11} because 3 > 2? No: 3\sqrt{2}=\sqrt{18} but 2\sqrt{11}=\sqrt{44}.
Example 5

Ordering without a calculator

Arrange in order from greatest to least:   \text{i) }6\sqrt{3}\qquad\text{ii) }4\sqrt{5}\qquad\text{iii) }\sqrt{40}\qquad\text{iv) }3\sqrt{6}\qquad\text{v) }2\sqrt{11}

Convert to entire form: 6\sqrt{3}=\sqrt{108}, 4\sqrt{5}=\sqrt{80}, \sqrt{40}, 3\sqrt{6}=\sqrt{54}, 2\sqrt{11}=\sqrt{44}.
Radicands: 108 > 80 > 54 > 44 > 40, so \mathbf{6\sqrt{3}\ >\ 4\sqrt{5}\ >\ 3\sqrt{6}\ >\ 2\sqrt{11}\ >\ \sqrt{40}}
6

Radicals in the Pythagorean Theorem

Lengths found with the Pythagorean theorem, c^2=a^2+b^2, are often irrational. So answers are usually left exact, in simplest mixed radical form, instead of rounded.

!
Roots don’t split over +. \sqrt{4+16}=\sqrt{20}\approx4.47, but \sqrt{4}+\sqrt{16}=6. The product and quotient rules work for × and ÷ only.
Example 6

Exact side length

In the diagram, AD is the altitude from A to BC. Use the Pythagorean theorem to find the exact length of AB.

(a) Express AB as an exact value in simplest mixed radical form.
△ADC: AD^2=13^2-5^2=144, so AD=12
△ADB: AB^2=12^2+4^2=160
AB=\sqrt{16\times10}=\mathbf{4\sqrt{10}}
(b) Express AB as a decimal, rounded to the nearest hundredth.
4\sqrt{10}=4\times3.16227\ldots=12.649\ldots
AB\approx\mathbf{12.65}

Key ideas

Split a root over × and ÷, never over + or −:
\sqrt{ab}=\sqrt{a}\times\sqrt{b}\qquad\sqrt{\tfrac{a}{b}}=\tfrac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}}
Entire \sqrt{180} and mixed 6\sqrt{5} are the same number. 6\sqrt{5} means six \sqrt{5}’s.
Entire → mixed: pull out the largest perfect square. \sqrt{72}=\sqrt{36}\times\sqrt{2}=6\sqrt{2}
Mixed → entire: square the coefficient and bring it in: 4\sqrt{11}=\sqrt{176}. Use it to order radicals. Keep Pythagorean answers exact.
