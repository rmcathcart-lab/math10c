Prime Factorization at Work: GCF, LCM, and Perfect Powers

Once a number is broken into primes, a lot of questions get easy. Today prime factors give us a fast, reliable route to the greatest common factor, the least common multiple, and to perfect squares and cubes.

I can…
  • find the greatest common factor of a group of numbers, by inspection and by prime factorization
  • find the least common multiple of a group of numbers, by listing multiples and by prime factorization
  • use prime factorization to decide whether a number is a perfect square or a perfect cube, and find its square root or cube root
  • check GCF, LCM, square roots and cube roots on the calculator
AN1 Demonstrate an understanding of factors of whole numbers by determining the prime factors, greatest common factor, least common multiple, square root and cube root.
Warm-up

The Locker Problem

100 closed lockers, 100 students. Student 1 toggles every locker, Student 2 every 2nd one, Student 3 every 3rd one, …, Student 100 just #100. (Toggle = open it if it’s closed, close it if it’s open.)

1
Which students touch locker 12?
Students 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 — exactly the factors of 12.
2
Does locker 12 end open or closed?
Closed. 6 toggles: open, closed, open, closed, open, closed.
3
Which students touch locker 9? Is it open or closed at the end?
Students 1, 3, 9. Three toggles, so it ends open.
!
The big one. After all 100 students have had a turn, how many lockers are open? Guess first.
A 50
B 25
C 10
D 0
C — 10 lockers: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100 (press All 100 to watch). A locker ends open only if it’s toggled an odd number of times — it has an odd number of factors. Factors come in pairs (12 = 1 × 12 = 2 × 6 = 3 × 4), but a perfect square has one pair that is the same number twice (9 = 1 × 9 = 3 × 3), so it has an odd number of factors.
A (50)? That’s just the picture after Student 2.B (25)? There are 25 primes up to 100 — but a prime has exactly 2 factors, so it ends closed.D (0)? Not everything gets flipped back!

Greatest Common Factor

The greatest common factor (GCF) of a group of whole numbers is the largest whole number that divides evenly into every member of the group.
The GCF of 10, 15 and 25 is 5.

Back to the lockers: which students touch both locker 60 and locker 90? Exactly the numbers that are factors of both — the common factors. The last student to touch both is the greatest common factor.

!
Sanity check. The GCF can never be bigger than the smallest number in the group — it has to fit into that number.
Example 1

GCF by inspection

State the greatest common factor of each group.

(a) 16, 24, and 40
16 = 24, 24 = 23 × 3, 40 = 23 × 5
only 2 is in all three, lowest power 23
GCF = 8
(b) 21 and 28
21 = 3 × 7, 28 = 22 × 7
the only common prime is 7
GCF = 7
(c) 48 and 72
48 = 24 × 3, 72 = 23 × 32
lower power of each: 23 × 3
GCF = 24

A shortcut: prime factorization

Inspection gets hard once a number has lots of factors, like in (c). Prime factors give a shortcut. From the starter:

Division ladder: 60
260230315551
Division ladder: 90
290345315551
Factor tree: 60
To find the GCF, multiply together every prime factor (matching repeats) that appears in every factorization.

60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5     90 = 2 × 3 × 3 × 5both share one 2, one 3 and one 5, so the GCF of 60 and 90 is 2 × 3 × 5 = 30

With exponents: the lowest power

The GCF uses only the primes that appear in every factorization, each to its lowest power. Here 60 = 22 × 3 × 5 and 90 = 2 × 32 × 5, so

GCF = 21 × 31 × 51 = 30

Check a GCF by dividing. Every number in the group ÷ the GCF should be a whole number: 396÷44ENTER gives 9. If the answers (here 3, 5, 7, 9) share no common factor, nothing bigger will work.

Picture it: the biggest square tile

Cover a 60 by 90 rectangle with identical square tiles, none cut. A tile only fits if its side divides both sides of the rectangle — it must be a common factor. The biggest tile that fits is the GCF.

2

Least Common Multiple

The least common multiple (LCM) of a group of numbers is the smallest number that appears in every list of multiples.

9: 9, 18, 27, 36, 45, …     12: 12, 24, 36, 48, …the first number in both lists is 36, so the LCM of 9 and 12 is 36

!
Multiplying gives a common multiple — not always the least. 6 × 8 = 48, but 6 and 8 both land on 24 first.
!
Sanity check. The LCM can never be smaller than the largest number in the group.
Example 3

LCM by listing multiples

State the least common multiple of each group.

(a) 5 and 9
5 and 9 share no common factor (except 1), so the LCM is their product
LCM = 45
(b) 6, 8, and 12
multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, …
24 is also a multiple of 6 and of 12
LCM = 24
(c) 15, 18, and 21
15 = 3 × 5, 18 = 2 × 32, 21 = 3 × 7
highest powers: 2, 32, 5, 7
LCM = 2 × 9 × 5 × 7 = 630

A shortcut: prime factorization

Trial and error gets slow once the numbers grow, like in (c). Prime factors make it manageable:

15 = 3 × 5     18 = 2 × 3 × 3     21 = 3 × 7

To build the LCM, start with all the prime factors of one number, then bring in any extra factors needed from the others (factors already counted are not repeated).
3 and 5 from 15, an extra 2 and 3 from 18, a 7 from 21:  3 × 5 × 2 × 3 × 7 = 630

With exponents: the highest power

The LCM uses every prime that appears in any of the factorizations, each to its highest power. Here 15 = 3 × 5, 18 = 2 × 32 and 21 = 3 × 7, so

LCM = 2 × 32 × 5 × 7 = 630

Check an LCM by dividing. The LCM ÷ each number should be whole: 630÷21ENTER gives 30. (630 ÷ 15 = 42 and 630 ÷ 18 = 35 too.)
Example 4

LCM by prime factorization

Use prime factorization to determine the least common multiple of each group.

(a) 18 and 45
18 = 2 × 32, 45 = 32 × 5
highest: 2, 32, 5
LCM = 2 × 9 × 5 = 90
(b) 84 and 126
84 = 22 × 3 × 7, 126 = 2 × 32 × 7
highest: 22, 32, 7
LCM = 4 × 9 × 7 = 252
(c) 20, 45, and 75
20 = 22 × 5, 45 = 32 × 5, 75 = 3 × 52
highest: 22, 32, 52
LCM = 4 × 9 × 25 = 900
Think For 60 and 90: GCF × LCM = 30 × 180. Compare that with 60 × 90. Coincidence? Use the Venn diagram to explain.
Both are 5 400. The GCF is the middle of the Venn diagram and the LCM is everything, so GCF × LCM uses the middle twice plus each side once — exactly the primes of 60 times the primes of 90.
Fun fact1803 · 2024 · 2245

The cicadas that keep an LCM calendar

Periodical cicadas live underground for years, then millions climb out in the same spring. Brood XIX comes out every 13 years. Brood XIII comes out every 17 years.

In 2024 they came out together, in a strip of central Illinois: the first time since 1803, when Thomas Jefferson was president.

How many years apart are the times they come out together? When is the next one?
13 and 17 are both prime, so LCM = 13 × 17 = 221 years. The next time is 2024 + 221 = 2245.

Quick reference: GCF or LCM?

GCF greatest common factor
  • the biggest number that divides into all of them
  • primes in every factorization
  • each to its lowest power
  • the middle of the Venn diagram
  • never bigger than the smallest number
60 and 90 → 30
LCM least common multiple
  • the smallest number they all divide into
  • primes from any factorization
  • each to its highest power
  • everything in the Venn diagram
  • never smaller than the largest number
60 and 90 → 180

Prime Factorization of a Perfect Square

Numbers such as 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, … — each the square of a whole number — are perfect squares. \sqrt{\phantom{0}} means the positive, or principal, square root.
Factor a perfect square into primes and every prime appears an even number of times — the prime factors pair up perfectly.
Concretely: a perfect square is a number of square tiles that can be arranged into one solid square. 16 tiles make a 4 × 4 square, so 16 is a perfect square. 12 tiles only ever make rectangles (1 × 12, 2 × 6, 3 × 4), so 12 is not.
16 = 4 × 4 a square
12 = 3 × 4 not a square

Three perfect squares, factored and paired up:

4 = 2 × 2
100 = 2 × 2 × 5 × 5
144 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3
\sqrt{4} = 2
\sqrt{100} = 2\times5 = 10
\sqrt{144} = 2\times2\times3 = 12

Take one factor from each pair. If the prime factorization does not split into matching pairs, the number is not a perfect square.

!
The square root is not half. \sqrt{144} is 12, not 72. You halve the exponents: 144 = 24 × 32 → 22 × 31 = 12.
!
Even exponents, not even primes. 9 = 32 is a perfect square. What matters is how many times each prime appears.
Square root on the TI-30XIIS. \sqrt{\phantom{0}} is the 2nd function of x²: 2ndx²144ENTER gives 12. A whole-number answer means a perfect square.
Check it backwards. Square the answer with x²: 12x²ENTER should give back 144.

Example 6

Testing for a perfect square

In each case, use prime factorization to decide whether the number is a perfect square. If it is, state its square root.

(a) 1 089
1 089 ÷ 3 = 363, ÷ 3 = 121, ÷ 11 = 11, ÷ 11 = 1
1 089 = 32 × 112 — both exponents even
perfect square: \sqrt{1\,089} = 3 × 11 = 33
(b) 2 028
2 028 ÷ 2 = 1 014, ÷ 2 = 507, ÷ 3 = 169, ÷ 13 = 13, ÷ 13 = 1
2 028 = 22 × 3 × 132 — the 3 has no partner (odd exponent)
not a perfect square
Think Back to the warm-up: why were the lockers left open exactly the perfect squares?
A locker is toggled once for each of its factors. Factors come in pairs, except a perfect square’s middle pair (6 × 6 for 36), which counts once. So only perfect squares have an odd number of factors and end open.

Prime Factorization of a Perfect Cube

Perfect cubes of whole numbers include 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, …, with cube roots 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, …
In the prime factorization of a perfect cube, every prime factor appears a multiple of three times — the factors group into matching triples.
Concretely: a perfect cube is a number of small cubes that can be stacked into one solid larger cube. 27 cubes make a 3 × 3 × 3 cube, so 27 is a perfect cube. 18 cubes can’t be stacked into a solid cube (the best is a 3 × 3 × 2 box), so 18 is not.
27 = 3 × 3 × 3 a cube
18 = 3 × 3 × 2 a box

Three perfect cubes, factored and grouped into triples:

8 = 2 × 2 × 2
125 = 5 × 5 × 5
1 000 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 5 × 5 × 5
\sqrt[3]{8} = 2
\sqrt[3]{125} = 5
\sqrt[3]{1\,000} = 2\times5 = 10

Take one factor from each triple. If the prime factorization does not break into matching triples, the number is not a perfect cube.

Cube root on the TI-30XIIS. Type the 3 first, then \sqrt[x]{\phantom{0}} (2nd of ^), then the number: 32nd^1000ENTER gives 10.
Check it backwards. Cube the answer: 10^3ENTER should give back 1 000.

Square, cube, both, or neither?

Write the number with exponents and look only at the exponents: all even → perfect square; all multiples of 3 → perfect cube.

Example 7

Testing for a perfect cube

In each case, use prime factorization (a division ladder works well) to determine whether the number is a perfect cube. If it is, state its cube root.

(a) 5 832
5 832 ÷ 2 ÷ 2 ÷ 2 = 729, then ÷ 3 six times: 729 → 243 → 81 → 27 → 9 → 3 → 1
5 832 = 23 × 36 — both exponents are multiples of 3
perfect cube: \sqrt[3]{5\,832} = 2 × 32 = 18
(b) 39 304
39 304 ÷ 2 = 19 652, ÷ 2 = 9 826, ÷ 2 = 4 913, ÷ 17 = 289, ÷ 17 = 17, ÷ 17 = 1
39 304 = 23 × 173
perfect cube: \sqrt[3]{39\,304} = 2 × 17 = 34
Check (a) on the calculator: 32nd^5832ENTER — a whole number means a perfect cube.

Key ideas

GCF: the primes in every factorization, each to its lowest power. 60 = 22 × 3 × 5, 90 = 2 × 32 × 5 → GCF = 30.
LCM: every prime from any factorization, each to its highest power. 60 and 90 → LCM = 22 × 32 × 5 = 180.
Perfect square: every exponent is even (primes pair up). Halve the exponents for the square root: \sqrt{144} = 2^2\times3 = 12.
Perfect cube: every exponent is a multiple of 3 (triples). Divide the exponents by 3: \sqrt[3]{1\,000} = 2\times5 = 10.
