Perfect Squares and Perfect Cubes

Write a number as a product of primes and its exponents tell you at a glance whether it is a perfect square or a perfect cube — and hand you its square root or cube root. Today we see why that works, and put it to use.

I can…
  • use prime factorization to decide whether a number is a perfect square or a perfect cube, and explain why the exponents must be even or multiples of 3
  • find a square root or cube root from the prime factorization, and check it on the calculator
  • decide whether a number is a square, a cube, both, or neither
  • find the smallest multiplier that makes a perfect square or cube, and the side of a square or cube from its area or volume
AN1 Demonstrate an understanding of factors of whole numbers by determining the prime factors, greatest common factor, least common multiple, square root and cube root.
Warm-up

The Locker Problem

100 closed lockers, 100 students. Student 1 toggles every locker, Student 2 every 2nd one, Student 3 every 3rd one, …, Student 100 just #100. (Toggle = open it if it’s closed, close it if it’s open.)

1
Which students touch locker 12?
Students 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 — exactly the factors of 12.
2
Does locker 12 end open or closed?
Closed. 6 toggles: open, closed, open, closed, open, closed.
3
Which students touch locker 9? Is it open or closed at the end?
Students 1, 3, 9. Three toggles, so it ends open.
!
The big one. After all 100 students have had a turn, how many lockers are open? Guess first.
A 50
B 25
C 10
D 0
C — 10 lockers: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100 (press All 100 to watch). A locker ends open only if it’s toggled an odd number of times — it has an odd number of factors. Factors come in pairs (12 = 1 × 12 = 2 × 6 = 3 × 4), but a perfect square has one pair that is the same number twice (9 = 1 × 9 = 3 × 3), so it has an odd number of factors.
A (50)? That’s just the picture after Student 2.B (25)? There are 25 primes up to 100 — but a prime has exactly 2 factors, so it ends closed.D (0)? Not everything gets flipped back!

1

Prime Factorization of a Perfect Square

Numbers such as 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, … — each the square of a whole number — are perfect squares. \sqrt{\phantom{0}} means the positive, or principal, square root.
Factor a perfect square into primes and every prime appears an even number of times — the prime factors pair up perfectly.
Concretely: a perfect square is a number of square tiles that can be arranged into one solid square. 16 tiles make a 4 × 4 square, so 16 is a perfect square. 12 tiles only ever make rectangles (1 × 12, 2 × 6, 3 × 4), so 12 is not.
16 = 4 × 4 a square
12 = 3 × 4 not a square
Why even? A perfect square is a whole number times itself, so its factorization writes every prime factor down twice: from the starter, 122 = (22 × 3) × (22 × 3) = 24 × 32. Squaring doubles every exponent, and double a whole number is always even.

Squaring doubles every exponent; cubing triples it

Three perfect squares, factored and paired up:

4 = 2 × 2
100 = 2 × 2 × 5 × 5
144 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3
\sqrt{4} = 2
\sqrt{100} = 2\times5 = 10
\sqrt{144} = 2\times2\times3 = 12

Take one factor from each pair. If the prime factorization does not split into matching pairs, the number is not a perfect square.

!
The square root is not half. \sqrt{144} is 12, not 72. You halve the exponents: 144 = 24 × 32 → 22 × 31 = 12.
!
Even exponents, not even primes. 9 = 32 is a perfect square. What matters is how many times each prime appears.
Square root on the TI-30XIIS. \sqrt{\phantom{0}} is the 2nd function of x²: 2ndx²144ENTER gives 12. A whole-number answer means a perfect square.
Check it backwards. Square the answer with x²: 12x²ENTER should give back 144.

Think Back to the warm-up: why were the lockers left open exactly the perfect squares?
A locker is toggled once for each of its factors. Factors come in pairs, except a perfect square’s middle pair (6 × 6 for 36), which counts once. So only perfect squares have an odd number of factors and end open.
2

Prime Factorization of a Perfect Cube

Perfect cubes of whole numbers include 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, …, with cube roots 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, …
In the prime factorization of a perfect cube, every prime factor appears a multiple of three times — the factors group into matching triples.
Concretely: a perfect cube is a number of small cubes that can be stacked into one solid larger cube. 27 cubes make a 3 × 3 × 3 cube, so 27 is a perfect cube. 18 cubes can’t be stacked into a solid cube (the best is a 3 × 3 × 2 box), so 18 is not.
27 = 3 × 3 × 3 a cube
18 = 3 × 3 × 2 a box
Why multiples of 3? Cubing writes every prime factor down three times: 123 = (22 × 3) × (22 × 3) × (22 × 3) = 26 × 33. Every exponent triples.

Three perfect cubes, factored and grouped into triples:

8 = 2 × 2 × 2
125 = 5 × 5 × 5
1 000 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 5 × 5 × 5
\sqrt[3]{8} = 2
\sqrt[3]{125} = 5
\sqrt[3]{1\,000} = 2\times5 = 10

Take one factor from each triple. If the prime factorization does not break into matching triples, the number is not a perfect cube.

Cube root on the TI-30XIIS. Type the 3 first, then \sqrt[x]{\phantom{0}} (2nd of ^), then the number: 32nd^1000ENTER gives 10.
Check it backwards. Cube the answer: 10^3ENTER should give back 1 000.

Square, Cube, Both, or Neither?

Write the number with exponents and look only at the exponents: all even → perfect square; all multiples of 3 → perfect cube; all multiples of 6 (even and multiples of 3) → both. For example 64 = 26: \sqrt{64} = 2^3 = 8 and \sqrt[3]{64} = 2^2 = 4.

Making a Perfect Square or a Perfect Cube

When an exponent is odd, the number is not a perfect square — but multiplying by the right primes fixes it. For the smallest multiplier, raise each odd exponent to the next even number, and nothing more. (For a perfect cube, raise each exponent to the next multiple of 3.)

50 = 2 × 52  →  50 × 2 = 100 = 22 × 52 = 102the 2 had no partner, so one more 2 is all it takes

Example 5

The smallest multiplier

Consider the number 72.

(a) Find the smallest whole number 72 must be multiplied by to give a perfect square, and state the square root of the result.
72 = 23 × 32: 23 → 24 needs one more 2
multiply by 2: 72 × 2 = 144 = 24 × 32
\sqrt{144} = 22 × 3 = 12
(b) Find the smallest whole number 72 must be multiplied by to give a perfect cube, and state the cube root of the result.
72 = 23 × 32: 23 is fine, 32 → 33 needs one more 3
multiply by 3: 72 × 3 = 216 = 23 × 33
\sqrt[3]{216} = 2 × 3 = 6
!
Smallest, not just any. 72 × 72 is a perfect square too, but multiplying by the number itself is almost never the smallest multiplier. Fix only the exponents that need it.
5

Squares and Cubes in Geometry

The area of a square is side × side, so the side length is \sqrt{\text{area}}. The volume of a cube is edge × edge × edge, so the edge length is \sqrt[3]{\text{volume}}. Prime factorization finds these roots exactly; the calculator checks them.

Example 6

Side lengths from area and volume

(a) A square poster has an area of 3 136 cm2. Use prime factorization to find its side length.
3 136 = 26 × 72 (all exponents even)
side = \sqrt{3\,136} = 23 × 7
56 cm
(b) A cube-shaped block of ice has a volume of 3 375 cm3. Use prime factorization to find the length of each edge.
3 375 = 33 × 53 (all exponents multiples of 3)
edge = \sqrt[3]{3\,375} = 3 × 5
15 cm
Check (b) on the calculator: 32nd^3375ENTER gives 15.
✓

Key ideas

Perfect square: every exponent is even (squaring doubles them). Halve the exponents for the square root: \sqrt{144} = \sqrt{2^4\times3^2} = 2^2\times3 = 12.
Perfect cube: every exponent is a multiple of 3 (cubing triples them). Divide the exponents by 3: \sqrt[3]{1\,000} = 2\times5 = 10. Both: multiples of 6.
Smallest multiplier: raise each odd exponent to the next even number (for a cube, the next multiple of 3) — nothing more. 72 = 23 × 32 → × 2 = 144.
Geometry: side of a square = \sqrt{\text{area}}; edge of a cube = \sqrt[3]{\text{volume}}. 3 375 cm3 = 33 × 53 → edge 15 cm.
