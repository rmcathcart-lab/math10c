Common Factors: Binomial Common Factors and Grouping

The common factor can be a whole bracket, and pairing up the terms of a polynomial can create one. In Lesson 1 we pulled out 3x from 6x^2+9x. Today the thing we pull out is a whole block like (x+3), and grouping is how we make that block appear.

I can…
  • factor out a binomial common factor, treating the whole bracket as one block
  • check whether the leftover bracket still has a common factor, and spot opposite brackets like (7-x) and (x-7)
  • factor a four-term polynomial by grouping: pair up, pull out, pull out again
  • rearrange the terms when the first pairing doesn’t give matching brackets
  • check every factorization by expanding it back
AN5 Demonstrate an understanding of common factors and trinomial factoring, concretely, pictorially and symbolically.

Factoring Out a Binomial Common Factor

In Lesson 1 the common factor was a monomial: 6x^2+9x=3x(2x+3). Exactly the same idea works when the common factor is a whole binomial.

The common factor may be a whole binomial rather than a single term; pull that bracket out exactly as you would a monomial. In 5x(x+2)-3(x+2) the quantity (x+2) multiplies both 5x and -3, so 5x(x+2)-3(x+2)=(x+2)(5x-3).
Lesson 1: a monomial
\underbrace{3x}\cdot 2x+\underbrace{3x}\cdot 3=3x(2x+3)
same move
Today: a whole bracket
5x\cdot\underbrace{(x+2)}-3\cdot\underbrace{(x+2)}=(x+2)(5x-3)
!
Don’t lose the 1. In 2x(x+5)+(x+5) the second term is 1\cdot(x+5). Pulling out (x+5) leaves 2x and 1, so the answer is (x+5)(2x+1), not (x+5)(2x).
Example 1

Removing a common binomial factor

Factor each polynomial by removing the common binomial factor.

(a) (5x)(x+8)-(2)(x+8)
(x+8) multiplies both terms:
=\boldsymbol{(x+8)(5x-2)}
(b) 5(2-3y)+4y(2-3y)
(2-3y) multiplies both terms:
=\boldsymbol{(2-3y)(5+4y)}
(c) 8a(3a+1)+(3a+1)
Lone bracket =1\cdot(3a+1):
=\boldsymbol{(3a+1)(8a+1)}

Then check the leftover bracket

After the binomial is removed, check whether the leftover bracket has a common factor of its own. A factorization is only finished when no bracket can be factored any further: the simplest factored form.

Opposite brackets: (7-x) and (x-7)

Sometimes the two brackets almost match: same pieces, opposite order. (p-q) and (q-p) are opposites, so one can be flipped into the other by taking out -1.

q-p=-(p-q)so-k(q-p)=k(p-q)
!
Flipping a bracket costs a minus sign. 7(7-x) becomes -7(x-7). Forget the sign change and the answer comes out (x-7)(3x+7), which expands to something else.

Factoring by Grouping

A four-term polynomial usually has no factor common to all four terms. But splitting it into two pairs and removing each pair’s own common factor can leave the same binomial in both. Pair up, pull a monomial out of each pair, then pull out the shared binomial: this is factoring by grouping.

x^2+3x+4x+12=x(x+3)+4(x+3)=(x+3)(x+4)
factor →← expand

Expanding and factoring are the same equation read in opposite directions. The algebra tiles show it: each pair builds a rectangle, and the two rectangles share a side.

Pair up, pull out, pull out again

1
Pair up the first two terms and the last two terms.
2
Pull out each pair’s own common factor (its GCF).
3
The brackets match? Pull out the shared bracket.
✓
Check by expanding back to the four terms.
Sign care. If the second pair starts with a minus, pull out a negative factor so its bracket can match the first one. Pulling out a negative flips every sign inside: -4a+6=-2(2a-3).
!
Check the inside signs. -4a+6 is -2(2a-3), not -2(2a+3): expand it to be sure, -2\cdot(-3)=+6 ✓. Most grouping mistakes are a sign dropped right here.

No match? Rearrange and try again

You may need to rearrange the terms before grouping. If the first pairing does not leave matching binomials, regroup so that terms sharing a factor sit together. Terms can be moved around freely (addition is commutative), as long as each term keeps its own sign.

Example 3

Pair up, pull out, pull out again

Factor each polynomial by grouping.

(a) x^2+4x+5x+20
x(x+4)+5(x+4)
=\boldsymbol{(x+4)(x+5)}
(b) 6x^2-3x+8x-4
3x(2x-1)+4(2x-1)
=\boldsymbol{(2x-1)(3x+4)}
(c) 10a^2-15a-4a+6
5a(2a-3)-2(2a-3)
=\boldsymbol{(2a-3)(5a-2)}
(d) mn+mp-qn-qp
Group by common letters:
m(n+p)-q(n+p)=\boldsymbol{(n+p)(m-q)}
(e) 3x^2+10y^2-6xy^2-5x
The natural pairing shares no binomial, so rearrange: x-only terms together, y^2 terms together:
(3x^2-5x)+(10y^2-6xy^2)
=x(3x-5)-2y^2(3x-5)=\boldsymbol{(3x-5)(x-2y^2)}
Think Priya wrote x^2-2x-3x+6=x(x-2)-3(x+2) and decided it can’t be factored by grouping. What went wrong?
A sign slip in pair 2: -3x+6=-3(x-2), because -3\cdot(-2)=+6. The brackets do match: x(x-2)-3(x-2)=\boldsymbol{(x-2)(x-3)}. Expanding -3(x+2) gives -3x-6, which is how to catch it.

Key ideas

A whole bracket can be the common factor. Treat it as one block and pull it out once: 5x(x+2)-3(x+2)=(x+2)(5x-3). A lone bracket is 1\cdot(\ldots): keep the 1.
Then check the leftover bracket: (a-4)(5a-15)=5(a-4)(a-3). Opposite brackets flip with a minus sign: 7(7-x)=-7(x-7).
Grouping: pair up, pull out, pull out again: x^2+3x+4x+12 =x(x+3)+4(x+3) =(x+3)(x+4). Pair 2 starts with a minus? Pull out a negative.
No match? Rearrange so terms that share a factor sit together, each with its own sign. Always check by expanding: factoring is expanding run backwards.
