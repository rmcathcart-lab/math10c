Choosing a Factoring Strategy

No new technique today. You already own every tool: common factors, grouping, inspection, decomposition and the difference of squares. Today is about picking the right tool, in the right order, and not stopping one step too early. Then we practise on a mixture.

I can…
  • check for a common factor first, and take out a negative one when the leading coefficient is negative
  • count the terms that are left and choose the tool: difference of squares, inspection, decomposition or grouping
  • trace any expression through the factoring flowchart, possibly more than once
  • finish with a check: can any factor be factored again? Does my answer expand back to the question?
AN5 Demonstrate an understanding of common factors and trinomial factoring, concretely, pictorially and symbolically.
Warm-up

Factoring Airport

Every expression must clear security before it can board: fully factored. Lane 1 is security (common factor). Then count the terms to pick Lane 2, 3 or 4. The final check sends anything that can still factor back through the lanes.

Lane 3: product 12, sum 7 gives 3 and 4, so (x+3)(x+4)

Be security: trace each traveller’s route with your finger, then compare with a neighbour. The airport is today’s checklist: Lane 1 is step 1, Lanes 2–4 are steps 2–5, and the final check is step 6.

1

A Strategy for Factoring Any Expression

Work through this checklist, in order, every time you are asked to factor.

  1. 1
    Common factor first. Always check whether every term shares a common factor. If so, factor it out before doing anything else: this makes every later step easier. If the leading coefficient is negative, remove a negative common factor so the bracket starts with a positive term (Lesson 1A).
  2. 2
    Two terms left (binomial)? Check whether it is a difference of squares, A^2-B^2=(A-B)(A+B). A sum of squares A^2+B^2 does not factor.
  3. 3
    Three terms left (trinomial) of the form x^2+bx+c? Factor by inspection (find two integers that multiply to c and add to b). A trinomial in x^2 or x^3 (degrees 4, 2, 0 or 6, 3, 0) works the same way: treat x^2 (or x^3) as one block (Lesson 5).
  4. 4
    Three terms left (trinomial) of the form ax^2+bx+c with a\neq1? Factor by the method of decomposition (or an area box). Keep an eye out for perfect square trinomials: they decompose very quickly once you spot them. Degrees 4, 2, 0 or 6, 3, 0? Treat x^2 or x^3 as one block here too.
  5. 5
    Four terms left? Try factoring by grouping.
  6. 6
    Always finish with a check. Look at each factor you ended up with and ask: can this be factored again? Many mistakes on tests come from stopping one step too early.
!
Count the terms after step 1. In 3x^2+12x-36=3(x^2+4x-12) the tool is chosen by what’s inside the bracket: three terms with a=1, so inspection, not decomposition.

The checklist as a flowchart

This checklist is often drawn as a flowchart. Pick an expression and step through it: the path lights up one decision at a time, and the written work builds on the right. Every branch ends at the same box: always finish by checking.

The dashed “yes” loop is how the checklist’s step 6 works: a factor that can still be factored goes back to the top of the chart on its own.

The final check: can it board?

Each traveller below claims to be fully factored. Decide first, then tap the ticket. A ticket that fails goes back to security.

!
A sum of squares never factors. x^2+16\neq(x+4)(x-4): that product is x^2-16. Leave x^2+16 as it is.
!
Hidden common factors. A bracket like (2x-6) or (3x-6) still has a common factor. Taking it out at step 1 means it can’t hide there.

Working Through the Checklist

In each Example below, decide which line of the checklist applies (possibly more than once) before you factor. Call out each route, then tap the row to check it.

Example 1

Common factor, then a difference of squares

Factor completely.

(a) 8x^2-50
Common factor first, then difference of squares.
GCF =2: 8x^2-50=2(4x^2-25)
4x^2-25=(2x)^2-5^2
8x^2-50=\boldsymbol{2(2x-5)(2x+5)}
Check: no bracket factors further.
(b) -12x^2+27
Common factor first (take the negative out too), then difference of squares.
GCF =-3: -12x^2+27=-3(4x^2-9)
4x^2-9=(2x)^2-3^2
-12x^2+27=\boldsymbol{-3(2x-3)(2x+3)}
Think Why does the common factor have to come out first in (a)? Try the difference of squares on 8x^2-50 directly.
8 and 50 aren’t perfect squares, so 8x^2-50 doesn’t look like a difference of squares at all. Only after the 2 comes out does 4x^2-25 appear. Step 1 uncovers the pattern.
Example 2

Three terms: inspection or decomposition?

Factor completely.

(a) x^2-2x-63
No common factor; three terms with a=1 ⇒ inspection.
Product -63, sum -2: -9 and 7.
x^2-2x-63=\boldsymbol{(x-9)(x+7)}
(b) -6x^2+5x+21
Common factor of -1 first, then a\neq1 ⇒ decomposition.
-6x^2+5x+21=-(6x^2-5x-21)
ac=6\times(-21)=-126, sum -5: -14 and 9.
6x^2-14x+9x-21=2x(3x-7)+3(3x-7)
=(3x-7)(2x+3)
-6x^2+5x+21=\boldsymbol{-(3x-7)(2x+3)=(7-3x)(2x+3)}
!
Taking out -1 flips every sign. -6x^2+5x+21=-(6x^2-5x-21), not -(6x^2+5x+21). Check the bracket by multiplying it by -1 in your head.
Need the pair for ac=-126? Test divisors of 126: 126÷14ENTER gives 9, so 14\times9=126, and -14+9=-5 ✓
Example 3

Four terms, and a trinomial in disguise

Factor completely.

(a) x^2-xy+3x-3y
No common factor across all four terms; four terms ⇒ grouping.
(x^2-xy)+(3x-3y)=x(x-y)+3(x-y)
x^2-xy+3x-3y=\boldsymbol{(x-y)(x+3)}
(b) x^4-13x^2+36
A trinomial in x^2 with a=1 ⇒ inspection; then check each factor again.
Product 36, sum -13: -9 and -4, so (x^2-9)(x^2-4).
Not finished: both brackets are differences of squares.
x^4-13x^2+36=\boldsymbol{(x-3)(x+3)(x-2)(x+2)}

(b) is warm-up traveller D without its common factor: Lane 3, then Lane 2 twice.

Example 4

A perfect square, and a decomposition in two variables

Factor completely.

(a) 4a^2-20ab+25b^2
Three terms with a\neq1, but check for a perfect square trinomial first.
4a^2=(2a)^2, 25b^2=(5b)^2, and -20ab=-2(2a)(5b).
4a^2-20ab+25b^2=\boldsymbol{(2a-5b)^2}
(b) Extension 6t^6+5t^3p^2-6p^4
A trinomial in t^3 with a\neq1 ⇒ decomposition.
ac=6\times(-6)=-36, sum 5: 9 and -4.
6t^6+9t^3p^2-4t^3p^2-6p^4
=3t^3(2t^3+3p^2)-2p^2(2t^3+3p^2)
=\boldsymbol{(2t^3+3p^2)(3t^3-2p^2)}
!
Two different “a”s in (a). The variable is a, but the a in “a\neq1” means the leading coefficient, which here is 4.

Is it right? Check by expanding

Factoring is expanding run backwards, so every answer carries its own check: multiply it out and you must get the question back. Substituting a number into both forms is a fast second check.

Check Example 2(b) at x=2. The question: (−)6×2x²+5×2+21ENTER. The answer: (7−3×2)×(2×2+3)ENTER. Both give 7 ✓
!
Expanding checks the multiplying, not the “completely”. (2x-6)(2x+6) expands perfectly to 4x^2-36 and is still not fully factored. You need both checks: expand it back, and ask whether any factor factors again.

Lightning round: more travellers

A mixture, like a test. Name the route first, then factor completely. Tap a row to see its route and its boarding pass.

✓

Key ideas

Common factor first, every time. Negative leading coefficient? Take out a negative so the bracket starts positive.
Count the terms left: 2 → difference of squares (a sum of squares doesn’t factor); 3 → inspection (a=1) or decomposition (a\neq1, watch for perfect squares); 4 → grouping.
Disguises: degrees 4, 2, 0 (or 6, 3, 0) is a trinomial in x^2 (or x^3). Treat it as one block and use the same tools.
Finish with two checks: can any factor be factored again (send it back through), and does the answer expand back to the question?
