Math 10C · Unit 4 Factoring · Lesson 6

Further Factoring

Some trinomials are squares in disguise. Some are ordinary trinomials wearing a mask. And some factors can be opened again and again, like nesting dolls. Today: perfect square trinomials, the let u=x^2 disguise, and factoring completely. Every answer gets checked the same way: expand it back.

I can…
  • test a trinomial for the perfect square pattern (first a square, last a square, middle twice the product) and factor it as (a\pm b)^2
  • fill in the missing term that makes a trinomial a perfect square
  • factor a trinomial in disguise, such as m^4+7m^2+12 or 6m^4-7m^2-3, by letting u stand for the block
  • factor completely: remove the common factor, then keep using differences of squares until no factor splits
  • check any factorization by expanding it (or by substituting a number)
  • factor higher-degree disguises such as x^6-7x^3-18 Extension
AN5 Demonstrate an understanding of common factors and trinomial factoring, concretely, pictorially and symbolically.
Warm-up

The Nesting Dolls

Factor a doll to open it. A doll is solid when it can’t be factored any further. Tap a doll to open it.

1
Number dolls. Open 36=4\times9 all the way to primes. How many solid dolls?
36=(2\times2)\times(3\times3): 4 solid dolls, 2, 2, 3 and 3.
2
Open 2x^2-50 all the way.
2x^2-50=2(x^2-25)
=2(x-5)(x+5)
3
Open x^4-625. Hint: x^4=(x^2)^2. Can both new dolls open again?
(x^2-25)(x^2+25)=(x-5)(x+5)(x^2+25). Only x^2-25 opens again; x^2+25 is a sum of squares and stays solid.
!
The big one. Keep opening the doll x^8-1 until every doll is solid. How many solid dolls are inside? Guess first.
A 2
B 3
C 4
D 8
C, 4 solid dolls. x^8-1=(x^4-1)(x^4+1)=(x^2-1)(x^2+1)(x^4+1)=(x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1)(x^4+1). Each time only the “minus” doll opens; the “plus” dolls are sums of squares and stay shut. (Open “The big one” on the shelf to watch it.)
A stops after the first opening: the classic “not factored completely” mistake. B stops one step early, leaving x^2-1 unopened. D comes from the exponent 8, as if x^8-1 split into eight brackets.

1

Perfect Square Trinomials

In Unit 3 we squared binomials. Squaring a binomial always gives three terms:

(p+q)^2expand ⟶⟵ factorp^2+2pq+q^2
(p-q)^2expand ⟶⟵ factorp^2-2pq+q^2

A trinomial of this form is a perfect square trinomial. Factoring one is the square picture run backwards: given the area, find the side.

The three-part test

A trinomial is a perfect square trinomial exactly when all three of these hold:
1The first term is a perfect square.
2The last term is a perfect square, and positive.
3The middle term is twice the product of their square roots (with the matching sign).

In m^2+14m+49 the first term m^2 and the last term 49=7^2 are perfect squares, and the middle term 14m=2(m)(7) is exactly twice their product, so m^2+14m+49=(m+7)^2.

!
Twice, not once. x^2+5x+25 has square ends, but 2(x)(5)=10x, not 5x. It is not a perfect square.
!
The middle sign goes in the bracket. 81x^2-18x+1=(9x-1)^2. Writing (9x+1)^2 would expand to +18x.
Example 1

Applying the three-part test

Which of the following are perfect square trinomials?

(a) b^2+6b+9
\sqrt{b^2}=b, \sqrt{9}=3; middle 2(b)(3)=6b matches.
Perfect square: b^2+6b+9=\boldsymbol{(b+3)^2}
(b) x^2-11x+8
8 is not a perfect square, so the last-term test already fails.
Not a perfect square trinomial.
(c) 9x^2-42x+49
\sqrt{9x^2}=3x, \sqrt{49}=7; middle 2(3x)(7)=42x matches, with a minus sign.
Perfect square: 9x^2-42x+49=\boldsymbol{(3x-7)^2}
(d) y^2+6y-9
The last term -9 is negative, so it cannot be a perfect square.
Not a perfect square trinomial.
Think Why must the last term of a perfect square trinomial be positive, but the middle term can be negative?
The last term is q^2, and a square is never negative: 3^2=(-3)^2=9. The middle term is 2pq, which is negative when the bracket has a minus: (p-q)^2=p^2-2pq+q^2.

Reading the pattern backwards to fill a gap

If one term is missing, the square picture tells us what it must be. The first and last terms are the corner squares; their square roots are the sides; the middle term is the two rectangles, 2\times side \times side.

Example 2

Reading the pattern backwards to fill a gap

Fill in each blank so that the trinomial is a perfect square trinomial.

(a) x^2+\underline{\qquad}+121
\sqrt{x^2}=x, \sqrt{121}=11
Middle =2(x)(11)=\boldsymbol{22x}
(b) x^2-\underline{\qquad}+169
\sqrt{x^2}=x, \sqrt{169}=13
Middle =2(x)(13)=\boldsymbol{26x}
(c) 16x^2+\underline{\qquad}+81
\sqrt{16x^2}=4x, \sqrt{81}=9
Middle =2(4x)(9)=\boldsymbol{72x}
(d) 25m^2+40m+\underline{\qquad}
\sqrt{25m^2}=5m. Need 40m=2(5m)k=10mk, so k=4.
Last term =k^2=\boldsymbol{16}
Example 3

Factoring a perfect square trinomial

Factor each perfect square trinomial.

(a) 81x^2-18x+1
\sqrt{81x^2}=9x, \sqrt{1}=1, and 2(9x)(1)=18x matches (negative sign).
81x^2-18x+1=\boldsymbol{(9x-1)^2}
Check: (9x-1)^2=81x^2-18x+1 ✓
(b) 9+24x+16x^2
\sqrt{9}=3, \sqrt{16x^2}=4x, and 2(3)(4x)=24x matches.
9+24x+16x^2=\boldsymbol{(4x+3)^2}
Check: (4x+3)^2=16x^2+24x+9 ✓
(c) \dfrac{1}{4}a^2-3ab+9b^2
\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4}a^2}=\tfrac{1}{2}a, \sqrt{9b^2}=3b, and 2\left(\tfrac{1}{2}a\right)(3b)=3ab matches (negative sign).
\tfrac{1}{4}a^2-3ab+9b^2=\boldsymbol{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}a-3b\right)^2}
Think Is \left(\tfrac{1}{2}a-3b\right)^2 the same as \tfrac{1}{4}(a-6b)^2?
Yes. Take out the common factor \tfrac{1}{2} first: \tfrac{1}{2}a-3b=\tfrac{1}{2}(a-6b), and squaring gives \tfrac{1}{4}(a-6b)^2. Both expand to \tfrac{1}{4}a^2-3ab+9b^2.
Check (b) at x=2. The trinomial: 9+24×2+16×2x²ENTER. The square: (4×2+3)x²ENTER. Both give 121 ✓

Trinomials of the Form u^2+bu+c, where u Is a Monomial

In x^2+bx+c the terms have degrees 2, 1, 0. Inspection still works when the degrees are 4, 2, 0: the variable x has simply been replaced by the monomial x^2 (or by a product such as pq). Degrees 6, 3, 0 and higher work the same way (see the Extension in Section 5).

Key idea: treat the squared monomial as a single block. To factor m^4+7m^2+12, notice it can be written (m^2)^2+7(m^2)+12. Substituting u=m^2 turns it into the familiar trinomial u^2+7u+12=(u+3)(u+4). Replacing u by m^2 again gives m^4+7m^2+12=(m^2+3)(m^2+4).
!
Write the substitution down until it’s automatic, and always take the disguise off at the end. (u+3)(u+4) is not an answer about m: the final brackets would be holding the wrong variable.
Example 4

Substitute, factor, substitute back

Factor completely.

(a) b^4-3b^2-40
Let u=b^2: u^2-3u-40. Product -40, sum -3: -8 and 5.
=(u-8)(u+5). Back-substitute u=b^2:
b^4-3b^2-40=\boldsymbol{(b^2-8)(b^2+5)}
Neither bracket factors further (8 is not a perfect square; b^2+5 is a sum) ✓
(b) y^4+3y^2-4
Let u=y^2: u^2+3u-4. Product -4, sum 3: 4 and -1.
=(u+4)(u-1)=(y^2+4)(y^2-1)
y^2-1 is a difference of squares, so keep going:
y^4+3y^2-4=\boldsymbol{(y^2+4)(y-1)(y+1)} ✓
Think Both parts made a bracket of the form (\text{square})-(\text{number}). Why did y^2-1 open again, but b^2-8 didn’t?
1=1^2 is a perfect square, so y^2-1 is a difference of squares. 8 is not a perfect square, so b^2-8 can’t be split using integers.
Check (a) at b=2. The question: 2^4−3×2x²−40ENTER. The answer: (2x²−8)×(2x²+5)ENTER. Both give −36 ✓
3

Trinomials of the Form au^2+bu+c, where u Is a Monomial

The same disguise works with decomposition (Lesson 5) when the leading coefficient is not 1. There are two equivalent ways to handle 6m^4-7m^2-3:

Method 1: Substitution
Let u=m^2: 6m^4-7m^2-3=6u^2-7u-3
Decompose: 6u^2-9u+2u-3
=3u(2u-3)+1(2u-3)=(2u-3)(3u+1)
Replace u by m^2: =(2m^2-3)(3m^2+1)
Method 2: Direct decomposition
6m^4-7m^2-3=6m^4-9m^2+2m^2-3
=3m^2(2m^2-3)+1(2m^2-3)
=(2m^2-3)(3m^2+1)
Split the middle term directly in terms of m^2.
Example 5

Decomposition in disguise

Factor completely.

(a) 10x^4-3x^2-4
ac=10(-4)=-40; product -40, sum -3: -8 and 5.
10x^4-8x^2+5x^2-4
=2x^2(5x^2-4)+1(5x^2-4)
=\boldsymbol{(5x^2-4)(2x^2+1)}
5x^2-4 is not a difference of squares (5 is not a perfect square) ✓
(b) 3p^2q^2-20pq+12
The block is pq, since p^2q^2=(pq)^2.
ac=36; product 36, sum -20: -18 and -2.
3p^2q^2-18pq-2pq+12
=3pq(pq-6)-2(pq-6)
=\boldsymbol{(pq-6)(3pq-2)} ✓
(c) 12x^4+8x^2-15
ac=12(-15)=-180; product -180, sum 8: 18 and -10.
12x^4+18x^2-10x^2-15
=6x^2(2x^2+3)-5(2x^2+3)
=\boldsymbol{(2x^2+3)(6x^2-5)}
No common factor and no difference of squares remains ✓
4

More Complex Differences of Squares

The pattern A^2-B^2=(A-B)(A+B) also holds when A and B are themselves polynomials.

To factor m^4-81n^4, rewrite it as a difference of two squares: m^4-81n^4=(m^2)^2-(9n^2)^2=(m^2-9n^2)(m^2+9n^2). The first factor, m^2-9n^2, is itself a difference of squares, so it factors again: =(m-3n)(m+3n)(m^2+9n^2).
“Factor completely” means no stopping until every common factor has been removed and every possible difference of squares has been used. Like the warm-up dolls: keep opening until every doll is solid.
!
A sum of squares stays shut. w^2+4 is not (w+2)^2: that expands to w^2+4w+4. No two integer brackets multiply to w^2+4 (Lesson 4).
!
Common factor first. 162-2x^4 doesn’t look like a difference of squares until the 2 comes out: 2(81-x^4).
Example 6

Keep going until nothing splits

Factor completely.

(a) w^4-16
w^4-16=(w^2)^2-4^2
=(w^2-4)(w^2+4)
w^2-4 is again a difference of squares:
w^4-16=\boldsymbol{(w-2)(w+2)(w^2+4)}
w^2+4 is a sum of squares, so it does not factor ✓
(b) 162-2x^4
Common factor first: 162-2x^4=2(81-x^4)
=2\big(9^2-(x^2)^2\big)=2(9-x^2)(9+x^2)
9-x^2 factors again:
162-2x^4=\boldsymbol{2(3-x)(3+x)(9+x^2)} ✓
(c) 3p^5q^4-48pt^4
GCF first: the terms share 3p.
=3p(p^4q^4-16t^4)
=3p\big((p^2q^2)^2-(4t^2)^2\big)
=3p(p^2q^2-4t^2)(p^2q^2+4t^2)
p^2q^2-4t^2 factors again:
=\boldsymbol{3p(pq-2t)(pq+2t)(p^2q^2+4t^2)} ✓
Think How many solid dolls are inside each answer to Example 6? Which part has the most, and why?
(a) 3 dolls. (b) and (c) have 4 each: the common factor (2, or 3p) is an extra solid doll that comes out first, before any difference of squares.
Check (a) at w=3. 3^4−16ENTER and (3−2)×(3+2)×(3x²+4)ENTER both give 65 ✓ Why not w=2? Then w-2=0 and both sides are 0: a check that almost anything passes.

Extension: Higher-Degree Disguises

Extension Beyond the Math 10C outcome: the same disguise, with a bigger block.

The block can be any power. In x^6-7x^3-18 the degrees are 6, 3, 0, so the block is x^3. In z^{10}-3z^5-10 the block is z^5. The test is the same as before: the first exponent is double the middle one, and the last term is a constant.

Example 7 · Extension

Bigger blocks

Factor completely.

(a) x^6-7x^3-18
Degrees 6, 3, 0: the block is x^3. Let u=x^3: u^2-7u-18.
Product -18, sum -7: -9 and 2. =(u-9)(u+2)
Back-substitute: x^6-7x^3-18=\boldsymbol{(x^3-9)(x^3+2)}
Neither bracket is a difference of squares, so this is complete ✓
(b) z^{10}-3z^5-10
Degrees 10, 5, 0: the block is z^5. Let u=z^5: u^2-3u-10.
Product -10, sum -3: -5 and 2. =(u-5)(u+2)
Back-substitute: z^{10}-3z^5-10=\boldsymbol{(z^5-5)(z^5+2)} ✓
✓

Key ideas

Perfect square trinomial: first a square, last a square (and +), middle twice the product. Then a^2\pm2ab+b^2=(a\pm b)^2. It’s the square picture run backwards.
Fill a gap: the ends are the corner squares, the middle is the two rectangles: 2\times side \times side.
Disguise: degrees 4, 2, 0 (or 6, 3, 0…)? Let u be the block, factor, then put the block back. Works with decomposition too.
Factor completely: common factor first, then keep opening differences of squares until every doll is solid. A sum of squares never opens. Check by expanding.
