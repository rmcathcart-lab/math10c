Factoring Trinomials of the Form x^2+bx+c

In Unit 3, multiplying two binomials built a rectangle of algebra tiles. Today we run that picture backwards: arrange the tiles into a rectangle and read the factors off its sides. Then a shortcut that needs no tiles at all: two integers whose product is c and whose sum is b.

I can…
  • model x^2+bx+c with algebra tiles and arrange the tiles into a rectangle, adding zero pairs when needed
  • explain why the two numbers in (x+p)(x+q) must multiply to c and add to b
  • use the signs of b and c to decide the signs of the two integers
  • factor x^2+bx+c by inspection, taking out a common factor first, and recognize a trinomial that can’t be factored
  • factor x^2+bxy+cy^2, and check any factoring by expanding
AN5 Demonstrate an understanding of common factors and trinomial factoring, concretely, pictorially and symbolically.

Why Algebra Tiles Work

With algebra tiles, an x^2-tile is a square of side x, an x-tile is a 1\times x rectangle, and a unit tile is a 1\times1 square. The collection below (one x^2-tile, seven x-tiles and ten unit tiles) is the polynomial x^2+7x+10, and those same tiles rearrange, with no gaps or overlaps, into a rectangle.

→
The rectangle’s area is the original polynomial, so its length and width are the two binomial factors: length x+5, width x+2, giving x^2+7x+10=(x+2)(x+5). Multiplying back out confirms it: (x+2)(x+5)=x^2+5x+2x+10=x^2+7x+10 ✓

Arranging the tiles

The x^2-tile goes in a corner, the x-tiles line its two sides, and the unit tiles fill the last corner. The whole puzzle is the unit-tile block: it has to use every unit tile, and its rows and columns decide how many x-tiles it needs.

Example 1

From a pile of tiles to a factored form

Consider the collection of algebra tiles shown below.

(a) Write the polynomial expression represented by the tiles.

\boldsymbol{x^2+9x+8}
(b) Rearrange the tiles into a rectangle, and state the length and width.
Product 8, sum 9: +1 and +8.
Length \boldsymbol{x+8}, width \boldsymbol{x+1}.
(c) Use your results to write the polynomial in factored form.
x^2+9x+8=\boldsymbol{(x+1)(x+8)}
Check: x^2+8x+x+8=x^2+9x+8 ✓

No. A 1\times15 block needs 1+15=16 x-tiles beside it, and there are only 8. Only the 3\times5 block uses exactly 3+5=8.

Finding the Pattern: Sum and Product

Tiles get clumsy fast: nobody wants to push 84 unit tiles around. In every rectangle so far, the two numbers in the factors multiplied to the unit tiles and added to the x-tiles. Expanding a few products shows that this always happens, and why.

Example 3

Expand first, then read the pattern backwards

(a) Expand each pair of binomials.

(x+3)(x+4)
x^2+4x+3x+12
=\boldsymbol{x^2+7x+12}

x^2+9x+x+9

x^2-3x-4x+12
=\boldsymbol{x^2-7x+12}
(x+9)(x-5)
x^2-5x+9x-45
=\boldsymbol{x^2+4x-45}
In each case, how are the coefficient b and the constant term c related to the two numbers being added to x?
In every case b (the coefficient of x) is the sum of the two numbers added to x, and c (the constant term) is their product. For (x+9)(x-5): 9+(-5)=4=b and 9\times(-5)=-45=c.
(b) Use FOIL to expand (x+p)(x+q) and show that it can always be written in the form x^2+(p+q)x+pq.
(x+p)(x+q)=\underbrace{x\cdot x}_{\text{F}}+\underbrace{x\cdot q}_{\text{O}}+\underbrace{p\cdot x}_{\text{I}}+\underbrace{p\cdot q}_{\text{L}}=x^2+qx+px+pq=\boldsymbol{x^2+(p+q)x+pq}
Comparing with x^2+bx+c: b=p+q and c=pq, for every p and q.

The rule

Part (b) proves in general what part (a) suggested: to factor x^2+bx+c, find two integers whose sum is b and whose product is c.

Factoring x^2+bx+c by Inspection

To factor x^2+bx+c, find two integers with product c and sum b. If no such pair exists, the trinomial cannot be factored over the integers. If the terms share a common factor, remove it first: factoring is not complete until no further factoring is possible.
1Common factor out front, if there is one.
2Signs of the pair, from c and b.
3List the factor pairs of c; find the sum b.
4Write the brackets, then check by expanding.
!
Finished means nothing else factors. (3x-6)(x-8) does multiply out to 3x^2-30x+48, but 3x-6 still has a common factor of 3. Take the common factor out first and this can’t happen: 3(x-2)(x-8).
Example 5

Positive c, including two with a common factor

Factor each expression completely.

(a) x^2+9x+14
+2 and +7: product 14, sum 9.
=\boldsymbol{(x+2)(x+7)}
(b) x^2+15x+14
+1 and +14: product 14, sum 15.
=\boldsymbol{(x+1)(x+14)}
(c) x^2-15x+14
c>0,\ b<0: both negative. -1 and -14: product 14, sum −15.
=\boldsymbol{(x-1)(x-14)}
(d) m^2-13m+12
-1 and -12: product 12, sum −13.
=\boldsymbol{(m-1)(m-12)}
(e) y^2+4y+3
+1 and +3: product 3, sum 4.
=\boldsymbol{(y+1)(y+3)}
(f) x^2+21x+20
+1 and +20: product 20, sum 21.
=\boldsymbol{(x+1)(x+20)}
(g) 3x^2-30x+48
GCF 3 first: 3(x^2-10x+16).
-2 and -8: product 16, sum −10.
=\boldsymbol{3(x-2)(x-8)}
(h) 2x^3+24x^2+40x
GCF 2x first: 2x(x^2+12x+20).
+2 and +10: product 20, sum 12.
=\boldsymbol{2x(x+2)(x+10)}
Think Parts (a), (b) and (c) all have c=14. How many integers b make x^2+bx+14 factorable? List them.
The factor pairs of 14 are 1,14 and 2,7, both positive or both negative. Their sums: 15,\ 9,\ -9,\ -15. So four values: b=\pm9 or \pm15. Any other b and it won’t factor.

Negative c

Negative c works the same way; only now one integer is positive and the other negative. For x^2-2x-15 we need product -15 and sum -2. These are -5 and 3, so x^2-2x-15=(x-5)(x+3).

!
Put the minus on the right number. For x^2-x-20, both -5,\ 4 and 5,\ -4 multiply to −20, but only -5+4=-1 gives the right sum. The number further from 0 takes the sign of b.

Modelling Negative Terms with Algebra Tiles

A negative tile is drawn white with a minus sign (see the key in Section 1). Build the rectangle exactly as before: the x^2-tile in the corner, the x-tiles along the top and the side, and the unit tiles filling the corner. The sign of each tile in the filled corner is the product of the signs along its row and column, so positive × negative gives negative unit tiles. For x^2+x-6: three positive x-tiles across the top and two negative x-tiles down the side give +3x-2x=+x, and the corner holds 3\times2=6 negative unit tiles.

  • Top: x+3. Side: x-2.
  • Tiles: x^2, +3x-2x=+x, -6.
  • So x^2+x-6=(x+3)(x-2).
  • The pair +3 and -2: product −6, sum +1. The same rule as before.

Building it from the pile: zero pairs

The pile for x^2+x-6 has only one x-tile, but the rectangle uses three positive and two negative. The fix is a zero pair: a +x-tile and a -x-tile together are worth 0, so adding them doesn’t change the polynomial, but it gives us the tiles the rectangle needs.

Example 8

Reading and building rectangles with negative tiles

(a) Write the trinomial modelled by the rectangle, and its length and width.
One x^2-tile; 3+2=5 negative x-tiles; 6 positive unit tiles (negative × negative).
Trinomial \boldsymbol{x^2-5x+6}; top x-3, side x-2.
(b) Write the trinomial in factored form, and verify by expanding.
x^2-5x+6=\boldsymbol{(x-2)(x-3)}
Check: (x-2)(x-3)=x^2-3x-2x+6=x^2-5x+6 ✓
(c) Sketch the tile rectangle for x^2+2x-8 and use it to factor the trinomial.
Need product −8, sum +2: +4 and -2. Four positive x-tiles across, two negative x-tiles down (two zero pairs added to the pile’s two x-tiles), 8 negative unit tiles in the corner.
x^2+2x-8=\boldsymbol{(x+4)(x-2)}

Trinomials of the Form x^2+bxy+cy^2

A trinomial such as x^2+10xy+24y^2 factors exactly like x^2+10x+24: the second variable rides along with the second term of each factor. If x^2+bx+c=(x+p)(x+q), then x^2+bxy+cy^2=(x+py)(x+qy), since p and q still only have to multiply to c and add to b.

Example 9

Expanding shows why the y tags along

(a) Expand (x+4y)(x+6y).
F x^2; O 6xy; I 4xy; L 24y^2
=x^2+6xy+4xy+24y^2=\boldsymbol{x^2+10xy+24y^2}
(b) Use your answer to part (a) to factor x^2+10xy+24y^2 without any extra work.
Part (a) read backwards: x^2+10xy+24y^2=\boldsymbol{(x+4y)(x+6y)}.
The pair is still +4 and +6 (product 24, sum 10); the y simply rides along.

Two variables, by inspection

Same steps as Section 3. Find the pair from the numbers alone, then put the second variable next to each number in the brackets. A common factor still comes out first.

A big c? Let the calculator find the partners. Divide c by 1, 2, 3, … and keep the whole-number answers. 84÷7ENTER gives 12, so 7 and 12 are a factor pair of 84.

Key ideas

Tiles: arrange the tiles for x^2+bx+c into a rectangle (add zero pairs if needed). Its length and width are the factors: x^2+7x+10=(x+2)(x+5).
Why it works: (x+p)(x+q)=x^2+(p+q)x+pq. So find two integers with product c and sum b. No such pair: it can’t be factored over the integers.
Signs: c>0, both have the sign of b. c<0, one of each, and the one further from 0 has the sign of b.
Common factor first, then the pair: 3x^2-30x+48=3(x-2)(x-8). A second variable tags along: (x+4y)(x+6y). Always check by expanding.
