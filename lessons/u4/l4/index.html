Factoring ax^2+bx+c (where a\neq1)

When the x^2 has a coefficient, the Lesson 2 trick of “product c, sum b” stops working. Today we factor trinomials like 6x^2+19x+10 three ways: with algebra tiles, with an area box, and by decomposition. We’ll also see why the magic number is ac.

I can…
  • arrange algebra tiles for ax^2+bx+c into a rectangle and read the factors off its sides
  • fill in an area box and read the factors from the row and column common factors
  • factor by decomposition: find two integers with product ac and sum b, split the middle term, and group
  • explain why the two middle pieces always multiply to ac
  • list factor pairs systematically when ac is large, take out a common factor (or a negative) first, and check by expanding
AN5 Demonstrate an understanding of common factors and trinomial factoring, concretely, pictorially and symbolically.

Expanding collects the two middle pieces into one term. Factoring has to un-collect it: split 19x back into 15x+4x. The whole lesson is about finding those two pieces. First with algebra tiles, then with an area (box) model, then by decomposition.

Factoring ax^2+bx+c Using Algebra Tiles

The method is the same as for x^2+bx+c: put the x^2-tiles together in a corner, line x-tiles up along the side and the bottom, and fill the corner with unit tiles. The tiles must make a rectangle with no gaps; its length and width are the factors.

!
Use every tile, leave no gaps. If x-tiles are left over (or you run short), the unit tiles are in the wrong block shape. Rearrange them and try again.

Reminder: Factoring by Grouping

From Lesson 1B: group the first two terms and the last two, remove the common factor from each group, then remove the common binomial.

\underbrace{10x^2+4x}_{2x(5x+2)}\;\underbrace{+15x+6}_{+3(5x+2)}=2x(\textcolor{#c2417a}{5x+2})+3(\textcolor{#c2417a}{5x+2})=(\textcolor{#c2417a}{5x+2})(2x+3)

Decomposition (Section 5) is just a way of creating the four terms that grouping needs.
4

The Area (Box) Model

Tiles become tedious when a, b and c are large, and they need negative tiles when b or c is negative. An area box works in every case. It’s the warm-up’s rectangle, without drawing it to scale.

Draw a 2\times2 grid. Put ax^2 in the top-left cell and c in the bottom-right cell. The other two cells hold the middle term bx, split into two pieces whose product is ac\cdot x^2 and whose sum is bx. Then take the common factor of each row and each column: those are the factors.

Each row of the box is one group in factoring by grouping: 6x^2-4x+3x-2=2x(3x-2)+1(3x-2).

!
Divide, don’t guess the sign. The bottom row’s label is the bottom-left cell ÷ the first column’s label. If the cell is negative, the label is negative, and then it must also work for the bottom-right cell.

The Method of Decomposition, and Why the Pair Multiplies to ac

Why ac? Expand two general binomials without collecting the middle terms:

(px+q)(rx+s)=\underbrace{pr}_{a}\,x^2+\underbrace{ps\,x+qr\,x}_{\text{the two middle pieces}}+\underbrace{qs}_{c}

The two middle coefficients add to b=ps+qr, and they multiply to (ps)(qr)=(pr)(qs)=ac. So the middle term of every factorable ax^2+bx+c splits into two pieces whose product is ac and whose sum is b. (When a=1 this is exactly the Lesson 2 rule: the product is 1\cdot c=c.)

The method of factoring ax^2+bx+c by splitting the value of b into two integers whose product is ac and whose sum is b is called the method of decomposition.
1Compute acMultiply the first and last coefficients.
2Find the pairTwo integers: product ac, sum b.
3SplitRewrite bx as two terms using the pair.
4GroupCommon factor of each pair, then the common bracket.
5VerifyExpand your answer. You should get the trinomial back.

The area box and decomposition are the same calculation. The box’s two middle cells are the split, and each row of the box is one group. Watch them side by side:

Decomposition, checked against the tiles

Factor the following using the method of decomposition, verify by expanding, and compare your answers with Example 1 and Example 2.

(a) 3x^2+8x+4
a=3,\ b=8,\ c=4, so ac=3(4)=12. Pair with product 12 and sum 8: 2 and 6.
3x^2+8x+4=3x^2+2x+6x+4
=x(3x+2)+2(3x+2)
=\boldsymbol{(3x+2)(x+2)}
This matches the rectangle in Example 1 ✓
(b) 4x^2+13x+3
a=4,\ b=13,\ c=3, so ac=4(3)=12. Pair with product 12 and sum 13: 1 and 12.
4x^2+13x+3=4x^2+x+12x+3
=x(4x+1)+3(4x+1)
=\boldsymbol{(4x+1)(x+3)}
This matches the rectangle in Example 2 ✓
Think In (a), 4+4 also adds to 8. Why can’t we split 8x as 4x+4x?
The pair must also multiply to ac=12, and 4\times4=16. Try it anyway: 3x^2+4x+4x+4=x(3x+4)+4(x+1). The brackets don’t match, so grouping gets stuck.
!
Product ac, not c. The Lesson 2 rule (product c) only works when a=1. For 3x^2+8x+4 a pair with product 4 and sum 8 doesn’t exist; a pair with product ac=12 does.
6

Finding the Pair When ac Is Large

List factor pairs systematically. Start at 1\times|ac| and work up, stopping when the pair reaches \sqrt{|ac|} (after that the pairs repeat). The signs tell you what to look for:

ac>0: the two numbers have the same sign as b. Look for a pair that adds to |b|.
ac<0: the two numbers have opposite signs. Look for a difference of |b|; the bigger one gets the sign of b.
Test a factor of |ac|. Is 12 a factor of 180? 180÷12ENTER gives 15, a whole number, so 180=12\times15.
Where to stop. 2ndx²180ENTER gives \sqrt{180}\approx13.4, so only divisors up to 13 need testing. Checking your answer: the TI-30XIIS has no table or graph, so put the same value of x into the trinomial and into your factored form (see Example 5). If the two values differ, the factoring is wrong. Two different quadratics can agree at no more than two values of x, so if three values all match, the factoring is right.
Example 5

A large ac

Factor 18x^2+3x-10. Use a factor-pair list (dividing on the calculator) to find the pair, then verify with the calculator.

ac=18(-10)=-180: opposite signs, difference 3. Pairs of 180: 1\cdot180,\ 2\cdot90,\ 3\cdot60,\ 4\cdot45,\ 5\cdot36,\ 6\cdot30,\ 9\cdot20,\ 10\cdot18,\ 12\cdot15. Only 15-12=3, so the pair is 15 and -12.
18x^2+15x-12x-10=3x(6x+5)-2(6x+5)=\boldsymbol{(6x+5)(3x-2)}
Check: both forms give the same value for every x, e.g. at x=1 both are 11 ✓
Verify at x=2 on the TI-30XIIS. The trinomial: 18×2x²+3×2−10ENTER. The factored form: (6×2+5)×(3×2−2)ENTER. Both give 68 ✓

Practice: Putting It All Together

Decomposition also handles a negative leading term (rearrange into descending order, then remove a negative common factor so the bracket starts with a positive term, as in Lesson 1A), perfect-square trinomials, and two-variable trinomials ax^2+bxy+cy^2.

Always look for a common factor first. Removing it before decomposing leaves a much smaller ac: 20a^2+40a-25=5(4a^2+8a-5), and 8x^3+6x^2+x=x(8x^2+6x+1) turns a cubic into a trinomial. A factorization is not finished until no bracket factors any further.
!
Not finished! 6x^2+14x+4=(2x+4)(3x+1) is true, but 2x+4=2(x+2). Fully factored: 2(x+2)(3x+1). Take the GCF out first and this can’t happen.
!
Taking out -1 flips every sign. -6x^2+x+2=-(6x^2-x-2), not -(6x^2+x+2).

The full range of cases

Factor.

(a) 6x^2+19x-7
ac=6(-7)=-42; pair: 21 and -2.
6x^2+21x-2x-7
=3x(2x+7)-1(2x+7)
=\boldsymbol{(2x+7)(3x-1)}
(b) 4n^2-5n-6
ac=4(-6)=-24; pair: -8 and 3.
4n^2-8n+3n-6
=4n(n-2)+3(n-2)
=\boldsymbol{(n-2)(4n+3)}
(c) 9x^2-12x+4
ac=9(4)=36; pair: -6 and -6 (repeated, so a perfect square).
9x^2-6x-6x+4
=3x(3x-2)-2(3x-2)
=(3x-2)(3x-2)=\boldsymbol{(3x-2)^2}
(d) 20-3y-2y^2
Descending order: -2y^2-3y+20=-(2y^2+3y-20).
ac=2(-20)=-40; pair: 8 and -5.
2y^2+8y-5y-20=2y(y+4)-5(y+4)=(y+4)(2y-5)
So 20-3y-2y^2=\boldsymbol{-(y+4)(2y-5)}, or equivalently \boldsymbol{(y+4)(5-2y)}.
Check: (y+4)(5-2y)=5y-2y^2+20-8y=-2y^2-3y+20 ✓
(e) 3x^2-7xy+2y^2
Two variables, same method: the split pieces carry an xy.
ac=3(2)=6; pair: -1 and -6.
3x^2-xy-6xy+2y^2
=x(3x-y)-2y(3x-y)
=\boldsymbol{(3x-y)(x-2y)}
Check: 3x^2-6xy-xy+2y^2=3x^2-7xy+2y^2 ✓
Think In (c) the pair was -6 and -6. What does a repeated pair tell you about the trinomial? What does its area box look like?
The two factors are the same, so the trinomial is a perfect square: (3x-2)^2. Its area box is a square: the rows and the columns have the same labels (3x, -2), and the two middle cells are equal. More in Lesson 5.
✓

Key ideas

Factoring is expanding run backwards. Arrange the tiles for ax^2+bx+c into a rectangle with no gaps: its sides are the factors.
Area box: ax^2 top-left, c bottom-right, the two middle cells multiply to acx^2 and add to bx. The row and column common factors are the factors.
Decomposition: compute ac, find the pair (product ac, sum b), split, group, check. It works because (ps)(qr)=(pr)(qs)=ac.
Every time: descending order, GCF (or -1) out first, list pairs up to \sqrt{|ac|} when ac is large, and check by expanding.
