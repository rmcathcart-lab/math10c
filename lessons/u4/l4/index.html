Math 10C · Unit 4 Factoring · Lesson 4

Difference of Squares

Take a square, cut a smaller square out of its corner, and the leftover L-shape slides together into a rectangle. That one picture is today’s whole lesson: A^2-B^2=(A-B)(A+B). It is the trinomial x^2+bx+c with a middle coefficient of 0, and it turns 58\times62 into mental math.

I can…
  • explain why x^2-k^2 factors as (x-k)(x+k): the sum–product pair is k and -k, and the middle terms cancel
  • recognize a difference of squares: both terms perfect squares (square coefficients, even exponents) with a minus between them
  • factor A^2-B^2 when A and B are numbers, variables or monomials, and check by expanding
  • remove a greatest common factor first, and explain why a sum of squares never factors
  • use the pattern for mental math and for area problems
AN5 Demonstrate an understanding of common factors and trinomial factoring, concretely, pictorially and symbolically.

1

Where the Pattern Comes From

A difference of squares such as x^2-49 is the case of x^2+bx+c whose middle coefficient is 0, with c the negative of a perfect square. By the sum–product method we need two integers with sum 0 and product −49. Only 7 and -7 work:

x^2-49=x^2+7x-7x-49=(x+7)(x-7)

Algebra tiles: the rectangle needs a zero pair

With tiles, x^2-9 is one x^2-tile and nine negative unit tiles. There are no x-tiles, so the pieces can’t close up into a rectangle. Add +3x and -3x: together they are zero, so the value doesn’t change, but now the rectangle closes, and its sides are the factors.

The general pattern

In general, if c=-k^2, the sum–product pair is always k and -k. That gives an identity worth memorizing:

Difference of squares
A^2-B^2=(A-B)(A+B)
factor →← expand
Capital letters, so they don’t clash with the b and c of x^2+bx+c.

Why it works geometrically. Remove a B\times B square from the corner of an A\times A square: the L-shape left has area A^2-B^2. Cut it along the dashed line into a striped piece (A by A-B) and a grey piece (A-B by B). Turn the grey piece and slide it onto the end of the striped piece: one rectangle, A-B by A+B.

!
One plus, one minus. x^2-36\neq(x-6)^2. Squaring gives (x-6)(x-6)=x^2-12x+36, with a middle term. The brackets must be (x-6)(x+6) so the middle terms cancel.

Important: a sum of squares never factors

A sum of squares such as x^2+16 can never be factored over the integers. We would need two integers with a positive product and a sum of zero, but a positive product forces the same sign, so no such pair exists.
2

Substituting Expressions for A and B

In A^2-B^2=(A-B)(A+B), the letters A and B can stand for numbers, variables, monomials or larger expressions. Rewriting 25x^2-64 as (5x)^2-(8)^2 matches the pattern with A=5x and B=8, so 25x^2-64=(5x-8)(5x+8).

Check both terms first. If either one is not a perfect square, it is not a difference of squares. A monomial is a perfect square when its coefficient is a perfect square and every exponent is even (halve each exponent to find its square root).
Perfect squares to know
x^2=(x)^2x^4=(x^2)^2x^6=(x^3)^2a^2b^2=(ab)^249p^2q^2=(7pq)^2
!
Keep the order. In 121-y^2 the square being subtracted is y^2, so A=11 and B=y: (11-y)(11+y). Writing (y-11)(y+11) gives y^2-121, the opposite.

Removing a Common Factor First

Always look for a greatest common factor first. An expression often becomes a recognizable difference of squares only after the GCF is removed, and an answer with a common factor still inside a bracket is not fully factored. Then check each new bracket: it may itself be a difference of squares.

4

An Application

A rectangle’s area is length × width. So when an area is given as a difference of squares, factoring it gives two expressions for the side lengths. Taking the larger factor as the length: A+B is the length and A-B the width. Adding them for the perimeter, the B-terms cancel: another zero pair.

Example 4

A rink whose area is a difference of squares

The surface of a rectangular skating rink has area 25m^2-4n^2 square metres.

(a) Write expressions for the length and width of the rink.
25m^2-4n^2=(5m)^2-(2n)^2=(5m-2n)(5m+2n). Taking the larger factor as the length:
Length =\boldsymbol{(5m+2n)} m,   Width =\boldsymbol{(5m-2n)} m
(b) The perimeter of the rink is 120 metres. Form an equation in m and n and solve for m.
The n-terms cancel: 2\big[(5m+2n)+(5m-2n)\big]=2(10m)=20m
20m=120, so \boldsymbol{m=6}
(c) Determine the length and width of the rink if the length is 50% greater than the width.
“50% greater” means length =1.5\times width: 5m+2n=1.5(5m-2n)=7.5m-3n\Rightarrow5n=2.5m\Rightarrow n=0.5m
With m=6: n=3. Length =5(6)+2(3)=\boldsymbol{36} m, width =5(6)-2(3)=\boldsymbol{24} m.
Check: 36=1.5\times24 ✓ and 2(36+24)=120 ✓
Check the area. With m=6 and n=3: 25×6x²−4×3x²ENTER gives 864, and so does 36\times24 ✓

Run it backwards: mental math

Two numbers the same distance from a friendly number multiply to a difference of squares: 58\times62=(60-2)(60+2)=60^2-2^2. And a difference of two big squares is easier factored: 49^2-51^2=(49-51)(49+51).

Check it on the calculator. 58×62ENTER and 60x²−4ENTER both give 3 596.

A ring: GCF first, then the pattern

A circle of radius R with a circle of radius r removed leaves a ring of area \pi R^2-\pi r^2. Take out the common factor \pi, and the bracket is a difference of squares: \pi(R-r)(R+r). With decimals for R and r, the factored form saves squaring them.

As a decimal. For R=6.5 and r=3.5 the ring is 30\pi: 30πENTER gives about 94.2. The long way, \pi(6.5^2-3.5^2), gives the same.
Math History1643

Fermat’s square-hunting trick

In a 1643 letter to Marin Mersenne, the French mathematician Pierre de Fermat showed how to factor big odd numbers with today’s pattern, run backwards: if N=a^2-b^2, then N=(a-b)(a+b).

Start at the first whole number a above \sqrt N and try a, a+1, a+2, … until a^2-N is a perfect square. Fermat’s own example was N=2\,027\,651\,281. On the 12th try, 45\,041^2-N=1\,020^2, so N = 44 021 × 46 061.

Factor 391 Fermat’s way. (\sqrt{391}\approx19.8, so start at a=20.)
20^2-391=400-391=9=3^2, a square on the first try! So 391=20^2-3^2=(20-3)(20+3)=\mathbf{17\times23}.

Key ideas

A^2-B^2=(A-B)(A+B). It is x^2+bx+c with b=0: the pair is k and -k, and expanding, the middle terms cancel. One bracket +, one bracket −.
Check both terms: perfect-square coefficients, even exponents, and a minus between them. 16p^2-9q^2=(4p)^2-(3q)^2.
GCF first, then the pattern, then check every bracket: 2x^3-32x=2x(x-4)(x+4). A sum of squares like x^2+16 never factors.
Use it backwards: 58\times62=60^2-2^2=3\,596; a ring is \pi(R-r)(R+r); a rectangle of area A^2-B^2 is A+B by A-B.
