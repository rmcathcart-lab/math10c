Polynomials — Review and Preview

A polynomial is built from terms, the way a laundry pile is built from socks. Today we name polynomials and find their degree, model them with algebra tiles, and add and subtract them by gathering like terms, which sets up everything else in this unit.

I can…
  • decide whether an expression is a polynomial, and name it a monomial, binomial or trinomial
  • find the degree of a term and of a polynomial, its leading coefficient and its constant term
  • write a polynomial in ascending or descending order
  • model a polynomial with algebra tiles, and read a tile picture
  • add and subtract polynomials by collecting like terms, with tiles and without
AN4 Demonstrate an understanding of the multiplication of polynomial expressions (limited to monomials, binomials and trinomials), concretely, pictorially and symbolically.
Warm-up

Sort the Socks

Laundry day! Pair the socks by their letters and exponents, not their colours. (Tap a sock to put it on a pile.)

1
How many socks are on the line?
10 socks. Each sock is one term; the whole line is one polynomial with 10 terms.
2
Sort them into piles of matching socks. How many piles? Which is the odd sock with no partner?
4 piles: x^2, x, numbers, and the odd sock xy. Colours are a decoy: 3x and 7x^2 are both blue but don’t match.
3
Squash each pile into one sock (add them up). What socks are left?
6x^2, -2x, -2 and xy: 6x^2+xy-2x-2, 4 socks. A bare x counts as 1x.
!
The big one. Only one of these is a true matching pair. Guess first: which one?
A x^2 and 2x
B 3xy and -5yx
C 4x and 4y
D x^3 and 3x
B. yx is the same as xy (you can multiply in either order), so they match: 3xy-5yx=-2xy.
A both have an x and a 2, but the exponents differ. C same number, different letter: the number never decides a match. D the 3 is an exponent in one and a coefficient in the other.
+
Early finishers. Hang one more sock so that, after squashing, only 3 socks are left. Four different socks work. Find them all.
-6x^2, -xy, 2x or 2. Each one squashes a whole pile down to zero.

Basic Vocabulary

A variable is a letter that stands for one or more numbers. An expression such as 3m-n+7 or \dfrac{4}{y}-1 is an algebraic expression.

Monomial — a number, a variable, or a product of numbers and variables. Every variable has a positive integer exponent, and no variable is in a denominator.
7,\ \ y,\ \ 8y,\ \ -\tfrac{1}{3}pq,\ \ 0.75m^3,\ \ xyz,\ \ 3a^5b^2
Polynomial — a monomial, or a sum or difference of monomials.
9,\ \ y,\ \ 9+y,\ \ 3m+8n,\ \ y^2-6y-11
Numerical coefficient — the number multiplying the variable(s) in a monomial. In 8y it is 8; in -\tfrac13pq it is -\tfrac13; in xyz it is 1.
!
\dfrac{3}{x}-2 is not a polynomial: \dfrac{3}{x}=3x^{-1}, and a negative exponent on a variable isn’t allowed. A variable in a denominator always means a negative exponent.

Terms, and naming a polynomial by its number of terms

A polynomial is made up of one or more terms, separated by + or - signs.

Monomial1 term
5y
Binomial2 terms
y-6
Trinomial3 terms
y^2+y-6
Polynomial4 or more terms
x^3-2x^2+x-5

Mono = one, bi = two, tri = three, poly = many: like a monorail, a bicycle and a tricycle.

Example 1

Is it a polynomial, and what is it called?

For each expression: state whether it represents a polynomial; if it does not, explain why; and if it does, state whether it is a monomial, binomial, or trinomial.

(a) \dfrac{2}{5}mn^3+7
Every exponent on a variable is a positive integer: a polynomial.
2 terms: binomial
(b) 5x^{2/3}y
The exponent \tfrac{2}{3} on x is not a positive integer.
Not a polynomial
(c) \sqrt{3}\,x^5-2x^2+6
\sqrt3 is only a coefficient; the exponents 5 and 2 are fine.
3 terms: trinomial
(d) 4y^3-7y+2y^{0.5}
The exponent 0.5 on y is not a positive integer.
Not a polynomial
(e) \dfrac{9}{b^2}
\dfrac{9}{b^2}=9b^{-2}: a negative exponent on a variable.
Not a polynomial
Think \sqrt{3}\,x^5 is allowed in a polynomial, but \sqrt{x} is not. Why?
\sqrt3 is just a number (the coefficient), and any number is fine there. But \sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}: the exponent on the variable is \tfrac12, not a positive integer.

Classifying Polynomials by Degree

Degree of a monomial — the sum of the exponents of its variable(s).
3x^4 has degree 4  ·  -\tfrac{5}{6}ab^2c^3 has degree 1+2+3=6
Degree of a polynomial — the degree of the term with the highest degree.
2x^2y^3-4x^5+xy^4-8 has degree 5
Constant term — a term of degree zero (no variable present). In 2x^2y^3-4x^5+xy^4-8 the constant term is -8.
!
Don’t add up every exponent in the polynomial. 2x^2y^3-4x^5+xy^4-8 has degree 5, not 2+3+5+1+4=15. Find each term’s degree, then take the highest.

3

Polynomials in a Single Variable

Descending order — powers decreasing.
-x^2+7x+4  (powers 2, 1, 0)
Ascending order — powers increasing.
4+7x-x^2  (powers 0, 1, 2)
Leading coefficient — the numerical coefficient of the term with the highest power of the variable. In 4+7x-x^2 it is -1, even though that term is written last.
!
A term keeps its sign when it moves. In 4-x^2+7x, the -x^2 card carries its minus sign wherever it goes.
Example 4

Ordering, leading coefficient and constant term

Consider the polynomial expression 3x-5x^3+9+\dfrac{2x^2}{3}.

(a) Write the polynomial in descending powers of x.
Terms -5x^3,\ \tfrac23x^2,\ 3x,\ 9; highest power first:
\boldsymbol{-5x^3+\tfrac{2}{3}x^2+3x+9}
(b) Write the polynomial in ascending powers of x.
Lowest power first:
\boldsymbol{9+3x+\tfrac{2}{3}x^2-5x^3}
(c) State the leading coefficient and the constant term.
Highest power x^3: leading coefficient -5.
No variable: constant term 9.
(d) State the numerical coefficient of the term in x^2.
\dfrac{2x^2}{3}=\dfrac{2}{3}x^2, so the coefficient is \boldsymbol{\tfrac{2}{3}}.
Think Why does a constant term like 9 have degree 0?
Because x^0=1, we can write 9=9x^0. The power of x is 0.

Representing Polynomials with Algebra Tiles

Each tile’s area is the term it stands for. The shape tells you which power of x; the shading tells you the sign.

Our legend: a shaded tile is positive; an unshaded (outline only) tile is negative.
!
The x-tile isn’t a whole number of unit tiles long, on purpose: x can stand for any number. That’s why you can’t trade an x-tile for unit tiles.

Reading a tile picture

Go the other way: count each shape, write shaded tiles as + and unshaded tiles as -.

Adding and Subtracting Polynomials

Zero pair — one shaded and one unshaded tile of the same shape. Together they are worth 0 (x+(-x)=0), so a zero pair can be removed without changing the value.

The tile mat: add tiles with the buttons, tap a tile to take it away. Shaded tiles sit above the line and unshaded below, so zero pairs line up.

Subtracting: add the opposite

Subtracting a polynomial is the same as adding its opposite. The opposite of a polynomial has every sign changed; in tiles, every tile is flipped:

(5x+2)-(3x-7)=(5x+2)+(-3x+7)

!
Flip every tile, not just the first. -(3x-7)=-3x+7, not -3x-7. The minus sign belongs to the whole bracket.
Example 6

Adding and subtracting with tiles

Use algebra tiles (or reasoning about zero pairs) to determine each result.

(a) Addition: (3x^2-4)+(x^2+5x-2) The tiles for (a) are shown as a model.
3x^2-4
+
x^2+5x-2
x^2-tiles: 3x^2+x^2=4x^2; x-tiles only in the second polynomial: 5x; unit tiles: -4-2=-6. No zero pairs form.
(3x^2-4)+(x^2+5x-2)=\boldsymbol{4x^2+5x-6}
(b) Subtraction: (-2x^2+5x-3)-(3x^2-2x-4)
Add the opposite: (-2x^2+5x-3)+(-3x^2+2x+4).
x^2: 2 unshaded join 3 unshaded, -5x^2 (nothing cancels); x: 5x+2x=7x; units: 3 unshaded meet 4 shaded, 3 zero pairs cancel and 1 is left.
(-2x^2+5x-3)-(3x^2-2x-4)=\boldsymbol{-5x^2+7x+1}
Check (b) by substituting x=2. Equal polynomials give the same value for every x. Type the original with 2 in place of each x (use (−) for the negative): it gives −5. The answer: (−)5×2x²+7×2+1ENTER also gives −5 ✓.
Think Kai wrote (5x+2)-(3x-7)=2x-5. What did he do wrong? What’s the right answer?
He only subtracted the first term: the -7 must flip too. (5x+2)+(-3x+7)=\boldsymbol{2x+9}. Check at x=2: 12-(-1)=13 and 2(2)+9=13 ✓.

Like terms: no tiles needed

Like terms — the same variable(s) raised to the same exponent(s).
4p,\ 9p,\ p  ·  3y^3,\ \tfrac14y^3,\ -6y^3
Unlike terms — different variables, or the same variable with different exponents.
3y^3,\ \tfrac14y^2,\ -6y  ·  5m and 5n

Only like terms can be combined into a single term: add their coefficients and keep the variable part. Tiles can only show x^2, x and 1, but the idea works for any variables: sort the terms into families.

!
x^2+x is not x^3 or 2x^2. They aren’t like terms (different tile shapes!), so x^2+x is already simplified.
!
xy and yx are like terms (multiply in any order), but m^2n and mn^2 are not: same letters, different exponents.
Example 7

Collecting like terms, horizontally and vertically

Simplify each of the following by collecting like terms.

(a) (5p-3q+4r)+(2q-5p-7r)
p: 5p-5p=0; q: -3q+2q=-q; r: 4r-7r=-3r
=\boldsymbol{-q-3r}
(b) \begin{array}{r}6x^2-5x+4\\-\ (3x^2-8x-2)\\\hline\end{array}
Column by column: 6x^2-3x^2=3x^2; -5x-(-8x)=3x; 4-(-2)=6
=\boldsymbol{3x^2+3x+6}
Example 8

Collecting like terms in longer expressions

Simplify.

(a) 7y-3y^2+4-5y^2+9-2y
y^2: -3y^2-5y^2=-8y^2; y: 7y-2y=5y; numbers: 4+9=13
=\boldsymbol{-8y^2+5y+13}
(b) m^2n-mn^2+5m^3n-6mn^2+4m^2n
m^3n: 5m^3n; m^2n: m^2n+4m^2n=5m^2n; mn^2: -mn^2-6mn^2=-7mn^2
=\boldsymbol{5m^3n+5m^2n-7mn^2}

Key ideas

A polynomial is a sum of terms whose variables have positive integer exponents. Name it by its terms: 1 monomial, 2 binomial, 3 trinomial, 4 or more polynomial.
A term’s degree is the sum of its exponents; a polynomial’s degree is its highest term degree. The leading coefficient belongs to the highest power.
Algebra tiles: the shape says x^2, x or 1; shaded is positive, unshaded is negative. A zero pair is worth 0.
Combine only like terms: add the coefficients, keep the variable part. To subtract, add the opposite: flip every sign in the bracket.
