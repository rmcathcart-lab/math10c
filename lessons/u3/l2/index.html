Math 10C · Unit 3 Polynomial Operations · Lesson 2

Multiplying Monomials and Binomials

Cut a rectangle into pieces and its area splits into the pieces’ areas. That one picture explains how to multiply polynomials, from 5(x+2) all the way to (x+4)(x+1), and why 41\times53 is secretly the same problem. Today: area models, algebra tiles, the distributive property and FOIL.

I can…
  • draw an area model for a monomial times a binomial, and for a binomial times a binomial
  • use the distributive property to expand a product, then collect like terms
  • multiply binomials with algebra tiles, using the shading rule and zero pairs
  • find a simplified area expression for a composite shape in two ways
  • use FOIL for a binomial times a binomial, and check any expansion by substitution
AN4 Demonstrate an understanding of the multiplication of polynomial expressions (limited to monomials, binomials and trinomials), concretely, pictorially and symbolically.

1

The Area Model

The area of a rectangle is length × width. Cut a rectangle into two side-by-side pieces and its area splits into the sum of the two smaller areas. That gives a picture for multiplying a monomial by a binomial: build a rectangle with those side lengths, split it along the binomial’s two terms, and add the pieces.

A rectangle 5 tall and x+2 wide has area 5(x+2). Its two pieces have areas 5x and 10. Both describe the same rectangle, so 5(x+2)=5x+10.
Negative entries. When a factor contains a subtraction, as in -2(x-4), keep the same picture and write the signed entries (-2 and -4) on its sides. A real length can’t be negative, so read it as a bookkeeping area model: it keeps track of every product, and a negative sign simply gives that piece a negative area.
Example 1

Monomial times binomial, drawn as an area

Use an area model to determine each product. Sketch the rectangle, label the two side lengths of each piece, and state the resulting sum (or difference) of areas.

(a) 4(x+3)
4(x+3)=\boldsymbol{4x+12}
(b) x(x+5)
x(x+5)=\boldsymbol{x^2+5x}
(c) -2(x-4)
Signed entries; (-2)(-4)=+8
-2(x-4)=\boldsymbol{-2x+8}
(d) 3x(x-1)
3x(x-1)=\boldsymbol{3x^2-3x}
(e) (x-3)(2x)
Put the monomial first: (x-3)(2x)=2x(x-3)
(x-3)(2x)=\boldsymbol{2x^2-6x}

The Distributive Property

Every product in Example 1 is an instance of one rule. The monomial multiplies each term inside the bracket, which is exactly what the pieces of the rectangle did.

Distributive property
a(b+c)=ab+ac
Monomial written second
(b+c)a=ba+ca
Any number of terms
a(b+c+d)=ab+ac+ad
Expanding means writing a product of polynomials as a sum or difference of terms. It combines the distributive property with the exponent rule x^a\times x^b=x^{a+b}: for example x^3\cdot x^2=x^{3+2}=x^5.
Example 2

Distributing over two and three terms

Use the distributive property to determine the following products.

(a) 5(4x+2)
5(4x)+5(2)=\boldsymbol{20x+10}
(b) -3(3x^2+x-5)
-3(3x^2)+(-3)(x)+(-3)(-5)
=\boldsymbol{-9x^2-3x+15}
(c) (x^3-3)x^2
Monomial written second: x^3\cdot x^2-3\cdot x^2
=\boldsymbol{x^5-3x^2}
(d) -4x(5x-3y+z)
-4x(5x)+(-4x)(-3y)+(-4x)(z)
=\boldsymbol{-20x^2+12xy-4xz}
3

Expanding and Simplifying

When an expression has more than one set of brackets, expand each bracket first, then combine like terms.
!
Don’t do 8-3 first. In 8-3(5x+2) the 3 is stuck to the bracket: multiply before you subtract. 8-15x-6, not 5(5x+2).
!
A bare minus means -1. -(6x+2x^2)=-6x-2x^2: every sign inside the bracket flips, not just the first.
Example 3

Expand every bracket, then collect

Expand and simplify.

(a) 8-3(5x+2)
Distribute the -3: 8-15x-6
Collect: =\boldsymbol{-15x+2}
(b) 3(4x-2)-3(x-5)
Distribute both: 12x-6-3x+15
Collect: =\boldsymbol{9x+9}
(c) 4x(2x^2-5x+3)-(6x+2x^2)
The bare - flips both signs:
8x^3-20x^2+12x-6x-2x^2
Collect: =\boldsymbol{8x^3-22x^2+6x}
4

Applying the Distributive Property: Area of Composite Shapes

An irregular (composite) shape gets a simplified area expression in two ways:

Add the areas of two rectangles that make up the shape.
Subtract a missing corner from a larger rectangle.

Both must give the same expression, so doing it both ways is a built-in check.

Example 4

One shape, two methods

Determine a simplified expression for the area of the shape below.

(i) by adding the areas of two rectangles
Cut across at the step. Top: (5x+4) by 3x. Bottom: (2x+7) by 6x-3x=3x.
A=(5x+4)(3x)+(2x+7)(3x)
=(15x^2+12x)+(6x^2+21x)=\boldsymbol{21x^2+33x}
(ii) by subtracting the area of one rectangle from another
Outer rectangle (5x+4) by 6x. Missing corner: (5x+4)-(2x+7)=3x-3 by 6x-3x=3x.
A=(5x+4)(6x)-(3x-3)(3x)
=(30x^2+24x)-(9x^2-9x)=\boldsymbol{21x^2+33x} ✓ same

Multiplying Binomials Using an Area Model

Think of (x+4)(x+1) as the area of a rectangle of length x+4 and width x+1. Splitting both sides breaks it into four smaller rectangles, and adding their areas gives the product in expanded form. It’s the garden from the warm-up, corner and all.

The rectangle doesn’t have to be drawn to scale every time. The four-cell grid is a quick sketch of the same area model: one factor’s terms across the top, the other’s down the side, and each cell is a product. In this course both pictures are called an area model.
!
Four pieces, not two. (x+4)(x+1)\neq x^2+4. That only counts the two corner pieces; the strips 4x and x are part of the area too. Every piece of the rectangle counts, just like the corner in the warm-up.

Multiplying with algebra tiles

Algebra tiles build the same area model out of real pieces. Lay the tiles for one factor across the top of a frame and the tiles for the other factor down the side. Then fill the rectangle: each tile inside is the product of the factor tile above it and the factor tile beside it.

Count the tiles inside the frame to read the product, removing any zero pairs (one shaded and one unshaded tile of the same kind add to zero).

Example 5

Multiplying with algebra tiles

The factor tiles are already in place. Fill in each rectangle using the shading rule, then state the product. Look for zero pairs before you write the answer.

(a) (x+2)(x-1)
One x^2, two shaded and one unshaded x-tile (a zero pair), two unshaded units.
x^2+2x-x-2=\boldsymbol{x^2+x-2}
(b) (x-2)(x-1)
One x^2, three unshaded x-tiles, two shaded units (unshaded × unshaded).
x^2-2x-x+2=\boldsymbol{x^2-3x+2}
Example 6

Area models as four-cell grids

Use an area model to determine each product.

(a) (x+5)(x+2)
x^2+5x+2x+10=\boldsymbol{x^2+7x+10}
(b) (a^2-3)(a^2-7)
a^4-3a^2-7a^2+21=\boldsymbol{a^4-10a^2+21}
(c) (2p+3q)(p+8q)
2p^2+3pq+16pq+24q^2
=\boldsymbol{2p^2+19pq+24q^2}

The same grid multiplies two-digit numbers

Split each number into tens and ones, then add the four partial products. No calculator needed, and it shows why the grid works: let t=10, and every two-digit number is a binomial in t.

Check the grid on the calculator. 41×53ENTER gives 2 173, the same as 2\,000+50+120+3.
Example 7

The same grid on two-digit numbers

Use an area model and no calculator to determine each product.

(a) 32\times45
Split 32=30+2 and 45=40+5.
1\,200+80+150+10=\boldsymbol{1\,440}
(b) 67\times84
Split 67=60+7 and 84=80+4.
4\,800+560+240+28=\boldsymbol{5\,628}
Think Sam says 41\times53=40\times50+1\times3=2\,003. Use the grid to explain what Sam forgot.
Sam only used two of the four cells. The other two, 50\times1=50 and 3\times40=120, are missing: 2\,003+170=\boldsymbol{2\,173}. It’s the same mistake as (x+4)(x+1)=x^2+4.
6

Multiplying Binomials Using the Distributive Property and FOIL

Distributing a whole binomial works exactly like distributing a monomial: treat (c+d) as one thing, distribute, then distribute again.

(a+b)(c+d)=a(c+d)+b(c+d)=ac+ad+bc+bd

These are the same four terms the area model produced. The grid and the distributive property just organize the same calculation differently.

Example 8

Distributing a binomial

Use the distributive property to determine each product.

(a) (x+5)(x+3)
x(x+3)+5(x+3)=x^2+3x+5x+15
=\boldsymbol{x^2+8x+15}
(b) (a-6)(3a-2)
a(3a-2)-6(3a-2)=3a^2-2a-18a+12
=\boldsymbol{3a^2-20a+12}
(c) (m-9)(n-9)
m(n-9)-9(n-9)=mn-9m-9n+81
No like terms: \boldsymbol{mn-9m-9n+81}
(d) (x+3y)(x-8y)
x(x-8y)+3y(x-8y)=x^2-8xy+3xy-24y^2
=\boldsymbol{x^2-5xy-24y^2}
Think Parts (a), (b) and (d) each had two middle terms to collect. Why didn’t (c)?
In (c) the two binomials use different letters, so the middle products -9m and -9n aren’t like terms. In the others both middle products have the same letters (3x and 5x; -8xy and 3xy).

FOIL: a memory device for the four products

Those four products always follow the same pattern, so they can be remembered with the acronym FOIL. It is not a new method: it applies the distributive property to two binomials in a fixed order, so no term is missed.

Firstac
Outsidead
Insidebc
Lastbd

FOIL is only the 2 × 2 case

FOIL works only for a binomial times a binomial: exactly 2\times2=4 products. A binomial times a trinomial (Lesson 4) has 2\times3=6 products, and FOIL would miss two of them. The distributive property, “every term times every term”, always works.

Example 9

FOIL

Use FOIL to determine each product.

(a) (x+7)(x+2)
F x^2; O 2x; I 7x; L 14
x^2+2x+7x+14=\boldsymbol{x^2+9x+14}
(b) (y-4)(y+6)
F y^2; O 6y; I -4y; L -24
y^2+6y-4y-24=\boldsymbol{y^2+2y-24}
(c) (2x+3)(x-5)
F 2x^2; O -10x; I 3x; L -15
2x^2-10x+3x-15=\boldsymbol{2x^2-7x-15}
(d) (4a-3b)^2
Write it out: (4a-3b)(4a-3b). F 16a^2; O -12ab; I -12ab; L 9b^2
=\boldsymbol{16a^2-24ab+9b^2}
!
A square is two copies. (4a-3b)^2\neq16a^2+9b^2. Write the square as (4a-3b)(4a-3b) first; the O and I terms don’t disappear. (Lesson 3 is all about these.)

Is my expansion right? Check by substitution

A product and its expansion are equal for every value of the variable. Substitute a small number such as x=2 into both: they must give the same value. For Example 9(a): (2+7)(2+2)=36 and 2^2+9(2)+14=36 ✓

Check Example 9(a) at x=2. The product: (2+7)×(2+2)ENTER. The expansion: 2x²+9×2+14ENTER. Both give 36 ✓
!
0 and 1 make a weak test. At x=0, (x+7)(x+2) and the wrong x^2+14 both give 14, so the mistake slips past. Use 2 or 3.
Example 10

Three methods, one answer

A rectangle has length (3a+2) cm and width (a+5) cm. Determine the area of the rectangle, in cm², three ways, and confirm that all three methods agree.

(i) use an area model
3a^2+2a+15a+10
=\boldsymbol{3a^2+17a+10}
(ii) use the distributive property
3a(a+5)+2(a+5)
=3a^2+15a+2a+10
=\boldsymbol{3a^2+17a+10}
(iii) use FOIL
F 3a^2; O 15a; I 2a; L 10
=\boldsymbol{3a^2+17a+10} cm²
All three agree ✓
Check at a=2. Length 3(2)+2=8, width 2+5=7: area 56. The expansion: 3×2x²+17×2+10ENTER should also give 56.
✓

Key ideas

Area model: a rectangle split into pieces, one piece per product. Add the pieces: 5(x+2)=5x+10. Negative entries give negative “bookkeeping” areas.
Distributive property: a(b+c)=ab+ac. Multiply every term in the bracket (exponents add: x^3\cdot x^2=x^5), then collect like terms.
Binomial × binomial = four products: (a+b)(c+d)=ac+ad+bc+bd, by grid, tiles, distributing or FOIL. FOIL is only the 2 × 2 case.
Check by substitution at x=2: both forms give the same number. And two-digit multiplication is binomial multiplication: 41\times53=(40+1)(50+3).
