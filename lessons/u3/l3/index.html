Multiplying Polynomials I — Special Products

A few binomial products turn up so often that it pays to know them on sight. Today a square splits into a^2, ab, ab and b^2, we see why (a+b)^2 is never just a^2+b^2, and we cut and slide a square into a sum-times-difference rectangle. Then we use the patterns: forward, inside longer expressions, and in reverse.

I can…
  • square a binomial with the pattern: square the first, double the product, square the last
  • explain with an area picture why (a+b)^2\neq a^2+b^2
  • multiply a sum by a difference of the same two terms: (a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2
  • decide which pattern a product fits, or that it fits neither and needs FOIL
  • expand a product with a number out front, and combine several products by collecting like terms
  • write x^2+kx+c in the form (x+a)^2+b (extension)
AN4 Demonstrate an understanding of the multiplication of polynomial expressions (limited to monomials, binomials and trinomials), concretely, pictorially and symbolically.

How to square any number ending in 5, in your head.

Now try 45² and 65². Check one of them the long way (or with a calculator).
4\times5=20, then 25: 2 025.   6\times7=42, then 25: 4 225.

The picture cuts a 35 by 35 square into 30 + 5 each way. Tap each piece to find its area, then add them. Which piece makes the “25”?
900+150+150+25=1\,225. The corner is always 5\times5=25; the two strips make 300, and 900+300=1\,200=3\times4\times100.

The big one. No calculator, no paper. What is 105²? Guess first.
A 10 025

A is 100^2+5^2: it squares each part and forgets both middle strips (the classic mistake we fix today). B counts only one strip, 500 instead of 1 000.

4\times5=20, then 1\times9=09: 2 009. Why it works: 41\times49=(45-4)(45+4)=45^2-4^2=2\,025-16. That’s a sum times a difference, pattern #2 today.

1

Two Patterns Worth Memorizing

Picture a square of side length a+b. Its area is (a+b)^2, but the same square also slices into four pieces: two squares and two equal rectangles. Adding their areas instead gives a^2+ab+ab+b^2=a^2+2ab+b^2. (The warm-up square is this picture with a=30 and b=5.)

!
Watch out: (a+b)^2\neq a^2+b^2. Try numbers: (3+4)^2=7^2=49, but 3^2+4^2=9+16=25. The missing 24 is the two ab rectangles (2\times3\times4=24). Squaring a binomial always produces a middle term.
Think Is (a+b)^2=a^2+b^2 ever true? For which numbers?
Only when the missing piece is zero: 2ab=0, so a=0 or b=0. Example: (5+0)^2=25=5^2+0^2. For any other numbers the two ab rectangles are really there.

Squaring a difference: (a-b)^2

Start with a square of side a. Cut a strip of width b off the right side and another off the top. Each strip has area ab, but the two strips overlap in the b-by-b corner, so that corner gets cut away twice. Add it back once: (a-b)^2=a^2-ab-ab+b^2=a^2-2ab+b^2.

Sum times difference: (a+b)(a-b)

Multiply a sum by a difference of the same two terms and the two middle terms of FOIL are opposites, so they cancel: (a+b)(a-b)=a^2-ab+ab-b^2=a^2-b^2. The answer is a difference of squares, and a picture shows it too.

Two shortcuts to remember

Squaring a binomial
square the first term, double the product of the two terms, square the last term
(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2
(a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2
3 terms. The middle sign matches the bracket; the last term is always +.
Sum times difference
the sum and the difference of the same two terms multiply to the difference of their squares
(a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2
2 terms. No middle term: -ab and +ab cancel.

Before expanding, check which pattern fits. Same two terms in both brackets? Same signs: a square. Opposite signs: sum times difference. Anything else: neither pattern, so use FOIL.

Example 1

Recognizing the two patterns

For each product, first decide whether one of the two patterns applies. If it does, use it to expand; if not, expand with FOIL.

(a) (x+9)^2
=x^2+2(x)(9)+9^2
=\boldsymbol{x^2+18x+81}
(b) (4x-1)^2
=(4x)^2-2(4x)(1)+1^2
=\boldsymbol{16x^2-8x+1}
(c) (3m-2n)^2
=(3m)^2-2(3m)(2n)
\phantom{=}+(2n)^2
=\boldsymbol{9m^2-12mn+4n^2}
(d) (6-5x)(6+5x)
Sum times difference of 6 and 5x:
=6^2-(5x)^2=\boldsymbol{36-25x^2}
(e) (4a+7b)(4a-7b)
=(4a)^2-(7b)^2
=\boldsymbol{16a^2-49b^2}
(f) (3p-8q)(3p-8q)
Same binomial twice, so it is a square:
=(3p)^2-2(3p)(8q)
\phantom{=}+(8q)^2
=\boldsymbol{9p^2-48pq+64q^2}
(g) (x+3)(x-5)
Not a pattern: the second terms differ (+3 and -5). FOIL:
x^2-5x+3x-15
=\boldsymbol{x^2-2x-15}
(h) (-2x+5)^2
Rewrite first: (5-2x)^2, a square.
=5^2-2(5)(2x)+(2x)^2
=25-20x+4x^2
=\boldsymbol{4x^2-20x+25}
!
Square the whole term. In (b), the first term is 4x, so its square is (4x)^2=16x^2, not 4x^2. Brackets around each term keep the coefficient in the square.

Special products as mental math

The patterns work for numbers too. Split a number into a friendly part and a small part, and the arithmetic almost disappears. Two numbers the same distance from a friendly number make a sum times a difference.

Check it on the calculator. 49×51ENTER and 50x²−1ENTER both give 2 499.

A Coefficient Out Front

A special product may be multiplied by a number (or a negative sign) sitting outside the brackets. Expand the binomial product first, then distribute the outside factor across every resulting term.
!
Don’t push the number inside a square. 3(x+2)^2\neq(3x+6)^2. The exponent belongs to the bracket only: (3x+6)^2=9(x+2)^2, three times too big.
Example 2

Expand first, then distribute

Expand and simplify.

(a) 3(4x-5)(x-2)
Expand the product first:
(4x-5)(x-2)=4x^2-8x-5x+10
\phantom{(4x-5)(x-2)}=4x^2-13x+10
Now distribute the 3:
3(4x^2-13x+10)=\boldsymbol{12x^2-39x+30}
(b) -6(5p+2)^2
Expand the square first: (5p+2)^2=25p^2+20p+4
Now distribute the -6 across every term:
-6(25p^2+20p+4)=\boldsymbol{-150p^2-120p-24}
3

Combining Two or Three Products

A longer expression may add or subtract several products in a row. Expand every product completely (a subtraction sign in front of a product flips the sign of every term that comes out of it), and only then collect like terms.
!
Keep the second product in brackets until the minus is used. -(x^2+12x+27)=-x^2-12x-27. Writing -x^2+12x+27 flips only the first term, and that’s the most common mistake in this section.
Example 3

Several products at once

Expand and simplify.

(a) (x+6)(x-6)-(x+3)(x+9)
(x+6)(x-6)=x^2-36 (difference of squares)
(x+3)(x+9)=x^2+12x+27
The subtraction flips every term of the second:
x^2-36-x^2-12x-27=\boldsymbol{-12x-63}
(b) 4x(2x^2-5x+3)-(2x^2+3x)(4x-7)
4x(2x^2-5x+3)=8x^3-20x^2+12x
(2x^2+3x)(4x-7)=8x^3-14x^2+12x^2-21x
\phantom{(2x^2+3x)(4x-7)}=8x^3-2x^2-21x
(8x^3-20x^2+12x)-(8x^3-2x^2-21x)
=\boldsymbol{-18x^2+33x}
The x^3 terms cancel, leaving a quadratic.
(c) 3(3x-4)(2x+3)-5(x-2)(4x-3)
(3x-4)(2x+3)=6x^2+x-12, so 3(6x^2+x-12)=18x^2+3x-36
(x-2)(4x-3)=4x^2-11x+6, so 5(4x^2-11x+6)=20x^2-55x+30
(18x^2+3x-36)-(20x^2-55x+30)=\boldsymbol{-2x^2+58x-66}
Check (c) by substitution at x=3. The original and the answer must give the same number: 3(5)(9)-5(1)(9)=90 and -2(9)+58(3)-66=90 ✓. Use the (−) key for a negative.

Extension: Matching the Form (x+a)^2+b

Extension Rewriting a trinomial this way is called completing the square, a Math 20-1 skill.

Because (x+a)^2=x^2+2ax+a^2, any trinomial x^2+kx+c can be rewritten as (x+a)^2+b: choose a so that 2a matches the middle coefficient k, then adjust b so the two expressions agree once expanded. Example 4 · Extension

Using the square pattern in reverse

For every value of x, the polynomial x^2+16x+70 can be written in the form (x+a)^2+b. Determine the values of a and b.

Expand the target form: (x+a)^2+b=x^2+2ax+(a^2+b). Match it to x^2+16x+70 term by term.
Coefficients of x: 2a=16, so a=8.  Constant terms: a^2+b=70, so 64+b=70 and b=6.
Check: (x+8)^2+6=x^2+16x+64+6=x^2+16x+70 ✓   \boldsymbol{a=8,\ b=6}
✓

Key ideas

Square of a binomial: square the first, double the product, square the last. (a\pm b)^2=a^2\pm2ab+b^2: always three terms.
(a+b)^2\neq a^2+b^2. The two ab rectangles are real: (3+4)^2=49, not 25.
Sum times difference: (a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2. The middle terms cancel, leaving a difference of squares.
A number out front waits: expand first, then distribute. A minus in front of a product flips every term. Collect like terms last.
