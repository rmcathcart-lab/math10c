Applying Polynomial Products to Area, Volume, and Equations

A polynomial can measure a shape. Today we write the area of an L-shape, a picture frame and a garden as one polynomial, multiply three factors for a volume, and solve equations hiding in a figure, where the squared terms cancel and a simple linear equation is left.

I can…
  • write the area of a composite figure as a polynomial, by adding the pieces or by taking a notch away from a bounding box
  • find the area of a frame or a region with holes: outer area − area of the hole(s)
  • write a volume as a product of three polynomials and simplify it
  • form an equation from a rule about a figure (like the Pythagorean theorem), expand both sides, and solve it when the squared terms cancel
  • check an answer by putting a number into the figure and into the polynomial
AN4 Demonstrate an understanding of the multiplication of polynomial expressions (limited to monomials, binomials and trinomials), concretely, pictorially and symbolically.

1

Composite Area Figures

A figure made entirely of horizontal and vertical segments has two common strategies for writing its area as a single polynomial.

Strategy A: add the pieces. Slice the figure into non-overlapping rectangles, find each area as a product of its side lengths, and add.
Strategy B: subtract from a bounding box. Take the smallest rectangle that contains the figure and subtract the missing corner piece (the notch).

Both give the same polynomial once expanded. Choose whichever is easier to see in a given figure.

!
Subtract the whole notch. Keep its brackets: -(x^2+5x+6)=-x^2-5x-6. Dropping the brackets gives -x^2+5x+6, which only subtracts the first term.

Strategy A on the same figure: slice it and add

A slice makes new rectangles, and some of their sides aren’t labelled. A missing side is a subtraction: the whole side minus the part you know.

Example 1

Bounding box minus a notch

The L-shaped figure below is the piece left over after a rectangular notch is cut from the top-right corner of a larger rectangle.

(a) Write the area of the figure as a trinomial ax^2+bx+c.
Box (3x+4)(2x+5)=6x^2+15x+8x+20=6x^2+23x+20
Notch (x+1)(x+2)=x^2+2x+x+2=x^2+3x+2
Area =(6x^2+23x+20)-(x^2+3x+2)=\mathbf{5x^2+20x+18}, so a=5,\ b=20,\ c=18
(b) Evaluate the area when x=2.
5(2)^2+20(2)+18=20+40+18=\mathbf{78}
Check with the figure: box 10\times9=90, notch 3\times4=12, 90-12=78 ✓
Check (b) two ways. Put x=2 into the trinomial: 5×2x²+20×2+18ENTER. Then use the figure’s own lengths at x=2: box minus notch. Both should say 78.
Think Slice the L sideways instead, along the bottom of the notch. Do you get the same trinomial?
Yes. Top piece (2x+3)(x+2)=2x^2+7x+6, bottom piece (3x+4)(x+3)=3x^2+13x+12. Sum: 5x^2+20x+18. Any slicing (or the box minus the notch) gives the same area.

Framed Regions

A rectangular frame is a rectangle with a smaller rectangle removed from its interior. The shaded (remaining) area is
shaded area = outer area − inner area

If the opening is set in by the same amount on two opposite sides, that amount comes off the outer length twice.

!
Set in on both sides means subtract twice. A frame 3 cm wide around a 40 cm board leaves an opening 40-2(3)=34 cm wide, not 37 cm.
Example 2

A frame with two different widths

In the figure below, a rectangular frame surrounds a smaller rectangular opening. The frame’s sides are p wide and its top and bottom are q wide, so the opening is set in by p on the left and right and by q along the top and bottom.

(a) Determine a simplified expression, in terms of p and q, for the shaded area.
Opening: (4p+3q)-2p=2p+3q wide, (3p+5q)-2q=3p+3q high
Outer (4p+3q)(3p+5q)=12p^2+29pq+15q^2
Inner (2p+3q)(3p+3q)=6p^2+15pq+9q^2
Shaded =\mathbf{6p^2+14pq+6q^2}
(b) Calculate the shaded area when p=2 and q=1.
6(4)+14(2)(1)+6(1)=24+28+6=\mathbf{58}
Check: outer 11\times11=121, opening 7\times9=63, 121-63=58 ✓
Two letters, two values. Type each value where its letter was: 6×2x²+14×2×1+6×1x²ENTER. The figure check is outer minus opening.
Think When p=2 and q=1, how big is the outer rectangle? Is the figure drawn to scale?
4(2)+3(1)=11 by 3(2)+5(1)=11: it’s a square, with an opening 7 wide and 9 tall. The figure is not to scale. Trust the labels, not the picture.

Modelling with Squares and Rectangles

Many real-world area problems are modelled the same way: a large region with one or more smaller regions removed from inside it.

region left over = big region − (number of pieces) × (area of one piece)
!
(n+2)^2\ne n^2+4. Squaring a binomial gives three terms: n^2+4n+4.
!
3(n+2)^2: square first, then multiply by 3. It is not (3n+6)^2.
Example 3

Garden with three flower beds

A rectangular garden has length (3n+12) m and width (n+9) m. Three square flower beds, each with side length (n+2) m, are planted inside the garden. The rest of the garden is grass.

(a) Write and simplify an expression for the area of grass in the garden.
Garden (3n+12)(n+9)=3n^2+27n+12n+108=3n^2+39n+108
Three beds 3(n+2)^2=3(n^2+4n+4)=3n^2+12n+12
Grass =\mathbf{27n+96} m² (the n^2 terms cancel)
(b) Determine the area of grass when n=2, and check your answer using the dimensions.
27(2)+96=\mathbf{150} m²
Check: garden 18\times11=198, beds 3\times4^2=48, 198-48=150 ✓
Check with the dimensions. At n=2 the garden is 18 m by 11 m and each bed is 4 m by 4 m: 18×11−3×4x²ENTER should match 27(2)+96.
Think Why did the n^2 terms cancel in this garden?
The garden’s n^2 term is 3n\times n=3n^2, and three beds give 3\times n^2=3n^2. They’re equal, so the grass area grows only linearly as n grows.

From area to volume: one more factor

A rectangular box has volume length × width × height, so its volume is a product of three polynomials. Multiply two of them first (the base area), then multiply that result by the third (the height).

!
Pick a friendly pair first. In (x-2)(x+5)(x-5), the pair (x+5)(x-5) is a difference of squares, x^2-25: multiply that pair first and only four partial products are left.
Check a cubic. x^3+6x^2+11x+6 at x=2: 2^3+6×2x²+11×2+6ENTER gives 60, and so does 3\times4\times5.
4

Equation Solving

Some problems describe a figure with polynomial expressions plus a rule that must hold, for example the Pythagorean theorem for a right triangle. Expanding both sides of the resulting equation often cancels the highest-degree terms, leaving a linear equation to solve.

1Write the rule with the polynomials in it.
2Expand each side and collect like terms.
3The x^2 terms cancel: solve the linear equation.
4Check: put the value back into the figure.
!
The squared terms must match exactly to cancel. If they don’t, recheck the squares: the middle term is the usual casualty. (4x+8)^2=16x^2+64x+64, not 16x^2+64.
Think A square photo gets a frame 2 cm wide all the way around, and the frame’s area is 96 cm². How big is the photo?
Side s: outside s+4. (s+4)^2-s^2=96\Rightarrow 8s+16=96\Rightarrow s=\mathbf{10} cm. The s^2 terms cancel. (Replay it with the Framed photo chip above.)
Example 4

The Pythagorean theorem with polynomial sides

The hypotenuse of a right triangle is (5x+1) cm long, and the lengths of the other two sides are (4x+8) cm and (3x-15) cm.

Form an equation using the Pythagorean theorem, and solve it to determine the lengths of all three sides of the triangle.
(5x+1)^2=(4x+8)^2+(3x-15)^2
25x^2+10x+1=(16x^2+64x+64)+(9x^2-90x+225)
25x^2+10x+1=25x^2-26x+289
36x=288, so x=8
The sides are 9 cm, 40 cm and 41 cm (check: 9^2+40^2=1\,681=41^2 ✓)
Check the triangle. The two legs squared and added should equal the hypotenuse squared: 9x²+40x²ENTER and 41x²ENTER both give 1 681.
Think Why must x be greater than 5 in this triangle?
A side length must be positive: 3x-15>0 means x>5. Our x=8 passes, giving 3(8)-15=9 cm.
✓

Key ideas

Composite figures: add the pieces (A) or take the notch from the bounding box (B). A missing side is a subtraction. Both give the same polynomial.
Frames and holes: shaded = outer − hole(s). An opening set in on both sides loses that width twice. Keep brackets when you subtract.
Volume: three factors. Multiply a friendly pair first, then the third, and collect like terms.
Equations from figures: expand both sides, the squared terms cancel, solve the linear equation, then check with real lengths.
