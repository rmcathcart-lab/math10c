Right Triangle Trigonometry: A Cumulative Review

The whole unit in one lesson. Every problem starts the same way: find a right triangle (or make one), stand at an angle, and name the sides opposite, adjacent and hypotenuse. Then pick the tool: SOH CAH TOA, an inverse ratio, Pythagoras, or 90^\circ minus the other angle.

I can…
  • name opposite, adjacent and hypotenuse from either acute angle, write \sin, \cos and \tan, and use the cofunction relationship
  • find a missing side (unknown on top: multiply; on the bottom: divide) and a missing angle (\sin^{-1}, \cos^{-1}, \tan^{-1})
  • turn an angle of elevation or depression into a labelled right triangle
  • split an oblique or isosceles triangle into right triangles along an altitude, and use it for area
  • Extension use bearings (and 3-D right triangles)
M4 Develop and apply the primary trigonometric ratios (sine, cosine, tangent) to solve problems that involve right triangles.
ExtensionBearings (the last teaching card) and review questions 17–18 (bearings and 3-D) go beyond the Math 10C outcome. Everything else is core M4.

Naming Sides and the Primary Ratios

Sine, cosine and tangent are defined only relative to a chosen acute angle of a right triangle, so every problem starts by naming which leg is opposite that angle, which leg is adjacent to it, and which side is the hypotenuse (always opposite the right angle, always the longest side). \sin\theta=\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}\qquad\cos\theta=\frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}\qquad\tan\theta=\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}
The two acute angles of a right triangle are complementary (they add to 90^\circ), which is why the sine of one always equals the cosine of the other: the cofunction relationship, \sin x^\circ=\cos(90-x)^\circ.
!
The hypotenuse is never “adjacent”. It touches the angle too, but it has its own name. Adjacent is the leg that touches the angle.

Why sine of one is cosine of the other

Solving for an Unknown Side

With one acute angle and one side length known, SOH CAH TOA gives any other side: name the known side and the unknown side relative to the given angle, pick the ratio that holds both names, write the equation, then multiply or divide to isolate the unknown.
Unknown on top: multiply. \tan42^\circ=\dfrac{x}{11}\ \Rightarrow\ x=11\tan42^\circ
Unknown on the bottom: multiply by it, then divide. \cos42^\circ=\dfrac{11}{x}\ \Rightarrow\ x=\dfrac{11}{\cos42^\circ}
!
Round only at the end. Keep the calculator’s full value of \tan42^\circ (or type the whole expression in one go) and round the final answer.

Solving for an Unknown Angle

Finding an angle runs SOH CAH TOA in reverse: with two sides known, name them relative to the unknown angle, evaluate the matching ratio as a decimal, then apply \sin^{-1}, \cos^{-1} or \tan^{-1} to recover the angle.
Think In Example 3, why can you state the other acute angle in part (b) without the calculator, but not the hypotenuse in part (c)?
The acute angles always add to 90^\circ, so (b) is one subtraction. The hypotenuse needs a computation: Pythagoras with the two legs (or a trig ratio with the angle from (a)).

Elevation, Depression, and Applied Problems

The angle of elevation and the angle of depression are both measured between a horizontal line of sight and the line of sight up to, or down to, an object. The horizontal at the observer and the horizontal at the object are parallel, so the angle of elevation of an object from an observer equals the angle of depression of that observer from the object.

Translate the words into a labelled right triangle first; SOH CAH TOA then solves it directly. If an angle is measured from someone’s eyes, the triangle starts at eye level: add the eye height at the end.

Think A student puts the angle of depression at the top corner of the triangle, inside the cliff. What goes wrong, and how big is the angle that really sits there?
Depression is measured down from the horizontal, not from the vertical cliff. The angle inside the triangle at the top is 90^\circ minus the angle of depression, so the opposite and adjacent sides get swapped and the answer is wrong.

Combining Two Right Triangles

A triangle with no right angle can still be solved once an altitude is dropped from one vertex to the opposite side. The altitude splits the figure into two right triangles that share it as a common leg, and each of those is solved on its own; the results are then added or subtracted.
!
Add the unrounded pieces. 19.8089\ldots+32.6163\ldots=52.4252\ldots\approx52.4. Rounding each piece first can make the total come out a tenth off.

Isosceles Triangles and Area

Dropping an altitude from the apex of an isosceles triangle (where the two equal sides meet) to the base splits it into two congruent right triangles; the altitude bisects both the base and the apex angle. That same altitude is the height in the area formula A=\tfrac12(\text{base})(\text{height}), so a given area supplies the height.
Example 6

A=\tfrac12(\text{base})(\text{height})

Bearings

Extension
A bearing describes a direction as an angle measured clockwise from north, always written with three digits (065^\circ, not 65^\circ). To use a bearing in a right triangle, work with the acute angle between the direction and the north–south line.
000^\circ–090^\circthe bearing, from north (toward E)
090^\circ–180^\circ180^\circ-bearing, from south (toward E)
180^\circ–270^\circbearing -\,180^\circ, from south (toward W)
270^\circ–360^\circ360^\circ-bearing, from north (toward W)

For a leg of length d, the leg is the hypotenuse: the east–west part is opposite the acute angle (d\sin) and the north–south part is adjacent (d\cos). Review questions 17 and 18 (bearings and 3-D) use these ideas.

Extension
17. (Extension) A boat leaves harbour H and sails 14 km on a bearing of 062^\circ to a marker M.
(a) Determine, to the nearest tenth of a kilometre, how far north of H and how far east of H the marker M is.
A bearing of 062^\circ is 62^\circ clockwise from north, so in the right triangle formed with the north line the 14 km leg is the hypotenuse and 62^\circ sits at H.
north =14\cos62^\circ=6.5726\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{6.6\textbf{ km}}
east =14\sin62^\circ=12.3612\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{12.4\textbf{ km}}
(b) From M the boat sails due south for 9.4 km to a point N. Determine the distance HN to the nearest tenth of a kilometre, and the three-figure bearing of N from H to the nearest degree.
Sailing 9.4 km south leaves N at 12.3612 km east of H and 9.4-6.5726=2.8274 km south of H.
HN=\sqrt{12.3612^2+2.8274^2}=\sqrt{160.80}=12.6805\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{12.7\textbf{ km}}
Due east is the bearing 090^\circ, and N lies below that line by \tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{2.8274}{12.3612}\right)=12.88^\circ, so the bearing of N from H is 90^\circ+12.88^\circ=102.88^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{103^\circ}
18. (Extension) A vertical flagpole AP stands at corner A of a horizontal rectangular courtyard ABCD, where AB=12.0 m, AD=5.0 m and AP=8.0 m.
(a) Determine the length of the diagonal AC, to the nearest tenth of a metre.
AC is the diagonal of rectangle ABCD: AC=\sqrt{12.0^2+5.0^2}=\sqrt{169}=\boldsymbol{13.0\textbf{ m}}
(b) Determine the angle of elevation of P from C, to the nearest tenth of a degree.
AP is vertical and AC lies in the horizontal courtyard, so \triangle PAC is right-angled at A. From C: opp =AP=8.0, adj =AC=13.0: TOA.
\tan(\angle PCA)=\dfrac{8.0}{13.0}=0.6153\ldots\ \Rightarrow\ \angle PCA=31.61^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{31.6^\circ}

Key ideas

Name the sides first. Stand at the angle: opposite doesn’t touch it, adjacent is the leg that does, the hypotenuse faces the right angle. Switch angles and the legs swap names: \sin x^\circ=\cos(90-x)^\circ.
Side: SOH CAH TOA. Unknown on top: multiply; on the bottom: divide. Angle: \sin^{-1}, \cos^{-1}, \tan^{-1} of the ratio. Two sides and no angle wanted: Pythagoras. Round only at the end.
Elevation = depression, both measured from the horizontal (parallel horizontals, the Z). Angles from someone’s eyes: add the eye height.
No right angle? Drop an altitude. Two right triangles share it; solve each, then add or subtract. Isosceles: the altitude halves the base and the apex angle, and it is the height in A=\frac12bh.
