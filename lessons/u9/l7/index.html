Math 10C · Unit 9 Trigonometry · Lesson 7

Chaining Right Triangles Together

Two right triangles joined by a shared side: solve the one that has enough information, then hand the shared side to the other. Towers, altitudes, two sightings from the ground, and (Extension) boxes and wedges in 3-D.

I can…
  • find the shared side of two right triangles and decide which triangle to solve first
  • carry the shared side into the second triangle at full calculator accuracy, and round only the final answer
  • find a height from an angle of depression and an angle of elevation taken from the top of another structure
  • use an altitude to find a length or an angle in a triangle split into two right triangles
  • find a height from two angles of elevation taken a known distance apart, by writing both distances in terms of h and subtracting
  • Extension solve 3-D problems: the diagonal of a box, and a right triangle hidden in the side face of a wedge
M4 Develop and apply the primary trigonometric ratios (sine, cosine, tangent) to solve problems that involve right triangles.
Warm-up

The Unreachable Cliff

A river blocks the cliff’s base. You measure two angles to the top, 40 m apart. Walk from A to B: the angle to the top grows from 30^\circ to 60^\circ. Angles 1 and 2 are angles of the shaded triangle.

1
Angle 1 sits next to the 60^\circ angle on a straight line. What is it?
180^\circ-60^\circ=\mathbf{120^\circ}
2
The shaded triangle’s angles add to 180^\circ. What is angle 2?
180^\circ-30^\circ-120^\circ=\mathbf{30^\circ}
3
Two equal angles! So how long is the sight line from B to the top?
40 m. The angle at A and angle 2 are both 30^\circ, so the triangle is isosceles: the side opposite A equals the side opposite angle 2, the 40 m walk.
!
The big one. Without ever crossing the river: how tall is the cliff? Guess first.
A 20 m
B about 35 m
C 40 m
D about 69 m
B: about 35 m. Hand the 40 m sight line to the right triangle at the cliff. It is the hypotenuse, and the height is opposite 60^\circ: h=40\sin 60^\circ\approx34.6 m. Check: from A the base is 20+40=60 m away, and \tan^{-1}(34.64\div60)=30^\circ, as measured.
A (20 m) is 40\cos 60^\circ: the adjacent side, B’s distance to the cliff base, not the height. C (40 m) mistakes the sight line (the hypotenuse) for the height. D (69 m) is 40\tan 60^\circ: pretending B is 40 m from the base.
The big one on the TI-30XIIS. 40×SIN60)ENTER gives 34.64…, and the check 2ndTAN40×SIN60)÷60)ENTER gives 30^\circ.

1

Indirect Measurement Across Two Structures

Two right triangles in a diagram are often joined along one side: an altitude, a vertical building, a common horizontal distance. That shared side is the link: solve the triangle in which enough is known, then carry the shared side into the second triangle to finish.

Never round the shared side. Keep every value computed partway through a problem at full calculator accuracy and round only the final answer. Solve from the labelled numbers, never by measuring the picture.
1Find the triangle with enough: a known side and a known angle (or two known sides).
2Solve it for the shared side. Keep every digit (or use the whole calculator expression).
3Carry it into the other triangle, pick SOH, CAH or TOA there, and finish.

When an observer at the top of one structure sights both the top and the base of a second structure, the angle of depression to the base gives the horizontal distance between the structures (using the first structure’s height), and the angle of elevation to the top gives how much taller the second structure is. The two pieces add to the second structure’s full height.

Example 1

Two towers, one shared horizontal distance

Ashgrove Tower and Birchwood Tower stand 38 m apart. From the top of Ashgrove Tower, the angle of depression to the base of Birchwood Tower is 51^\circ, and the angle of elevation to the top of Birchwood Tower is 22^\circ. Determine the height of each tower, to the nearest metre.

Height of each tower
Shared side: the 38 m horizontal distance, a leg of both right triangles. Solve the Ashgrove triangle first: it has a known side and a known angle.
Depression to Birchwood’s base = elevation of Ashgrove’s top seen from that base (alternate angles between parallel horizontals):
\tan 51^\circ=\dfrac{\text{Ashgrove}}{38} \Rightarrow \text{Ashgrove}=38\tan 51^\circ =46.92609\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{47\text{ m}}
The same 38 m with the 22^\circ elevation gives how far Birchwood rises above Ashgrove: extra height =38\tan 22^\circ=15.35300\ldots m
Add the unrounded values: Birchwood =46.92609+15.35300=62.27909\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{62\text{ m}}
Check: \tan^{-1}(46.92609/38)=51^\circ ✓   \tan^{-1}(15.35300/38)=22^\circ ✓
On the TI-30XIIS (DEG mode). TAN opens a bracket: 38×TAN51)ENTER gives 46.92609… To keep every digit, do the whole height in one line: 38×TAN51)+38×TAN22)ENTER.
Think Why do the two pieces add to Birchwood’s height? When would you subtract instead?
The horizontal at eye level cuts Birchwood at Ashgrove’s height: below it is a piece equal to Ashgrove (47 m), above it the extra 15 m seen at the 22^\circ elevation. If the observer looked down to the top of the second tower (an angle of depression), that tower is shorter: subtract the piece instead.
2

Two Right Triangles Sharing a Common Side

A segment from a vertex to the opposite side that meets that side at a right angle is an altitude, and it splits the original triangle into two right triangles that share that altitude. SOH CAH TOA applies inside each right triangle separately, and the altitude is the link that connects the two calculations.
opposite, adjacent, hypotenuse: named from the reference angle in that triangle
The shared side can change roles: opposite in one triangle, adjacent in the other.
Example

Reading the shared side off both triangles

Complete the following work for the diagram shown.

XW is the altitude from X to base YZ, meeting it at W.
In right triangle XWY:  \tan(\angle Y)=\dfrac{XW}{\ \ ?\ \ }, so YW=.
In right triangle XWZ:  \tan(\angle Z)=\dfrac{XW}{\ \ ?\ \ }, so WZ=.
Since W lies between Y and Z:  YZ= + .
\tan(\angle Y)=\dfrac{XW}{\boldsymbol{YW}}, so YW=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{XW}{\tan(\angle Y)}};   \tan(\angle Z)=\dfrac{XW}{\boldsymbol{WZ}}, so WZ=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{XW}{\tan(\angle Z)}};   YZ=\boldsymbol{YW}+\boldsymbol{WZ}.
Both right triangles have the altitude XW as one leg: that is the shared side, and it is the same number in both calculations. In each triangle XW is opposite the base angle and the unknown piece of YZ is adjacent, so TOA applies with the unknown in the denominator.
!
Unknown on the bottom → divide. From \tan(\angle Y)=\dfrac{XW}{YW}, multiplying gives the wrong answer XW\tan(\angle Y). Multiply both sides by YW, then divide by \tan(\angle Y): the unknown and the tangent swap places.
Example 2

An altitude shared by two right triangles

In the diagram, QS=24 mm, \angle PQS=48^\circ, and \angle RPS=57^\circ, where PS is perpendicular to RQ. Determine the length of QR to the nearest millimetre.

Length of QR
Shared side: the altitude PS. Solve \triangle PSQ first: it has a known side (QS=24) and a known angle (48^\circ); \triangle PSR has only the 57^\circ angle until PS arrives.
\triangle PSQ: \tan 48^\circ=\dfrac{PS}{24} \Rightarrow PS=24\tan 48^\circ=26.6547004\ldots mm
\triangle PSR (from \angle RPS, RS is opposite and PS adjacent): \tan 57^\circ=\dfrac{RS}{PS} \Rightarrow RS=26.6547004\tan 57^\circ =41.0446392\ldots mm
S lies between R and Q: QR=QS+RS=24+41.0446392 =65.0446392\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{65\text{ mm}}
Check: \tan^{-1}(26.65470/24)=48^\circ ✓   \tan^{-1}(41.04464/26.65470)=57^\circ ✓
Chain it on the calculator. 24×TAN48)ENTER gives PS=26.6547\ldots. Keep going without retyping: 24×TAN48)×TAN57)ENTER gives RS with every digit.
Think Could you start with \triangle PSR instead?
No. \triangle PSR has only one known measure, the 57^\circ angle. A right triangle needs a side and an angle (or two sides) to be solved. Its side PS has to come from \triangle PSQ first.

The chain: the shared side travels from one triangle to the other

Watch the shared side glow as it is found in the first triangle, then the triangles pull apart and it is carried, unrounded, into the second. Notice that it can change roles on the way: in Example 3 it is opposite in one triangle and adjacent in the other.

Example 3

Finding an angle through the shared side

In the diagram, EC is perpendicular to BA, EB=5.8 m, EA=4.5 m, and \angle EAC=35^\circ. Determine the measure of \angle BEC to the nearest degree.

Measure of \angle BEC
Shared side: the altitude EC. Solve \triangle EAC first: it has the hypotenuse EA=4.5 and the 35^\circ angle; \triangle EBC has only EB=5.8 until EC arrives.
\sin 35^\circ=\dfrac{EC}{4.5} \Rightarrow EC=4.5\sin 35^\circ=2.58109396\ldots m
\triangle EBC, angle at E: EC is adjacent, EB=5.8 is the hypotenuse \Rightarrow CAH.
\cos(\angle BEC)=\dfrac{2.58109396}{5.8}=0.44501620\ldots \Rightarrow \angle BEC=\cos^{-1}(0.44501620) =63.5756\ldots^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{64^\circ}
Rounding matters here: rounding EC to 2.6 first gives \cos^{-1}(2.6/5.8)=63.4^\circ\to63^\circ, the wrong answer.
Check: BC=\sqrt{5.8^2-2.58109^2}=5.19403 and 2.58109^2+5.19403^2 =33.64=5.8^2 ✓
Inverse cosine with the shared side inside. 4.5×SIN35)ENTER gives EC. Then 2ndCOS is \cos^{-1}(: 2ndCOS4.5×SIN35)÷5.8)ENTER gives 63.5756…°.

Which triangle first? Which ratio? How to finish?

Every question in this lesson follows the same plan. The only real decision is the first one: does one of the triangles have enough to be solved on its own?

3

Two Sightings, One Unknown Distance

Sometimes neither right triangle has a known side: an observer measures two angles of elevation from two points on a line, and only the distance between the points is known. Write each horizontal distance in terms of the unknown height h, then subtract to eliminate the unknown distance.

Example 4

Two angles of elevation, 25 m apart

From point A on level ground, the angle of elevation of the top T of a building is 32^\circ. Walking 25 m straight toward the building to point B, the angle of elevation becomes 47^\circ. Determine the height h of the building to the nearest metre.

(a) Write x=BC in terms of h, using \triangle TBC.
From 47^\circ: h is opposite, x is adjacent: \tan 47^\circ=\dfrac{h}{x}\Rightarrow x=\dfrac{h}{\tan 47^\circ}
(b) Write x+25=AC in terms of h, using \triangle TAC.
\tan 32^\circ=\dfrac{h}{x+25}\Rightarrow x+25=\dfrac{h}{\tan 32^\circ}
(c) Subtract (a) from (b) to eliminate x, then solve for h.
25=\dfrac{h}{\tan 32^\circ}-\dfrac{h}{\tan 47^\circ}=h\left(\dfrac{1}{\tan 32^\circ}-\dfrac{1}{\tan 47^\circ}\right)=h(1.6003345-0.9325151)=h(0.6678194)
h=\dfrac{25}{0.6678194}=37.4353\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{37\text{ m}}
Check: x=37.4353/\tan 47^\circ=34.909, and \tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{37.4353}{59.909}\right)=32^\circ ✓
Keep the two quotients unrounded: their difference is small, so rounding them first changes h.
One line, no rounding. The bracket holds the difference: 25÷(1÷TAN32)−1÷TAN47))ENTER gives 37.4353…
Think A classmate writes \tan 32^\circ=\dfrac{h}{25}. What went wrong?
The 25 m is not a side of \triangle TAC: it is only the piece AB between the two observers. The side adjacent to 32^\circ is the whole AC=x+25.

Why never round the shared side?

Example 3 and Example 4, each worked twice: once with every digit carried, once rounded partway. Watch where the final answers land.

4

Extending Into Three Dimensions

Extension
ExtensionSection 4 and Examples 5 and 6 go beyond the Math 10C outcome; they use the same chain in 3-D.

A solid can also hide two right triangles that share a side. The usual plan: find a right triangle lying in the base, solve it, then hand its side to a right triangle standing vertically on it.

Example 5

The diagonal of a box

Extension

A box has base ABCD with AB=4 cm and BC=3 cm, and height CG=12 cm (G is directly above C).

(a) Determine the length of the base diagonal AC.
\triangle ABC lies in the base with the right angle at B: AC=\sqrt{4^2+3^2}=\sqrt{25}=\boldsymbol{5\text{ cm}}
(b) Determine the length of the box’s diagonal AG, and the angle \angle GAC it makes with the base, to the nearest degree.
GC is vertical, so \triangle ACG is right-angled at C, with legs AC=5 and CG=12.
AG=\sqrt{5^2+12^2}=\sqrt{169}=\boldsymbol{13\text{ cm}}
\tan(\angle GAC)=\dfrac{12}{5} \Rightarrow\angle GAC=\tan^{-1}(2.4) =67.38^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{67^\circ}
Roots and inverse tangent. 2ndx² is \sqrt{\ } and opens a bracket: 2ndx²4x²+3x²)ENTER gives 5. 2ndTAN12÷5)ENTER gives 67.38…°.
Example 6

A wedge sliced from a rectangular prism

Extension

The wedge below hides its right triangle in a side face instead: a trapezoid formed by two parallel vertical edges and the horizontal edge joining their bases. A horizontal line drawn across that face, from the shorter vertical edge to the taller one, creates a right triangle whose legs are the horizontal distance between the edges and the difference in their heights.

The solid below was formed by slicing a wedge off a rectangular prism, leaving a slanted top face EFGH. In the diagram, HD=GC=9 cm, EA=FB=2 cm, AD=6 cm, and AB=11 cm.

(a) Name four rectangles that appear as faces of this solid.
Base ABCD, front ABFE, back DCGH, and the slanted top EFGH.
EFGH is a rectangle: EF and HG both run in the AB direction, while EH and FG lie in the planes ADHE and BCGF, which are perpendicular to AB, so adjacent edges meet at 90^\circ.
The side faces ADHE and BCGF are trapezoids, not rectangles, since EA=2\ne HD=9.
(b) Determine the measure of \angle HEA to the nearest degree.
Work in the side face ADHE: a right trapezoid with EA=2 and HD=9 vertical and AD=6 horizontal. Draw the horizontal from E across to DH, meeting it at E' (directly above D, level with E). EE' is the shared side of the split.
EE'=AD=6 cm, E'H=HD-EA=9-2=7 cm
\tan(\angle HEE')=\dfrac{7}{6} \Rightarrow\angle HEE'=\tan^{-1}(7/6) =49.39871\ldots^\circ
EA is vertical and EE' horizontal, so \angle AEE'=90^\circ, and H lies past the horizontal from A:
\angle HEA=90^\circ+49.39871^\circ =139.39871\ldots^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{139^\circ} (obtuse: E is the notch corner of the wedge)
(c) Determine the length of EH to the nearest tenth of a centimetre.
EH is the hypotenuse of the same right triangle EE'H, legs 6 cm and 7 cm:
EH=\sqrt{EE'^2+E'H^2}=\sqrt{6^2+7^2} =\sqrt{85}=9.2195444\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{9.2\text{ cm}}
Check: \sin 49.39871^\circ=7/9.21954 ✓ 49.39871^\circ+40.60129^\circ+90^\circ=180^\circ ✓
The side face on the calculator. 2ndTAN7÷6)ENTER gives 49.39871…°; add the right angle in the same line with 90+ in front. 2ndx²6x²+7x²)ENTER gives EH.

✓

Key ideas

The shared side links two right triangles: an altitude, a vertical building, a common horizontal distance. It can be opposite in one triangle and adjacent in the other.
Solve first the triangle with a known side and a known angle (or two sides). Then carry the shared side across and pick SOH, CAH or TOA there.
Never round the shared side. Keep full calculator accuracy (or one long calculator line) and round only the final answer: 63.58^\circ\to64^\circ, not 63^\circ.
No known side? Write both distances in terms of h and subtract (two sightings). Extension In 3-D: base triangle first, then the vertical one.
