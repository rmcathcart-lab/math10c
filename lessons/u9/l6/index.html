Multi-Step Trigonometry: Eye Level, Elevation and Depression

Real measuring jobs rarely fit in one step. A height is measured from someone’s eyes, not the ground; a second question reuses the answer to the first; a right angle hides in a circle. Today: layered right-triangle problems, and measuring a real height with a home-made clinometer.

I can…
  • solve a problem that needs two calculations in one right triangle, reusing the unrounded answer from the first part
  • spot a hidden right angle: an angle inscribed in a semicircle is 90^\circ
  • work with an angle of elevation measured from eye level, and add the eye height back at the end
  • read a clinometer (angle of elevation =|90^\circ-\text{string reading}|) and use it to measure a height indirectly
  • Extension build right triangles from tangent lines to a circle and from bearings measured clockwise from north
M4 Develop and apply the primary trigonometric ratios (sine, cosine, tangent) to solve problems that involve right triangles.
Warm-up

Lighthouse Lookout

The keeper’s eyes are 40 m above the water. She looks down at three boats.

1
Each angle is measured down from which line?
The horizontal through the keeper’s eyes (the dashed line), never the lighthouse wall.
2
Boat A is 45^\circ down. How far is it from the lighthouse?
40 m. At 45^\circ the triangle is isosceles: distance = height.
3
As a boat sails away, does its angle get bigger or smaller?
Smaller. Farther boats need a shallower look down, so the biggest angle belongs to the closest boat.
!
The big one. Boat C’s angle (15^\circ) is one third of boat A’s (45^\circ). How far is C from the lighthouse? Guess first.
A about 11 m
B 120 m
C about 149 m
C: about 149 m. The angle of depression equals the angle of elevation from the boat up to the keeper (alternate angles between the two horizontals). From the boat, 40 m is opposite 15^\circ and the distance d is adjacent: \tan15^\circ=\dfrac{40}{d}\Rightarrow d=\dfrac{40}{\tan15^\circ}\approx149.3 m.
A is 40\tan15^\circ\approx10.7: multiplying instead of dividing (a boat 11 m out would be almost straight down). B is “a third of the angle, so three times as far”: distance is not proportional to the angle. (Boat B: 40\div\tan25^\circ\approx85.8 m.)

Look down at your feet, then at the floor by the door. Which angle of depression is bigger?

1

Multi-Step Problems with a Single Triangle

Some situations are solved inside a single right triangle but still need two separate calculations: one for part (a), and a second for part (b) that uses a different vertex or a different pair of sides.

Read every part first. A later part frequently reuses a length found in an earlier one. Keep that length unrounded (all the calculator digits) and round only the final answer.
Example 1

Two calculations in one triangle

Land surveyor Priya uses a transit (a tripod-mounted sighting instrument) to determine the height of a utility pole. The transit is set up 45 m from the base of the pole, and the angle from the transit’s eyepiece up to the top of the pole is measured to be 15^\circ.

(a) If the transit’s eyepiece is 1.6 m above the ground, calculate the height of the pole to the nearest tenth of a metre.
From the 15^\circ at the eyepiece: the rise above eye level is opposite, 45 is adjacent \Rightarrow TOA.
rise =45\tan15^\circ=45(0.267949)=12.0577 m
pole =1.6+12.0577=13.6577\approx\boldsymbol{13.7} m
(b) Determine, to the nearest degree, the angle of elevation of the top of the pole from the base of the transit (at ground level, not the eyepiece).
From the base, the whole pole is opposite and 45 is adjacent. Use the unrounded height.
\tan\theta=\dfrac{13.6577}{45}=0.303504
\theta=\tan^{-1}(0.303504)=16.88^\circ\approx\boldsymbol{17^\circ}
On the TI-30XIIS. (a) 45×TAN15)+1.6= gives 13.6577… (b) 2ndTAN13.6577÷45)= gives 16.88…; 2ndTAN is \tan^{-1}, and it opens a bracket too.

Hidden right angles

SOH CAH TOA needs a right angle, and sometimes the right angle is not marked. One you met in Math 9:

An angle inscribed in a semicircle (its vertex on the circle and its two arms through the ends of a diameter) is always 90^\circ. The diameter is the hypotenuse.
2

Angles Measured from Eye Level

An angle of elevation or depression is often measured from an observer’s eye level, not from the ground. Before applying SOH CAH TOA, check whether the diagram’s vertical measurements need to be adjusted by an eye height.

The trigonometry returns only the rise above eye level, so the eye height must be added back at the end:
height = eye height + rise above eye level
!
Which height is the angle measured from? Draw the horizontal through the eyes first. Everything below that line (the eye height) is not in the triangle, so it is not in the trig. Forget it and the answer comes out exactly one eye height too short.
Example 2

Eye level at both ends of the line of sight

Malik stands on the ground 18 ft from the base of an apartment building and looks up at his friend, who is leaning out of a window. The angle of elevation from Malik’s eye level to his friend’s eye level is 55^\circ. Malik’s eyes are 5.5 ft above the ground, and his friend’s eyes are 4.3 ft below the very top of the building. Determine the height of the building to the nearest tenth of a foot.

Height of the building
From the 55^\circ at Malik’s eye level: the rise to his friend’s eye level is opposite and 18 is adjacent \Rightarrow TOA.
rise =18\tan55^\circ=18(1.428148)=25.7067 ft
friend’s eye level =5.5+25.7067=31.2067 ft
height =31.2067+4.3=35.5067\approx\boldsymbol{35.5} ft
All in one line. 18×TAN55)+5.5+4.3= gives 35.5066… so nothing is rounded until the end.

Example 2 check: stack the heights

Three pieces make the building: below Malik’s eye level, the rise the trig gives, and the bit above his friend’s eyes.

3

Indirect Measurement With a Clinometer

A clinometer measures an angle of elevation. A simple one is a protractor with a drinking straw taped along its straight edge and a string, weighted with a washer, hanging from the centre hole. Sight the top of an object through the straw: the string hangs straight down, so the angle of elevation is the difference between the string’s reading and 90^\circ:

angle of elevation =\left|\,90^\circ-\text{string reading}\,\right|

Activity: How tall is it?

Work in groups of three (sighter, reader, recorder).

  1. Build the clinometer: tape the straw along the protractor’s straight edge; tie the string through the centre hole and hang a washer on it.
  2. Measure the sighter’s eye height with a metre stick.
  3. Measure the horizontal distance from the sighter’s feet to the base of a tall object (flagpole, wall, goalpost) with a measuring tape or metre stick.
  4. Sight the top of the object through the straw. The reader records where the string crosses the protractor; convert it to the angle of elevation.
  5. Calculate: height = eye height + distance \times\tan(\text{angle of elevation}).
  6. Measure the height directly where you can (a wall or doorframe with the metre stick, or count bricks \times the height of one brick) and compare.

Example 3

Reading a clinometer

Ana’s eyes are 1.55 m above the ground. Standing 18.0 m from the base of the school flagpole, she sights the top through her clinometer, and the string crosses the protractor at 58^\circ.

(a) What is the angle of elevation?
The string hangs vertically, so the reading is measured from the vertical:
90^\circ-58^\circ=\boldsymbol{32^\circ}
(b) Determine the height of the flagpole to the nearest tenth of a metre.
From eye level (TOA):
rise =18.0\tan32^\circ
=18.0(0.624869)=11.2476 m
height =1.55+11.2476
=12.7976\approx\boldsymbol{12.8} m
(c) The caretaker says the pole is 12.5 m tall. Give two reasons the measurement could be off.
A 1^\circ reading error matters: 32^\circ\pm1^\circ gives heights from 12.4 m to 13.2 m. Also: the string swinging, the ground not level, the distance not measured to the point directly below the top, or the eye height changing (tilting the head).
Part (b), and the ±1° check for (c). 18×TAN32)+1.55= gives 12.7976…; change 32 to 31 or 33 for the range.

Example 3(c): how much does one degree matter?

The string is thin, the washer swings, and the scale has tiny marks. Misread by just 1^\circ and watch where the line of sight hits the pole.

4

Tangent Lines to a Circle

Extension
A line tangent to a circle meets the radius drawn to the point of tangency at a right angle. When two tangent segments are drawn to a circle from the same outside point, the two segments are equal in length, and the segment joining that outside point to the circle’s centre bisects the angle between them.

Both facts let a right triangle be built to work with, even though no right angle is drawn in the original picture.

Example 4

A circle tangent to both arms of an angle

Extension

A circular sprinkler head has centre C and radius 15 mm, and is tangent to both arms of angle POQ. If angle POQ=64^\circ, determine the length of OC to the nearest millimetre.

Length of OC
OP and OQ are tangents from the same outside point O, so OC bisects angle POQ: 64^\circ\div2=32^\circ.
The radius to the point of tangency on OQ is perpendicular to OQ: 15 is opposite 32^\circ and OC is the hypotenuse \Rightarrow SOH.
\sin32^\circ=\dfrac{15}{OC}\Rightarrow OC=\dfrac{15}{\sin32^\circ}=\dfrac{15}{0.529919}=28.31\approx\boldsymbol{28} mm
Unknown on the bottom: divide. 15÷SIN32)= gives 28.306…

Bearings

Extension
A bearing describes a direction as an angle measured clockwise from north, always written using three digits (for example 065^\circ, not 65^\circ).

The four diagrams below show four different bearings, each measured at a single point; the ray shows the direction.

To use a bearing in a right triangle, work with the acute angle between the direction and the north–south line (its reference angle):

000^\circ–090^\circ090^\circ–180^\circ180^\circ–270^\circ270^\circ–360^\circ
bearing,
from north (toward E)		180^\circ-bearing,
from south (toward E)		bearing\,-180^\circ,
from south (toward W)		360^\circ-bearing,
from north (toward W)
For a leg of length d, the leg is the hypotenuse: the east–west part is opposite the reference angle (d\sin) and the north–south part is adjacent (d\cos). The picture tells you which direction (N or S, E or W) each part goes. Draw a north–south line at every point where the direction of travel changes.
Example 5

A ferry on a bearing

Extension

A ferry sails 8 km from a dock, D, on a bearing of 130^\circ to a buoy, M, as shown.

(a) At M the ferry changes direction and sails due north until it reaches a point N that is due east of D. How far east of D is the ferry when it reaches N? Answer to the nearest 0.1 km.
130^\circ points south of east: reference angle 180^\circ-130^\circ=50^\circ from south. Hypotenuse DM=8:
east part (opposite) =8\sin50^\circ=8(0.766044)=6.1284 km
south part (adjacent) =8\cos50^\circ=8(0.642788)=5.1423 km
Sailing due north does not change the east position: N is \boldsymbol{6.1} km east of D.
(b) The ferry continues sailing north for a further 10 km to a point W. Determine the three-figure bearing of W from D.
From M the ferry sails 5.1423 km north to N, then 10 km further: W is 10 km north and 6.1284 km east of D, between 000^\circ and 090^\circ.
\tan\theta=\dfrac{6.1284}{10}=0.61284\Rightarrow\theta=31.50^\circ
bearing \approx\boldsymbol{032^\circ} (three digits)
(c) If the ferry maintains an average speed of 12 km/h for the entire trip from D to W, determine, to the nearest tenth of an hour, the total time for the journey.
Total distance =DM+MN+NW
=8+5.1423+10=23.1423 km
t=\dfrac{23.1423}{12}=1.9285\approx\boldsymbol{1.9} h
!
Keep the digits. In (b) the unrounded 6.1284 gives 31.50^\circ, which rounds to 032^\circ. Use the rounded 6.1 instead and you get \tan^{-1}(0.61)=31.38^\circ, which rounds to 031^\circ: a wrong answer from an early rounding.

6

Combining Multiple Legs

Extension

When a journey has two or more legs on different bearings, draw a north–south line at every turning point. Split each leg of length d into an east–west part (d\sin of its reference angle) and a north–south part (d\cos of its reference angle), and use the picture to decide whether to add or subtract. The Pythagorean Theorem and an inverse tangent then give the direct distance and the bearing between the start and end points.

Example 6

Two legs on different bearings

Extension

A cyclist leaves a trailhead, T, and rides 14 km on a bearing of 038^\circ to a rest stop, R. At R she changes direction and rides 9 km on a bearing of 097^\circ to a lookout, L. TN and RM represent north lines at T and R.

(a) On the diagram, mark the sizes of angle NTR and angle MRL.
A bearing is measured clockwise from the north line at that point: angle NTR=\boldsymbol{38^\circ} and angle MRL=\boldsymbol{97^\circ}.
(b) Determine, to the nearest 0.1 km, how far L is east of T.
Leg TR: 38^\circ from north: east =14\sin38^\circ=8.6193
Leg RL: 097^\circ is just south of east, reference 180^\circ-97^\circ=83^\circ from south: east =9\sin83^\circ=8.9329
Both go east: 8.6193+8.9329=17.5522\approx\boldsymbol{17.6} km
(c) Determine, to the nearest 0.1 km, how far L is north of T.
Leg TR goes north: 14\cos38^\circ=11.0322
Leg RL goes south: 9\cos83^\circ=1.0968
North of T: 11.0322-1.0968=9.9354\approx\boldsymbol{9.9} km
(d) Determine, to the nearest 0.1 km, the direct distance from T to L.
Pythagorean Theorem on the unrounded parts:
TL=\sqrt{17.5522^2+9.9354^2}=\sqrt{308.078+98.711}
=\sqrt{406.789}=20.169\approx\boldsymbol{20.2} km
(e) Determine the three-figure bearing of L from T.
L is east and north of T, so the bearing is between 000^\circ and 090^\circ. The angle from north is opposite the east part, adjacent to the north part:
\tan\theta=\dfrac{17.5522}{9.9354}=1.76646\Rightarrow\theta=60.49^\circ
bearing \approx\boldsymbol{060^\circ}

Key ideas

Two calculations, one triangle. Read every part first. Part (b) often reuses part (a)’s length: keep it unrounded and round only the final answer.
Eye level. The angle is measured from the horizontal through the eyes. Trig gives the rise above eye level; add the eye height back at the end.
Clinometer. The string hangs straight down: angle of elevation =|90^\circ-\text{reading}|. A 1^\circ misreading can move the answer by tenths of a metre.
Ext Hidden right angles: in a semicircle; between a tangent and its radius. Bearings: clockwise from north, three digits; split each leg into d\sin (E–W) and d\cos (N–S).
