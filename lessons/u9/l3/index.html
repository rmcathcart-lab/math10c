Math 10C · Unit 9 Trigonometry · Lesson 3

Finding a Missing Side Length in a Right Triangle

Name the sides relative to the given angle, choose the ratio that holds the known side and the unknown, then multiply or divide. And when two sides are already known, no trigonometry is needed at all.

I can…
  • decide which tool a question calls for: the Pythagorean theorem (two sides known) or a trig ratio (an acute angle and one side known)
  • name the opposite, adjacent and hypotenuse from the given angle and choose SOH, CAH or TOA
  • solve when the unknown is on top of the ratio (multiply) and when it is on the bottom (cross-multiply, then divide)
  • sketch a triangle from a description such as \angle JLK=90^\circ, find two unknown sides from the given information, round only at the end, and sense-check every answer
M4 Develop and apply the primary trigonometric ratios (sine, cosine, tangent) to solve problems that involve right triangles.
Warm-up

Kite Height

You let out all 50 m of string. It makes a 40^\circ angle with the level ground.

!
The big one. At 40^\circ, about how high is the kite above your hand? Guess first.
A about 22 m
B about 29 m
C about 32 m
D about 38 m
C: about 32 m. The string is the hypotenuse and the height is opposite the 40^\circ angle, so \sin40^\circ=\dfrac{h}{50} and h=50\sin40^\circ\approx32.1 m.
A is \tfrac{40}{90}\times50: “height grows in step with the angle.” B is a straight line between the 30^\circ (25 m) and 90^\circ (50 m) rows, but sine is not linear: the height climbs fast at low angles and levels off near 90^\circ (at 60^\circ it is already 43 m). D is 50\cos40^\circ\approx38.3: the adjacent side, the ground distance to the spot under the kite. Wrong side.
1
If the string went straight up (90^\circ), how high is the kite?
50 m: all of the string is height.
2
If the string lay flat (0^\circ), how high is it?
0 m: the kite is at hand height.
3
At 30^\circ, the kite’s height is exactly half the string’s length. How high is it?
25 m. This is \sin30^\circ=\tfrac12 stated in words.
+
Early finishers. At the same 40^\circ, how much string do you need for the kite to be 40 m up?
\sin40^\circ=\dfrac{40}{x}\ \Rightarrow\ x=\dfrac{40}{\sin40^\circ}\approx62.2 m. The unknown is now on the bottom: a preview of Section 3.

Stand up and point at a kite at 30°, then at 40°. How far did your finger rise? (About 7 m of kite height for only 10°.)

The kite, solved

The string is the hypotenuse, the height is the side opposite the angle. That is the whole recipe of this lesson: name the sides, choose the ratio, then multiply or divide.

1

Review: The Pythagorean Theorem

In a right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c,

c^2=a^2+b^2,\qquad a^2=c^2-b^2,\qquad b^2=c^2-a^2
The theorem applies whenever two side lengths are known. When one side length and one acute angle are known instead, a trigonometric ratio is needed. Recognizing which tool the given information calls for is the first decision in every question.
Example 1

Two sides known, so no trigonometry is required

Calculate the length of the third side of each triangle, to the nearest tenth of a unit if necessary.

(a)
Two legs are known and no angle is given: Pythagorean theorem. r is the hypotenuse.
r^2=6.3^2+8.4^2=39.69+70.56=110.25
r=\sqrt{110.25}=\boldsymbol{10.5}\text{ cm} (exact: a 3–4–5 triangle scaled by 2.1)
(b)
Again both legs are known, and s is the hypotenuse.
s^2=12.0^2+5.0^2=144.00+25.00=169.00
s=\sqrt{169.00}=\boldsymbol{13.0}\text{ mm} (a 5–12–13 triangle)
The whole calculation in one line. 2ndx² is \sqrt{\phantom{x}}, and it opens a bracket: √6.3x²+8.4x²)ENTER gives 10.5. Close the bracket before ENTER.
2

The Unknown in the Numerator

Once an acute angle is chosen, every side of the triangle has a name relative to it: the opposite side, the adjacent side, and the hypotenuse. SOH CAH TOA then fixes the ratio to use:

SOH\sin\theta=\dfrac{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{\text{opp}}}{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{\text{hyp}}}
CAH\cos\theta=\dfrac{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{\text{adj}}}{\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{\text{hyp}}}
TOA\tan\theta=\dfrac{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{\text{opp}}}{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{\text{adj}}}
If the unknown side is on top of the chosen ratio, the equation is solved by a single multiplication: \dfrac{x}{k}=\sin\theta gives x=k\sin\theta.

Unknown on top → multiply

The two sides in the chosen ratio light up on the triangle. Put the equation on a balance: whatever divides the unknown, multiply both pans by it.

Example 2

Setting up the ratio with the unknown on top

The measure of an acute angle and the length of the hypotenuse are given. Complete the steps for finding x, the side opposite the given angle.

The given side, 16.4 cm, is the hypotenuse, and the side to be found, x, is opposite the given angle of 58^\circ.
We use the sine ratio.
\dfrac{x}{16.4}= \sin 58^\circ    x=16.4 \sin 58^\circ
To the nearest tenth, x= 13.9 cm.
16.4 cm is the hypotenuse and x is opposite 58^\circ, so SOH gives the sine ratio.
\dfrac{x}{16.4}=\sin58^\circ. The unknown is in the numerator, so multiply: x=16.4\sin58^\circ.
x=16.4(0.8480481)=13.90798\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{13.9}\text{ cm}
Sense check: x must be shorter than the hypotenuse, 16.4 cm ✓
DEG mode first. The SIN key opens a bracket, so close it: 16.4×SIN58)ENTER gives 13.90798… Check: \sin90^\circ should give exactly 1.
Example 3

A ladder against a wall

A ladder 6.8 m long leans against a wall, making an angle of 68^\circ with the ground.

(a) How high up the wall, to the nearest tenth of a metre, does the ladder reach?
h is opposite 68^\circ and the hypotenuse (6.8 m) is given: sine, unknown on top.
\sin68^\circ=\dfrac{h}{6.8}, so h=6.8\sin68^\circ=6.30485\ldots
h\approx\boldsymbol{6.3}\text{ m}
(b) Use trigonometry to determine, to the nearest tenth of a metre, the distance d between the foot of the ladder and the base of the wall.
d is adjacent to 68^\circ and the hypotenuse is given: cosine, unknown on top.
\cos68^\circ=\dfrac{d}{6.8}, so d=6.8\cos68^\circ=2.54732\ldots
d\approx\boldsymbol{2.5}\text{ m}
(c) Use your answer to (a) and the Pythagorean theorem to determine d again, to the nearest tenth of a metre.
Using the rounded h=6.3 m:
d=\sqrt{6.8^2-6.3^2}
=\sqrt{46.24-39.69}=\sqrt{6.55}
=2.55929\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{2.6}\text{ m}
(d) Explain why the answers to (b) and (c) are not quite the same.
(b) is built on the two given quantities, 6.8 m and 68^\circ. (c) is built on h after it was already rounded to 6.3 m, and the subtraction inside the square root magnifies that error: 2.5593 instead of 2.5473, which rounds to 2.6 rather than 2.5. Carrying h=6.30485\ldots unrounded gives 2.5473, matching (b): round only at the very end.
Three calculations. (a) 6.8×SIN68)ENTER, (b) the same with COS, (c) √6.8x²−6.3x²)ENTER.

The ladder, and the rounding trap

Slide the ladder to see the height and the distance trade off, then check Example 3, and see exactly why (b) and (c) disagree.

3

The Unknown in the Denominator

If the unknown side is the hypotenuse (or, more generally, sits underneath the known side in the ratio, for example the adjacent leg in \tan\theta=\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}} when the opposite leg is the one given), it lands in the denominator, and the equation is solved by cross-multiplying and then dividing:

\sin\theta=\dfrac{k}{x}\ \Rightarrow\ x\sin\theta=k\ \Rightarrow\ x=\dfrac{k}{\sin\theta},\qquad\text{and likewise}\qquad\tan\theta=\dfrac{k}{x}\ \Rightarrow\ x=\dfrac{k}{\tan\theta}
Where the unknown sits decides the whole calculation: on top it is a multiplication, underneath it is a division. Carry full calculator precision through and round only the final answer.

A quick way to remember: cover the unknown

Write each ratio as a little triangle: the side on top, the ratio and the other side on the bottom. Cover the side you want. If what is left sits side by side, multiply; if it is one over the other, divide.

Example 4

Cross-multiplying to free the unknown

The acute angle and the leg opposite it are known, but the hypotenuse c is unknown, so c appears in the denominator of the ratio. Complete the steps.

\sin 37^\circ=\dfrac{9.4}{c}
cross multiply:  c \sin 37^\circ=9.4
Divide both sides by \sin 37^\circ to get
c=9.4\div \sin 37^\circ= 15.6 cm (to the nearest tenth).
9.4 cm is opposite 37^\circ and c is the hypotenuse, so c sits underneath: \sin37^\circ=\dfrac{9.4}{c}.
Cross multiply: c\sin37^\circ=9.4. Divide by \sin37^\circ:
c=\dfrac{9.4}{\sin37^\circ}=\dfrac{9.4}{0.6018150}=15.61941\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{15.6}\text{ cm}
This is a division: 9.4\sin37^\circ=5.66 would be shorter than a leg, impossible for a hypotenuse. Check: adjacent leg =\sqrt{15.6194^2-9.4^2}=12.474, and 9.4\div12.474=0.7536=\tan37^\circ ✓
Divide, in one line. 9.4÷SIN37)ENTER gives 15.61941… Don’t round \sin37^\circ first.
Example 5

Reading the labels from a vertex-letter description

Determine, to one decimal place, the length of the hypotenuse JK of triangle JKL, in which angle JLK=90^\circ, angle LJK=34^\circ, and KL=11.2 mm.

\angle L=90^\circ, so JK is the hypotenuse. Relative to \angle J=34^\circ, KL=11.2 mm is opposite, and the unknown hypotenuse sits in the denominator.
\sin34^\circ=\dfrac{KL}{JK}=\dfrac{11.2}{JK}
Cross multiply and divide: JK=\dfrac{11.2}{\sin34^\circ}=\dfrac{11.2}{0.5591929}=20.02886\ldots
JK\approx\boldsymbol{20.0}\text{ mm}
Check: JL=\dfrac{11.2}{\tan34^\circ}=16.605, and 11.2^2+16.605^2=401.2=20.029^2 ✓
Unknown on the bottom: divide. 11.2÷SIN34)ENTER gives 20.02886… The check uses TAN: 11.2÷TAN34)ENTER.

From words to a labelled triangle

In \angle JLK the middle letter is the vertex where the angle is. A side is named by its two endpoints: KL runs from K to L, across from the third vertex J.

Math Historyc. 270 BCE · Samos

How far away is the Sun?

At half moon, exactly half the Moon is lit, so the Sun, Moon and Earth make a right angle at the Moon. Aristarchus measured the angle at Earth between the Moon and the Sun: 87°.

The Moon's distance is the adjacent side and the Sun's is the hypotenuse, so Sun ÷ Moon = 1 ÷ cos 87° ≈ 19. He said the Sun is 18 to 20 times as far away as the Moon, so it must be huge.

The angle is really about 89.85°. How many times as far away as the Moon is the Sun?
1 ÷ cos 89.85° ≈ 382 (the real value is about 390). His method was right; a 3° error in the angle made the answer 20 times too small.
4

Solving for More Than One Side

A single triangle may ask for two unknown sides at once, typically one numerator case and one denominator case. Set each one up from the given side and the given angle; never build the second calculation on the rounded answer to the first.
Example 6

An unknown leg in the denominator of tangent

In right triangle ABC, \angle C=90^\circ, \angle A=28^\circ, and the leg opposite \angle A is BC=9.5 cm. Determine the length of the adjacent leg AC, to the nearest tenth of a centimetre.

From \angle A=28^\circ: BC=9.5 is opposite and AC is adjacent: TOA. The unknown is the adjacent leg, which sits underneath:
\tan28^\circ=\dfrac{9.5}{AC}. Cross multiply: AC\tan28^\circ=9.5, then divide:
AC=\dfrac{9.5}{\tan28^\circ}=\dfrac{9.5}{0.5317094}=17.86690\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{17.9}\text{ cm}
Sense check: 28^\circ<45^\circ, so the adjacent leg must be longer than the opposite leg. Multiplying instead (9.5\tan28^\circ=5.05) would make it shorter: impossible.
Tangent, divided. 9.5÷TAN28)ENTER gives 17.86690…
Example 7

One triangle, two unknown sides

In triangle DEF, angle DEF=90^\circ, angle DFE=64^\circ, and EF=71 m. Use trigonometric ratios to determine the lengths of DE and DF, each to the nearest metre.

(a) Determine the length of DE.
Relative to \angle F=64^\circ: EF=71 m is adjacent and DE is opposite: TOA, unknown on top.
\tan64^\circ=\dfrac{DE}{71}, so DE=71\tan64^\circ=71(2.0503038)=145.57157\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{146}\text{ m}
(b) Determine the length of DF.
DF is the hypotenuse, so it lands in the denominator: \cos64^\circ=\dfrac{EF}{DF}=\dfrac{71}{DF}.
DF\cos64^\circ=71, so DF=\dfrac{71}{\cos64^\circ}=\dfrac{71}{0.4383711}=161.96321\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{162}\text{ m}
Both parts start from the given 71 m, never from the rounded DE. Check: 71^2+145.5716^2=26\,232.1=161.963^2 ✓ and 90^\circ+64^\circ+26^\circ=180^\circ ✓
One multiplication, one division. (a) 71×TAN64)ENTER   (b) 71÷COS64)ENTER

Two unknowns: two fresh starts

Each unknown gets its own ratio, set up from the given side and angle. Then check the pair with the Pythagorean theorem and the angle sum.

✓

Key ideas

Which tool? Two sides known: Pythagoras, c^2=a^2+b^2 (subtract first for a leg). An acute angle and one side: a trig ratio.
Which ratio? Name opp, adj, hyp from the given angle. Pick the one of SOH, CAH, TOA that holds the known side and the unknown.
Multiply or divide? Unknown on top: x=k\sin\theta. Unknown on the bottom: cross-multiply, then x=\dfrac{k}{\sin\theta}.
Precision and sense. Start each unknown from the given values, round only at the end, and check: the hypotenuse is the longest side.
