Right-Triangle Trigonometry: Sine, Cosine, and Tangent

Fix one acute angle of a right triangle and every ratio of its sides is fixed, however big or small the triangle is. Those ratios get names: sine, cosine and tangent. To use them, first name the sides opposite, adjacent and hypotenuse from the angle you are looking at.

I can…
  • explain why right triangles with the same acute angle are similar, so a ratio of their sides depends only on the angle, not on the size
  • label the hyp, opp and adj sides relative to a reference angle, and say which labels change when the other acute angle is used
  • state the three primary trigonometric ratios with SOH CAH TOA: \sin x^\circ=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}, \cos x^\circ=\dfrac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}, \tan x^\circ=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}
  • write the sine, cosine and tangent of either acute angle from the side lengths, as fractions in simplest form and as decimals, finding a missing hypotenuse first with the Pythagorean theorem
M4 Develop and apply the primary trigonometric ratios (sine, cosine, tangent) to solve problems that involve right triangles.
Warm-up

Shadow Height

Same sunny moment. A student and the school flagpole both cast shadows.

1
A kid 0.8 m tall stands beside the student. How long is the kid’s shadow?
1 m. Half the height, so half the shadow: 2\div2=1.
2
For the student: height \div shadow = ?
1.6\div2=\boldsymbol{0.8}. Right now, everything is 0.8 times as tall as its shadow.
3
Right now, would a tree give the same height \div shadow? Why?
Yes. The sun’s rays arrive parallel, so every upright object and its shadow make a right triangle with the same angle \theta. Same angles, similar triangles, same ratio.
!
The big one. At that same moment, the flagpole’s shadow is 15 m long. How tall is the flagpole? Guess first.
A 12 m
B 14.6 m
C 18.75 m
D 24 m
A: 12 m. Height =0.8\times15=12 m. Check: 12\div15=0.8, the same ratio as the student. The flagpole’s triangle is the student’s triangle scaled up \times7.5.
B is the adding trap (“the shadow is 0.4 m longer, so 15-0.4”): similar shapes scale by multiplying, not adding. C flips the ratio, 15\div0.8, but the pole must be shorter than its shadow, like the student. D is 15\times1.6: the height instead of the ratio.
+
Early finishers. Later that day the student’s shadow is 3.2 m. How long is the flagpole’s shadow now? At what sun angle is your shadow exactly as long as you are?
Now height \div shadow =1.6\div3.2=0.5, so the pole’s shadow is 12\div0.5=\boldsymbol{24} m. Shadow = height when the ratio is 1: the sun is 45^\circ up.

At lunch: measure a friend and their shadow. How tall is our school?

1

Similar Right Triangles and a Constant Ratio

Two right triangles that have an equal acute angle are similar, so the ratio of any pair of corresponding sides is the same in both, no matter how large or small each triangle is.
Example 1

A ratio that depends only on the angle

The four right triangles \triangle OA_1B_1,\ \triangle OA_2B_2,\ \triangle OA_3B_3 and \triangle OA_4B_4 all share the angle of 31^\circ at O. Using the side lengths shown, compute each ratio \dfrac{A_iB_i}{OA_i} and round it to one decimal place. The first row is done for you.

(a) Complete the ratios.
\dfrac{A_1B_1}{OA_1}=\dfrac{1.8}{3}=0.6
\dfrac{A_2B_2}{OA_2}=\dfrac{3.6}{6}= 0.6
\dfrac{A_3B_3}{OA_3}=\dfrac{5.4}{9}= 0.6
\dfrac{A_4B_4}{OA_4}=\dfrac{7.2}{12}= 0.6
All four are 0.6, even though the triangles have four different sizes.
(b) The ratio is therefore connected to the angle of 31^\circ itself.
\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}. Relative to the 31^\circ angle at O, each height A_iB_i is the opposite side and each base OA_i is the adjacent side.
(c) Evaluate \tan(31^\circ) on a calculator, to the nearest thousandth, and compare it to the ratios above.
\tan(31^\circ)\approx\boldsymbol{0.601}, the same value. The ratio depends only on the angle, not on the size of the triangle: it is the tangent ratio.
\tan(31^\circ) on the TI-30XIIS. Check the screen says DEG. TAN31)ENTER gives 0.600860619 → 0.601. The calculator opens the bracket for you after TAN.

Grow the triangle: the ratios don’t move

Keep the angle, change the size. Every side is multiplied by the same scale factor, so every ratio of two sides stays put. Change the angle instead, and the ratios change. Then watch what happens when the other acute angle becomes the reference angle.

2

Naming the Sides of a Right Triangle

In right triangle XYZ, with the right angle at Y and \angle YXZ=x^\circ, each side is named relative to x^\circ:

  • XZ, the side opposite the right angle, is the hypotenuse (hyp): always the longest side, and always the same side whichever acute angle is used.
  • YZ, the side opposite x^\circ, is the opposite side (opp).
  • XY, which forms x^\circ together with the hypotenuse, is the adjacent side (adj).

Using \angle XZY as the reference angle instead swaps opp and adj: the hypotenuse never changes, but “opposite” and “adjacent” are always defined relative to the angle being used.

Labelling routine (use it every time). 1. Label the hyp first: the side across from the right angle. 2. The opp side does not touch the reference angle. 3. The adj side touches the reference angle (and is not the hypotenuse).

Identifying Sides for a Given Reference Angle

Example 2

Reading opp, adj and hyp off a figure

For the reference angle x^\circ marked on each triangle, label the hypotenuse (hyp), the opposite side (opp), and the adjacent side (adj).

(a)
hyp = LM   opp = KL   adj = KM
The right angle is at K and x^\circ is at M. The hyp joins the two non-right-angle vertices: LM. The opp joins the right angle to the vertex away from x^\circ: KL. The adj joins the right angle to the vertex at x^\circ: KM.
(b)
hyp = TU   opp = SU   adj = ST
The right angle is at S and x^\circ is at T. hyp =TU (it joins T and U); opp =SU (right angle to U, the vertex away from x^\circ); adj =ST (right angle to T, where x^\circ is measured).
!
Tilted triangles. In (b) no side is horizontal, and the triangle is not sitting “the usual way”. That doesn’t matter: find the little square first, then follow the routine.

4

SOH CAH TOA

For a reference angle x^\circ in a right triangle, the three primary trigonometric ratios are

\sin x^\circ=\dfrac{\color{#7b4fc4}\text{opp}}{\color{#1f5bd6}\text{hyp}}
\cos x^\circ=\dfrac{\color{#c27a0e}\text{adj}}{\color{#1f5bd6}\text{hyp}}
\tan x^\circ=\dfrac{\color{#7b4fc4}\text{opp}}{\color{#c27a0e}\text{adj}}

memorized as SOH CAH TOA: Sine is Opposite over Hypotenuse; Cosine is Adjacent over Hypotenuse; Tangent is Opposite over Adjacent. The ratio found in Example 1 was exactly the tangent ratio, \dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}.

Size check

The hypotenuse is always the longest side, so for an acute angle \sin x^\circ and \cos x^\circ are always less than 1. The tangent compares the two legs, so it can be any positive number: less than 1 when opp < adj, and greater than 1 when opp > adj.
5

Computing the Ratios From Given Side Lengths

When all three side lengths of a right triangle are known, each ratio for either acute angle can be written down directly from SOH CAH TOA. Label the sides first, then write each fraction and simplify it.

Example 3

The same triangle, the two different reference angles

Right triangle PQR is shown, with the right angle at Q.

(a) Using \angle R as the reference angle, write \sin R, \cos R and \tan R as fractions in simplest form.
From \angle R: opp =PQ=9, adj =QR=12, hyp =PR=15.
\sin R=\dfrac{9}{15}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{3}{5}},   \cos R=\dfrac{12}{15}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{4}{5}},   \tan R=\dfrac{9}{12}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{3}{4}}
(b) Now use \angle P as the reference angle. Write \sin P, \cos P and \tan P as fractions in simplest form. How do these compare to your answers in (a)?
From \angle P the two legs trade places: opp =QR=12, adj =PQ=9, hyp =PR=15 (the hypotenuse never changes).
\sin P=\dfrac{12}{15}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{4}{5}},   \cos P=\dfrac{9}{15}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{3}{5}},   \tan P=\dfrac{12}{9}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{4}{3}}
So \sin R=\cos P and \cos R=\sin P. The two acute angles are complementary (\angle R+\angle P=90^\circ), and the sine of one is always the cosine of the other.
Example 4

Finding the missing hypotenuse first

In right triangle LMN the right angle is at N, NM=8 and NL=15. The hypotenuse LM is not given.

(a) Use the Pythagorean theorem to determine the length of LM.
LM^2=NM^2+NL^2=8^2+15^2=64+225=289, so LM=\sqrt{289}=\boldsymbol{17}.
(b) Using \angle L=x^\circ as the reference angle, write \sin x^\circ, \cos x^\circ and \tan x^\circ as fractions in simplest form, and then as decimals rounded to the nearest thousandth.
From \angle L=x^\circ: opp =NM=8, adj =NL=15, hyp =LM=17.
\sin x^\circ=\dfrac{8}{17}\approx\boldsymbol{0.471},   \cos x^\circ=\dfrac{15}{17}\approx\boldsymbol{0.882},   \tan x^\circ=\dfrac{8}{15}\approx\boldsymbol{0.533}
The square root and the decimals. 2ndx² is \sqrt{\phantom{x}}: √289)ENTER gives 17. Then 8÷17ENTER gives 0.470588235 → 0.471.

Missing hypotenuse? Pythagoras first

Sine and cosine both need the hypotenuse. If only the two legs are given, find it with c^2=a^2+b^2, check it is the longest side, then write the ratios.

More Practice With Two Triangles

Example 5

Two triangles, two different reference angles

Two right triangles are shown below. Determine each ratio as a fraction in simplest form.

(i) \sin A
\sin A=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{5}{13}}
(ii) \cos A
\cos A=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{12}{13}}
(iii) \tan A
\tan A=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{5}{12}}
(iv) \sin Z
\sin Z=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{24}{25}}
(v) \cos Z
\cos Z=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{7}{25}}
(vi) \tan Z
\tan Z=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{24}{7}}
Think How do you know which side is the hypotenuse in each triangle? Which of the six ratios is greater than 1, and why is that allowed?
In \triangle ABC the right angle is at C, so AB=13 is the hypotenuse; from \angle A, opp =CB=5 and adj =CA=12. In \triangle XYZ the right angle is at Y, so XZ=25 is the hypotenuse; from \angle Z, opp =XY=24 and adj =YZ=7. Only \tan Z=\frac{24}{7} is greater than 1: a tangent compares the two legs and may exceed 1; a sine or cosine never can.

Key ideas

Same angle, same ratios. Right triangles with an equal acute angle are similar: every side is multiplied by the same scale factor, so each ratio of two sides depends only on the angle.
Name the sides from the reference angle. hyp first (across from the right angle), opp doesn’t touch the angle, adj touches it. Switch angles: opp and adj swap, hyp stays.
SOH CAH TOA.
\sin x^\circ=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}, \cos x^\circ=\dfrac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}, \tan x^\circ=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}. Sine and cosine are less than 1; tangent can be any positive number.
From side lengths: missing hypotenuse? Pythagoras first. Write each fraction, then simplify. The sine of one acute angle is the cosine of the other.
