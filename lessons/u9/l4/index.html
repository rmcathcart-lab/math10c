Math 10C · Unit 9 Trigonometry · Lesson 4

Finding the Measure of an Angle in a Right Triangle

Two sides known and the angle unknown. Name the sides from the angle, choose the ratio that pairs them, then run the calculator backwards with \sin^{-1}, \cos^{-1} or \tan^{-1}.

I can…
  • use a calculator (in degree mode) to go from an angle to a ratio, and back from a ratio to the angle with \sin^{-1}, \cos^{-1}, \tan^{-1}
  • name the two known sides as opposite, adjacent or hypotenuse from the unknown angle, then choose SOH, CAH or TOA
  • find an angle from two sides, to the nearest degree or tenth of a degree
  • find an angle of elevation or depression, measured from the horizontal, and explain why the two are equal
  • find an angle in an isosceles triangle by splitting it, and an angle that spans two right triangles
M4 Develop and apply the primary trigonometric ratios (sine, cosine, tangent) to solve problems that involve right triangles.
Warm-up

Roof Pitch

A roof’s pitch is how far it rises for every 12 across. (6/12: up 6, across 12.)

1
A 6/12 roof goes 36 across. How far up does it rise?
18. Three groups of 12 across, so three groups of 6 up.
2
Write each pitch as a decimal (rise \div run): 4/12, 6/12, 12/12.
4/12\approx0.33, 6/12=0.5, 12/12=1.
3
On the 12/12 roof, rise = run. What angle does the roof make with the horizontal?
\mathbf{45^\circ}. Rise = run makes an isosceles right triangle, so its two acute angles are equal: 90^\circ\div2=45^\circ.
!
The big one. A 24/12 roof is twice as steep as a 12/12 roof. So is it a 90^\circ wall of shingles? Guess first: the 24/12 roof’s angle is…
A 90^\circ
B 67.5^\circ
C about 63^\circ
C: about 63^\circ. The pitch is \dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}, so \tan\theta=\dfrac{24}{12}=2 and \theta=\tan^{-1}(2)\approx63.4^\circ.
A is “twice as steep, so twice the angle, 2\times45^\circ”: doubling the ratio does not double the angle, and a 90^\circ roof would need an endless pitch. B is “halfway from 45^\circ to 90^\circ”: the same straight-line thinking, softened.
+
Early finishers. Double the 4/12 roof to 8/12. Does its angle double from 18.4^\circ to 36.9^\circ? And what pitch gives a 30^\circ roof?
No: \tan^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{8}{12}\right)\approx33.7^\circ. A 30^\circ roof rises 12\tan30^\circ\approx6.9, so about a 7/12 pitch.

Vote with your arm: hold it at the angle you think a 24/12 roof makes!

1

Inverse Trigonometric Ratios

A calculator can also run SOH CAH TOA backwards. If the value of a trigonometric ratio for an acute angle is known, the angle itself is recovered with the inverse ratio.

If \sin\theta^\circ=k then \theta^\circ=\sin^{-1}(k), and likewise \cos^{-1} and \tan^{-1}. On most calculators these are 2ndSIN, 2ndCOS and 2ndTAN, in degree mode. Read \sin^{-1}(k) as “the angle whose sine is k”; it does not mean 1\div\sin.
!
Degree mode. The TI-30XIIS shows DEG at the top of the screen. If it says RAD or GRAD, \sin 30 will not be 0.5: press MODE, move to DEG on the top line, press ENTER, then CLEAR.

Think What does the calculator say for \sin^{-1}(1.2)? Why must that happen?
An error (DOMAIN). \sin\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}, and the hypotenuse is the longest side, so a sine can never be more than 1. No angle has a sine of 1.2.

Why the inverse works: read the graph backwards

As an angle opens from 0^\circ to 90^\circ, its sine climbs, its cosine falls and its tangent climbs without end. Each ratio value meets the graph once, so it belongs to exactly one acute angle. Forwards: up from the angle, across to the ratio. Backwards: across from the ratio, down to the angle.

2

Determining an Angle From Two Known Sides

Calculating an angle requires the lengths of two of the triangle’s three sides.

1Name each known side from the unknown angle: opposite, adjacent or hypotenuse.
2Choose the ratio that pairs those two names: SOH, CAH or TOA.
3Write it as an equation, then solve with the inverse ratio.
!
Name first, choose second. Reading the ratio off the wrong pair of sides is the usual error. Name the sides from the angle you want, and only then pick SOH, CAH or TOA.
Example

Naming the sides before choosing the ratio

Complete the following for the diagram shown.

Relative to the angle p^\circ, 21 is the length of the ADJACENT side and 35 is the length of the HYPOTENUSE.
The trigonometric ratio which involves the ADJACENT side and the HYPOTENUSE is the COSINE ratio.
\cos p^\circ=\dfrac{21}{35}   so   p^\circ= 53^\circ
From p^\circ: 21 is ADJACENT and 35 is the HYPOTENUSE (it faces the right angle). Adjacent with hypotenuse \Rightarrow CAH.
\cos p^\circ=\dfrac{21}{35}=0.6\ \Rightarrow\ p^\circ=\cos^{-1}(0.6)=53.13^\circ\approx\mathbf{53^\circ}
One line on the TI-30XIIS. 2ndCOS21÷35)ENTER gives 53.13010235 → 53°.

Name the sides, then choose the ratio

The hypotenuse faces the right angle, the opposite side faces the angle, and the adjacent side touches the angle (and is not the hypotenuse). The two sides you know decide the ratio.

Example 2

Two sides given, angle to the nearest degree

In each case, determine the measure of the indicated angle to the nearest degree.

(a)
From a^\circ: 5 is OPPOSITE, 11 is the HYPOTENUSE \Rightarrow SOH.
\sin a^\circ=\dfrac{5}{11}=0.4545
a^\circ=\sin^{-1}(0.4545)
=27.04^\circ\approx\mathbf{27^\circ}
(b)
From b^\circ: 15 is ADJACENT, 23 is the HYPOTENUSE \Rightarrow CAH.
\cos b^\circ=\dfrac{15}{23}=0.6522
b^\circ=\cos^{-1}(0.6522)
=49.29^\circ\approx\mathbf{49^\circ}
(c)
From c^\circ: 14 is ADJACENT, 19 is OPPOSITE \Rightarrow TOA.
\tan c^\circ=\dfrac{19}{14}=1.3571
c^\circ=\tan^{-1}(1.3571)
=53.62^\circ\approx\mathbf{54^\circ}
Type the fraction straight in. 2ndSIN5÷11)ENTER keeps every decimal of 5\div11, so nothing is lost to rounding.
Think In (a), what is the other acute angle? Find it two ways.
The angles of a right triangle add to 180^\circ, so 90^\circ-27.04^\circ=62.96^\circ\approx63^\circ. Or name the sides from the bottom angle: 5 is now ADJACENT, so \cos^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{5}{11}\right)=62.96^\circ. The same side is opposite one acute angle and adjacent to the other.
3

Angle of Elevation and Angle of Depression

The angle of elevation is measured upward from a horizontal line to the line of sight of an object above the horizontal.
The angle of depression is measured downward from a horizontal line to the line of sight of an object below it.

Both are measured from the horizontal, never from the vertical. The horizontal line at the observer and the horizontal line at the object are parallel, and the line of sight is a transversal crossing both, so the alternate interior angles they form are equal:

angle of depression from the higher point = angle of elevation from the lower point

In the diagram, the angle of depression of the object from the observer equals the angle of elevation of the observer from the object.

Example 3

Angle of elevation of the sun

A flagpole 6.4 m tall casts a shadow 9.2 m long. Determine, to the nearest tenth of a degree, the angle of elevation of the sun.

From \theta^\circ: the pole (6.4 m) is OPPOSITE and the shadow (9.2 m) is ADJACENT \Rightarrow TOA.
\tan\theta^\circ=\dfrac{6.4}{9.2}=0.6957\ \Rightarrow\ \theta^\circ=\tan^{-1}(0.6957)=34.82^\circ\approx\mathbf{34.8^\circ}
2ndTAN6.4÷9.2)ENTER gives 34.82448916 → 34.8° to the nearest tenth.

The sun, the pole and its shadow

The sun’s ray that grazes the top of the pole lands at the tip of the shadow. The angle of elevation of the sun is the angle between that ray and the ground at the shadow’s tip.

Example 4

Angle of depression from the top of a cliff

A sea kayaker is on the water below a cliff. The cliff is 68 m high, and the kayaker is 155 m from the base of the cliff.

(a) Draw a diagram to represent this situation, and mark the angle of depression of the kayaker from the top of the cliff.
The cliff is vertical, the water is horizontal, and the dashed line at the top of the cliff is the horizontal through the observer. \alpha^\circ is the angle of depression.
(b) Determine, to the nearest degree, the angle of depression of the kayaker from the top of the cliff.
\alpha^\circ equals the angle of elevation of the cliff top from the kayaker (alternate interior angles), and that angle sits in the right triangle.
From it, the cliff (68 m) is OPPOSITE and the water distance (155 m) is ADJACENT \Rightarrow TOA.
\tan\alpha^\circ=\dfrac{68}{155}=0.4387
\alpha^\circ=\tan^{-1}(0.4387)=23.69^\circ\approx\mathbf{24^\circ}
2ndTAN68÷155)ENTER gives 23.68752634 → 24°.
!
The angle of depression is outside the triangle. It sits between the horizontal and the line of sight, at the top. The angle at the top inside the triangle is measured from the vertical cliff: 90^\circ-23.69^\circ=66.31^\circ, which is not the answer.

The cliff, the kayak and the Z

Two parallel horizontals and the line of sight between them make a Z. The angles tucked into the two corners of a Z are alternate angles, and they are always equal. That is why the angle of depression can be found inside the right triangle, at the kayak.

4

Angles in Isosceles Triangles

SOH CAH TOA applies only inside a right triangle. An isosceles triangle is not in general a right triangle, but dropping a perpendicular from its apex to its base splits it into two congruent right triangles, each of which the ratios do apply to.

The perpendicular bisects both the base and the apex angle, so the angle found in one half is half of the apex angle.
Example 5

Splitting an isosceles triangle

Consider isosceles triangle PQR, in which PQ=18 cm, QR=18 cm, and PR=26 cm.

(a) Why can SOH CAH TOA not be used directly in triangle PQR to determine the measure of angle PQR?
Triangle PQR has no right angle, so none of its sides is a hypotenuse and no side is opposite or adjacent to a right angle. SOH CAH TOA works only inside a right triangle.
(b) Determine, to the nearest degree, the measure of angle PQR by splitting triangle PQR into two congruent right triangles.
The perpendicular QM bisects PR, so PM=26\div2=13, and it bisects angle PQR.
In right triangle PMQ: PM=13 is OPPOSITE \angle PQM and PQ=18 is the HYPOTENUSE \Rightarrow SOH.
\sin(\angle PQM)=\dfrac{13}{18}=0.7222\ \Rightarrow\ \angle PQM=46.24^\circ
\angle PQR=2(46.24^\circ)=92.48^\circ\approx\mathbf{92^\circ}
Double before you round. 2×2ndSIN13÷18)ENTER gives the whole apex angle, 92.47651… → 92°, with no rounding along the way.

Split it, fold it, double it

No right angle, no SOH CAH TOA. Drop the perpendicular, check that the two halves match, solve one half, then double.

5

Combining Two Right Triangles

Some diagrams show two right triangles joined along a common side. The Pythagorean Theorem gives the length of the shared side, and the two triangles are then used together (one angle from each) to find an angle that spans both.

Example 6

An angle split across two right triangles

The diagram shows two right triangles sharing the common side AD, where D lies on BC between B and C.

(a) Use the Pythagorean Theorem to calculate the length of AD.
In right triangle ABD, AB=41 is the hypotenuse and BD=9 is a leg:
AD=\sqrt{41^2-9^2}=\sqrt{1681-81}=\sqrt{1600}=\mathbf{40}
(b) Determine, to the nearest degree, the measure of angle BAC.
First DC=\sqrt{85^2-40^2}=\sqrt{7225-1600}=\sqrt{5625}=75.
In \triangle ABD, from \angle BAD: BD=9 is OPPOSITE, AD=40 is ADJACENT \Rightarrow TOA, so \angle BAD=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{9}{40}\right)=12.68^\circ.
In \triangle ADC, from \angle DAC: DC=75 is OPPOSITE, AD=40 is ADJACENT \Rightarrow TOA, so \angle DAC=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{75}{40}\right)=61.93^\circ.
\angle BAC=12.68^\circ+61.93^\circ=74.61^\circ\approx\mathbf{75^\circ}
The whole chain. 2ndx² is \sqrt{\phantom{x}} (it opens a bracket too). Both angles in one line: 2ndTAN9÷40)+2ndTAN75÷40)ENTER.

Two triangles, solved in a chain

The shared side is the bridge: find it in the first triangle, use it in the second, then put the two angles together.

✓

Key ideas

Inverse ratios run SOH CAH TOA backwards: \sin\theta=k\Rightarrow\theta=\sin^{-1}(k), “the angle whose sine is k”. 2nd SIN, in degree mode. Not 1\div\sin.
Two sides → angle. Name the two known sides from the angle, choose SOH, CAH or TOA, solve with the inverse. Round only at the end.
Elevation (up) and depression (down) are measured from the horizontal. They form a Z with the line of sight, so depression from the top = elevation from the bottom.
No right angle? Make one. Split an isosceles triangle down the middle (then double), or find a shared side with Pythagoras and add the two angles.
