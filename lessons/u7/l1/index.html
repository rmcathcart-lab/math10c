Slope–Intercept Form and the Graph of a Line

Every non-vertical line has an equation y=\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{m}x+\textcolor{#12805a}{b}: m tilts the line and b slides it. Read them off a graph, read them off an equation, and use them to draw the line.

I can…
  • tell a linear relation from a non-linear one just by looking at its equation
  • read the slope and the y-intercept of a line off its graph, and explain why they match m and b in y=mx+b
  • rearrange an equation into the form y=\ldots before reading m and b
  • graph a line from y=mx+b: plot (0,b) and step off the rise and run, or find and join the two intercepts
RF6 Relate linear relations expressed in: slope–intercept form (y=mx+b); general form (Ax+By+C=0); slope–point form (y-y_1=m(x-x_1)) to their graphs.

Recognizing Linear Relations

(Review from Unit 5.)

A relation is linear when its graph is a straight line. Every non-vertical line can be written as y=mx+b for constants m,\,b\in R; vertical lines have equations of the form x=k. Such equations are linear equations. Every other relation is non-linear, and its graph is a curve rather than a straight line.

In y=mx+b, the variable x appears only to the first power. It is never…

y=x^{\textcolor{#c93a33}{2}}raised to a power
\textcolor{#c93a33}{x}y=6multiplied by another variable
y=\sqrt{\textcolor{#c93a33}{x}}under a root
y=\dfrac{1}{\textcolor{#c93a33}{x}}in a denominator
y=2^{\textcolor{#c93a33}{x}}an exponent

Reading Slope and y-intercept From a Graph

When a linear equation is already solved for y, use its graph to investigate what the numbers m and b in y=mx+b tell you about the line. Examples 2 and 3 lead to the rule.

From Unit 6: \text{slope}=\dfrac{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{\text{rise}}}{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{\text{run}}}, the same between any two points on a line. The y-intercept is where the line crosses the y-axis, at a point (0,\,b).

Remember the cups?

The stacks from the warm-up make a straight line. Its steepness and its starting height are hiding right in the equation.

Read it off the grid

Find where the line crosses the y-axis, then count a slope triangle from that point to another grid point on the line.

Example 2

Slope and y-intercept from two marked points

Naomi graphed the linear equation y=\dfrac{3}{4}x+2, shown below with points A and B marked on the line.

(a) Use points A and B to determine the slope and y-intercept of the graph.
A(-4,-1) and B(0,2).
m=\dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}=\dfrac{2-(-1)}{0-(-4)}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{3}{4}}
B is the point where the line crosses the y-axis, so the y-intercept is \boldsymbol{2}.
(b) Compare the values found in (a) with the coefficient of x and the constant term in y=\dfrac{3}{4}x+2.
The coefficient of x is \tfrac{3}{4}: the slope from (a). The constant term is 2: the y-intercept from (a). Because the equation is already solved for y, the coefficient of x and the constant term are m and b.
The slope on the calculator. Brackets around the top and the bottom, then 2ndF◂▸D turns the decimal into a fraction: (2−(−)1)÷(0−(−)4)ENTER2ndF◂▸D shows 3┘4, which means \tfrac{3}{4}.
Example 3

Two more lines, and a conjecture

(a) Two lines are graphed at the left. Use the grid to state the slope and y-intercept of each.
y=3x-2: slope = 3 y-intercept = -2
y=-\tfrac{3}{2}x+4: slope = -\tfrac{3}{2} y-intercept = 4
y=3x-2 crosses the y-axis at (0,-2), so b=-2; from there, right 1 and up 3 lands on (1,1), so m=3.
y=-\tfrac{3}{2}x+4 crosses at (0,4), so b=4; from there, right 2 and down 3 lands on (2,1), so m=-\tfrac{3}{2}.
(b) Make a conjecture about the slope and y-intercept of the graph of the linear equation y=mx+b.
For y=mx+b the graph is always a straight line whose slope is exactly m and whose y-intercept is exactly b. Both lines agree: the coefficient of x is the slope, and the constant term is the y-intercept.

Test the conjecture: two dials

The m dial turns the line; its needle always points along the line. The b dial slides the line up and down. Watch the equation as the dials turn, then drag the dials yourself.

!
The sign belongs to the number. In y=3x\mathbin{\textcolor{#12805a}{-}}\textcolor{#12805a}{2}, b=-2, not 2. In y=\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{-\tfrac{3}{2}}x+4, m=-\tfrac{3}{2}: the line falls.

When the Equation Is Not Yet Solved For y

If a linear equation does not arrive in the form y=mx+b, the coefficient of x and the constant term you can see are not automatically the slope and y-intercept.

Example 4

An equation that must be rearranged first

The graph of the linear equation 3y=6x-9 is shown below.

(a) Use the graph to determine the slope and y-intercept of 3y=6x-9.
The line crosses the y-axis at (0,-3), so the y-intercept is \boldsymbol{-3}.
From (0,-3) to (2,1): rise =1-(-3)=4, run =2-0=2.
\text{slope}=\dfrac{4}{2}=\boldsymbol{2}
(b) Explain why, in this case, the slope is not 6 (the coefficient of x) and the y-intercept is not -9 (the constant term).
3y=6x-9 is not solved for y: a factor of 3 still multiplies y. The 6 and the -9 belong to 3y, not to y alone. Divide every term by 3:
y=2x-3
which is now in slope–intercept form and gives slope 2 and y-intercept -3, matching the graph.

Getting y alone: moves that keep the balance

An equation is a balance. Whatever you do to one side, do to the whole other side: dividing by 3 means dividing every term by 3.

Slope–Intercept Form of the Equation of a Line y=\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{m}x+\textcolor{#12805a}{b}
The graph of an equation in the form y=mx+b (or of a function f(x)=mx+b) is a straight line with slope m and y-intercept b. This is the slope–intercept form of the equation of a line, and its graph can be drawn without a table of values. An equation that is not already solved for y must first be rearranged into this form before m and b can be read off directly.
Example 5

Reading m and b from the equation alone

Determine the slope and y-intercept of the graph of each linear equation.

(a) y=4x+5
Already solved for y.
slope =\boldsymbol{4}
y-intercept =\boldsymbol{5}
(b) y=9-\dfrac{3}{5}x
Rewrite in y=mx+b order:
y=-\dfrac{3}{5}x+9
slope =\boldsymbol{-\tfrac{3}{5}}
y-intercept =\boldsymbol{9}
(c) 8y=6x+3
Divide every term by 8:
y=\dfrac{6}{8}x+\dfrac{3}{8}=\dfrac{3}{4}x+\dfrac{3}{8}
slope =\boldsymbol{\tfrac{3}{4}}
y-intercept =\boldsymbol{\tfrac{3}{8}}
Think In (c), what goes wrong if you divide only 8y and 6x by 8, and leave the 3 alone?
You would get y=\tfrac{3}{4}x+3: the right slope but the wrong y-intercept. The balance tips, because the 3 is still 8 times too big. Every term must be divided.
(c) in lowest terms. The a b/c key builds a fraction and ENTER reduces it: 6a b/c8ENTER shows 3┘4, the slope. 3a b/c8ENTER stays 3┘8: already in lowest terms.

Graphing a Line From Its Equation

Once a line’s equation is written as y=mx+b, it can be sketched without a table of values in either of two ways.

Slope and y-intercept. Plot the y-intercept (0,b), then step out more points using the slope as \dfrac{\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{\text{rise}}}{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{\text{run}}}. Join them.
The two intercepts. Find the y-intercept (set x=0) and the x-intercept (set y=0), plot both points, and join them.
!
A negative slope has one negative part. -\tfrac{3}{4}=\tfrac{-3}{4}=\tfrac{3}{-4}: step it off as down 3, right 4, or as up 3, left 4. Both steps land on the same line.

The two-intercepts method

Every point on the y-axis has x=0, and every point on the x-axis has y=0. Put those zeros into the equation.

Example 6

Graphing from the slope, and from the two intercepts

(a) Consider y=3x-4. State the slope and y-intercept, mark the y-intercept on the grid, use the slope to mark three more points, and join them to graph the line.
Tap the grid to plot points (tap a point again to remove it).
m=3=\tfrac{3}{1} and b=-4, so plot (0,-4).
Right 1, up 3 gives (1,-1), then (2,2); left 1, down 3 gives (-1,-7). Join the four points.
(b) Consider y=\dfrac{5}{6}x-5. State the y-intercept, determine the x-intercept algebraically, mark both intercepts on the grid, and join them to graph the line.
Tap the grid to plot points (tap a point again to remove it).
y-intercept: set x=0, giving b=-5, so (0,-5) is on the line.
x-intercept: set y=0.
0=\dfrac{5}{6}x-5\ \Rightarrow\ \dfrac{5}{6}x=5\ \Rightarrow\ x=6
Join (0,-5) and (6,0).
(b) on the calculator. From \tfrac{5}{6}x=5, divide 5 by the whole fraction, in brackets: 5÷(5÷6)ENTER gives 6. Without the brackets, 5÷5÷6 gives 0.1\overline{6}: wrong.

Which line is which?

With y=mx+b you can match an equation to its graph at a glance: b says where the line crosses the y-axis, and m says which way it tilts and how steeply.

Key ideas

Linear or not? A linear equation has x only to the first power: never squared, under a root, in a denominator, an exponent, or multiplied by another variable. Its graph is a straight line.
Slope–intercept form y=\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{m}x+\textcolor{#12805a}{b}. The coefficient of x is the slope (it tilts the line); the constant term is the y-intercept (it slides the line). Each keeps its sign.
Solve for y first. 3y=6x-9 does not show m and b. Reorder terms or divide every term (y=2x-3), then read m=2, b=-3.
Graph without a table. Plot (0,b) and step the rise over the run; or set x=0 and y=0 to find both intercepts and join them.
