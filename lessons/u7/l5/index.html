Graphing Linear Equations: A Strategy for Every Form

Choosing the fastest hand-graphing strategy for the form you are given, and recovering an equation from a table of data.

I can…
  • recognize y=mx+b, y-y_1=m(x-x_1) and Ax+By+C=0 as the same line written three ways, and say what each form shows directly
  • graph y=mx+b from (0,b) and y-y_1=m(x-x_1) from (x_1,y_1) by stepping the slope, without rearranging
  • graph Ax+By+C=0 from its two intercepts, and find another point when C=0 or an intercept is a fraction
  • match equations to graphs using the sign and size of m and the y-intercept
  • find the equation of a line from a table of data, write it in general form, and solve it for either variable
RF6 Relate linear relations expressed in: slope–intercept form (y=mx+b); general form (Ax+By+C=0); slope–point form (y-y_1=m(x-x_1)) to their graphs.

1

Three Ways to Write the Same Line

Every non-vertical line can be written in any of the three forms below, and each one advertises different information up front.

Ax+By+C=0
General form
hides the slope and the intercepts, but both intercepts drop out by substitution
y=mx+b
Slope–intercept form
hands you the slope m and the y-intercept b
y-y_1=m(x-x_1)
Slope–point form
hands you the slope m and one point (x_1,y_1) on the line, not necessarily an intercept

The graphing strategy should exploit whichever piece of information the given form makes easiest to read, so that the equation never has to be rearranged at all.

2

Graphing by Hand Without Changing the Form

Choosing a Graphing Strategy from the Form
Given formStrategy: graph it without rearranging
Slope–intercept
y=mx+b		Plot the y-intercept (0,b). From there step the slope m=\dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} repeatedly to plot more points, then join them.
Slope–point
y-y_1=m(x-x_1)		Plot the given point (x_1,y_1). From there step the slope m repeatedly to plot more points, then join them.
General
Ax+By+C=0		Substitute y=0 and solve for the x-intercept; substitute x=0 and solve for the y-intercept. Plot both intercepts and draw the line through them. If C=0, both intercepts are (0,0); if an intercept is a fraction, it is hard to plot. Then substitute a convenient x to find another point with integer coordinates, or solve for y (Example 2).
Horizontal or vertical
y=k or x=k		y=k is horizontal through (0,k), slope 0; x=k is vertical through (k,0), slope undefined. Plot that single point and rule the line.

All of these strategies can describe the very same line. The three equations in Example 1 are one line written three ways, so the three graphs must come out identical.

Example 1

One line, three forms, three strategies

Without altering the form in which each equation is written, state the strategy you would use to graph it by hand, then draw the graph on the grid provided.

(a) y=3x-6
Slope–intercept form: the equation is already solved for y, so m=3 and b=-6 are read straight off.
Plot the y-intercept (0,-6).
m=3=\tfrac{3}{1}: from (0,-6) step up 3 and right 1 to (1,-3), again to (2,0), again to (3,3). Join the points.
(b) y+3=3(x-1)
Slope–point form: y-(-3)=3(x-1) matches y-y_1=m(x-x_1), so m=3 and the known point is (1,-3). It is not an intercept.
Plot (1,-3). Step up 3, right 1 to (2,0) and again to (3,3); step down 3, left 1 to (0,-6). Join the points.
(c) 3x-y-6=0
General form: find both intercepts by substitution.
y=0: 3x-6=0\Rightarrow x=2, so (2,0).
x=0: -y-6=0\Rightarrow y=-6, so (0,-6).
Plot both intercepts and draw the line through them.
Think All three graphs came out as the same line. How could you have known that before graphing?
y+3=3(x-1) and 3x-y-6=0 both rearrange to y=3x-6: same slope, same y-intercept, so the same line.
Tap a grid to plot points (tap a point again to remove it).

Slope–intercept and slope–point: start at a point, step the slope

Both forms hand you a starting point and the slope. Plot the point, then step the rise and the run again and again, in both directions.

!
Slope–point form flips the signs. y+1=-\tfrac{2}{3}(x-4) is y-(-1)=-\tfrac{2}{3}(x-4), so the point is (4,-1), not (-4,1): the numbers in the brackets have the opposite signs to the coordinates.

General form: cover up x, then cover up y

Every point on the y-axis has x=0, so covering the x-term leaves the y-intercept. Every point on the x-axis has y=0, so covering the y-term leaves the x-intercept. Two points, one line, unless they are the same point or a fraction gets in the way.

Example 2

When the quick strategy needs care

Graph each line by hand, without rearranging it. In (a), watch the signs; in (b) and (c), the intercepts alone are not enough: find another point.

(a) y+1=-\dfrac{2}{3}(x-4)
Slope–point form: y-(-1)=-\tfrac{2}{3}(x-4), so the point is (4,-1), not (-4,1): the numbers in the brackets have the opposite signs to the coordinates.
Plot (4,-1). m=-\tfrac{2}{3}=\tfrac{2}{-3}: step up 2, left 3 to (1,1), (-2,3), (-5,5). (Down 2, right 3 also works.)
(b) 3x-4y=0
General form with C=0: both intercepts are (0,0), only one point.
Substitute a convenient x: x=4 gives 12-4y=0, so y=3 and (4,3) is on the line; likewise (-4,-3).
Draw the line through (0,0) and (4,3).
(c) 2x+3y-4=0
y=0: x=2, so (2,0). x=0: 3y=4, y=\tfrac{4}{3}, a fraction, hard to plot accurately.
Find lattice points instead: x=-1 gives 3y=6, y=2; x=5 gives 3y=-6, y=-2.
Draw the line through (2,0), (-1,2) and (5,-2).
Why (c) needs care. The y-intercept is 4\div3: 4÷3ENTER gives 1.333333333, a third of the way between two grid lines. A point that is guessed by eye drags the whole line off course.
Tap a grid to plot points (tap a point again to remove it).
3

Reading a Graph Straight From Its Equation

Slope and y-intercept alone are enough to picture a line: the sign of m says whether it rises or falls, and the size of m says how steep it is. Rewriting each equation as y=mx+b is therefore enough to match equations to graphs without plotting a single point.

Example 3

Matching equations to graphs

Match each linear relation to its graph below. Each graph is used exactly once.

Equation 1: 3x-y+2=0
Equation 2: y=-2x+1
Equation 3: y-1=-\dfrac{1}{3}(x-3)
Equation 4: 3y-x-9=0
Eqn 1 → Graph D
Eqn 2 → Graph A
Eqn 3 → Graph C
Eqn 4 → Graph B
Show the slope and y-intercept you used for each equation.
Rewrite each as y=mx+b and compare with the four graphs.
Eqn 1: 3x-y+2=0\Rightarrow y=3x+2 (m=3, b=2): steep and rising, crossing the y-axis at 2: Graph D.
Eqn 2: y=-2x+1 (m=-2, b=1): steep and falling, crossing at 1: Graph A.
Eqn 3: y-1=-\tfrac{1}{3}(x-3)\Rightarrow y=-\tfrac{1}{3}x+2 (m=-\tfrac13, b=2): shallow and falling, crossing at 2: Graph C.
Eqn 4: 3y-x-9=0\Rightarrow y=\tfrac{1}{3}x+3 (m=\tfrac13, b=3): shallow and rising, crossing at 3: Graph B.

Example 3 check: rise or fall, steep or shallow, where it crosses

For each equation: solve for y, read m and b, and look for the graph with that direction, that steepness and that crossing.

4

From a Table of Data to an Equation, and Back Again

A linear relation can also be recovered from a table of values: plot the ordered pairs, confirm they are collinear, take any two of them to find the slope, and build the equation in whichever form is convenient before converting to general form. Solving that finished equation for the other variable reverses the relationship.

Example 4

From data to an equation, and back

The table records values of x and y taken from a continuous linear relationship.

(a) Plot the data on the grid and determine, in general form, the equation of the linear relation the data represents.
The eight plotted points lie on a straight line.
Using (0,32) and (70,158):
m=\dfrac{158-32}{70-0}=\dfrac{126}{70}=\dfrac{9}{5}
The y-intercept is 32 (the value at x=0), so y=\tfrac{9}{5}x+32.
Multiply every term by 5: 5y=9x+160.
General form (A>0, integer coefficients): 9x-5y+160=0.
Check with (30,86): 9(30)-5(86)+160=270-430+160=0 ✓
(b) Rewrite the equation of the linear relation (i) in terms of x, that is y=\ldots, and (ii) in terms of y, that is x=\ldots.
(i) In terms of x: y=\tfrac{9}{5}x+32.
(ii) Solve 9x-5y+160=0 for x: 9x=5y-160\Rightarrow x=\dfrac{5y-160}{9}=\dfrac{5}{9}(y-32).
(c) The formulas in (b) are well known in science. What quantities do you think x and y represent?
These are the Celsius–Fahrenheit conversion formulas: x is a temperature in °C and y is the same temperature in °F. Check: x=100 gives y=\tfrac{9}{5}(100)+32=212, the boiling point of water.
The slope as a fraction. (158−32)÷70ENTER gives 1.8; 2ndF◂▸D turns it into 9┘5, which means \tfrac{9}{5}. To test (c): 9÷5×100+32ENTER gives 212.

From the table to the equation, step by step

Equal steps in x giving equal steps in y mean the points are collinear. Two points give the slope, x=0 gives the intercept, and clearing the fraction gives general form.

And back again: solving for the other variable

y=\tfrac95x+32 is a two-step machine: multiply by \tfrac95, then add 32. Solving for x builds the reverse machine: undo each step in reverse order.

Both directions on the calculator. Body temperature: 9÷5×37+32ENTER gives 98.6 °F, and back: 5÷9×(98.6−32)ENTER gives 37 °C. The brackets make the subtraction happen first.
✓

Key ideas

One line, three forms. y=mx+b shows m and (0,b); y-y_1=m(x-x_1) shows m and (x_1,y_1) (signs flipped); Ax+By+C=0 gives both intercepts by substitution.
Graph without rearranging. Plot the point the form hands you and step \tfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}; or cover x, cover y for the intercepts. If C=0 or an intercept is a fraction, pick a convenient x for another integer point.
Match by m and b. Sign of m: rises or falls. Size of m: steep or shallow. b: where it crosses the y-axis.
Data → equation → back. Equal steps mean collinear; slope from two points, b at x=0, then clear fractions for general form (9x-5y+160=0). Solving for the other variable undoes the steps in reverse order: x=\tfrac59(y-32).
