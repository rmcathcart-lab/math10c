From Slope to Equation: Building Linear Equations with y=mx+b

Two numbers build a line: the slope m and the y-intercept b. Find them, drop them into y=mx+b, and the equation is done.

I can…
  • write y=mx+b straight from a given slope and y-intercept, and recognise a point (0,\ k) as the y-intercept
  • read m (count the rise and run) and b (where the line crosses the y-axis) off a grid, and write the equation
  • get the slope of a new line from a parallel line (the same slope) or a perpendicular line (the negative reciprocal)
  • write the equations of horizontal lines y=k and vertical lines x=k, and explain why a vertical line can’t be written as y=mx+b
RF7 Determine the equation of a linear relation, given: a graph; a point and the slope; two points; a point and the equation of a parallel or perpendicular line, to solve problems.
Warm-up

The Cricket Thermometer

This made-up cricket chirps faster when it’s warmer, at a steady rate.

1
How warm is it at 40 chirps per minute?
15\ ^\circ\text{C}. 40 chirps is halfway from 20 to 60, so it is halfway from 10\ ^\circ\text{C} to 20\ ^\circ\text{C}.
2
Each 1\ ^\circ\text{C} warmer adds how many chirps per minute?
4 chirps per ^\circ\text{C}: 40 more chirps for 10 more degrees, and 40\div10=4. Flip it: each chirp is worth \tfrac14\ ^\circ\text{C}.
3
Follow the pattern down. How cold is it when the cricket goes silent?
5\ ^\circ\text{C}. From 20 chirps, drop 20 more chirps: that is 20\div4=5 degrees colder than 10\ ^\circ\text{C}.
!
The big one. You hear the cricket chirp 100 times in one minute. How warm is it? Guess first.
A 25\ ^\circ\text{C}
B 30\ ^\circ\text{C}
C 33\ ^\circ\text{C}
D 50\ ^\circ\text{C}
B: 30\ ^\circ\text{C}. From 60 to 100 chirps is 40 more chirps, worth 10\ ^\circ\text{C} more: 20+10=30. As an equation, T=\tfrac14C+5, so T=\tfrac14(100)+5=30.
A did 100\div4: the slope without the +5 starting value. C and D scaled as if doubling the chirps doubled the temperature (\tfrac{100}{60}\times20 or \tfrac{100}{20}\times10). The line does not pass through (0,\ 0).
Real crickets really do this: Dolbear’s law (1897) for the snowy tree cricket.

1

Building an Equation Directly from Slope and y-Intercept

When the slope m and the y-intercept b are both known, nothing is left to calculate: substitute both values into y=mx+b. A given point of the form (0,\ k) is the y-intercept, so it supplies b=k immediately.
y=\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{m}x+\textcolor{#13807a}{b}
m slope =\dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}
b y-intercept: the line crosses the y\text{-axis} at (0,\ b)
!
A negative b becomes a minus sign. Substituting b=-5 gives y=\tfrac34x+(-5), written tidily as y=\tfrac34x-5. And only a point with x=0 is the y-intercept: (-5,\ 0) is not.
Example 1

Slope and y-intercept given directly

(a) Write the equation of the line that passes through (0,\ -5) and has slope \dfrac{3}{4}.
(0,\ -5) has x=0, so it is the y-intercept: b=-5. The slope is given, m=\tfrac34.
Substituting into y=mx+b: \boldsymbol{y=\dfrac{3}{4}x-5}
Check. x=0 gives y=\tfrac34(0)-5=-5, so (0,\ -5) is on the line, and the coefficient of x is \tfrac34 ✓.
(b) Write the equation of the line with y-intercept 7 and slope -\dfrac{2}{5}.
Both values are given directly: m=-\tfrac25 and b=7.
Substituting into y=mx+b: \boldsymbol{y=-\dfrac{2}{5}x+7}
Check. x=0 gives y=7, the required y-intercept, and the coefficient of x is -\tfrac25 ✓.
2

Reading Slope and y-Intercept from a Graph

When a line passes through lattice points (points whose coordinates are both integers), count the rise and run between two of them to get m, and read b where the line crosses the y-axis. Substituting both into y=mx+b gives the equation.

Example 2

Reading m and b off a grid

Each line below passes through points with integer coordinates. For each line, state the slope and the y-intercept, and determine the equation of the line.

Line l_1: m= \tfrac14 b= -6
Two lattice points on l_1: (0,\ -6) and (4,\ -5). Rise =-5-(-6)=1, run =4-0=4, so m=\tfrac14. It crosses the y-axis at (0,\ -6), so b=-6.
\boldsymbol{y=\dfrac14x-6}. Check at (4,\ -5): \tfrac14(4)-6=1-6=-5 ✓
Line l_2: m= -2 b= 5
Two lattice points on l_2: (0,\ 5) and (1,\ 3). Rise =3-5=-2, run =1-0=1, so m=-2. It crosses the y-axis at (0,\ 5), so b=5.
\boldsymbol{y=-2x+5}. Check at (1,\ 3): -2(1)+5=3 ✓
Think Would two other lattice points on l_1 give the same slope? Try (-8,\ -8) and (8,\ -4).
Yes. Run 16, rise 4: \tfrac{4}{16}=\tfrac14. Any two points on a line give the same slope, so pick two that are easy to count.

3

Parallel and Perpendicular Line Conditions

Two non-vertical lines are parallel exactly when their slopes are equal. All vertical lines are parallel.
Two non-vertical lines are perpendicular exactly when their slopes are negative reciprocals of each other, that is, when their product is -1. A horizontal line and a vertical line are always perpendicular.

Either condition supplies the slope of a new line from the equation, or from two points, of another line. For a parallel line, copy the slope triangle:

Why negative reciprocals?

The blue line has slope \tfrac{2}{3}: run 3, rise 2. Turn its slope triangle 90^\circ about the origin: the run of 3 becomes a rise of 3, and the rise of 2 becomes a run of 2 to the left.

The turned (red) line is perpendicular to the blue one, and its slope is \tfrac{3}{-2}=-\tfrac{3}{2}. Rise and run swap places and one sign changes, so \tfrac{2}{3}\times\left(-\tfrac{3}{2}\right)=-1.

Think A horizontal line and a vertical line are perpendicular. Why can’t the “product is -1” test show it?
The vertical line’s slope is undefined, so there is no number to multiply by 0. That is why the rule says non-vertical lines, and the horizontal–vertical case is stated on its own.
Example 3

Slope from a parallel or perpendicular condition

Determine the equation of each line.

(a) the line parallel to y=\dfrac{1}{2}x+7, with the same y-intercept as y=5x-3
Parallel lines have equal slopes, so m=\tfrac12, matching y=\tfrac12x+7.
The y-intercept of y=5x-3 is b=-3.
\boldsymbol{y=\dfrac12x-3}
Check. The slope \tfrac12 equals the slope of y=\tfrac12x+7, and x=0 gives y=-3, the same y-intercept as y=5x-3 ✓.
(b) the line passing through (0,\ 11), perpendicular to the line joining (3,\ -4) and (-6,\ 2)
Slope of the line joining (3,\ -4) and (-6,\ 2): m=\dfrac{2-(-4)}{-6-3}=\dfrac{6}{-9}=-\dfrac23.
Perpendicular slope = negative reciprocal =\tfrac32. The line passes through (0,\ 11), so b=11.
\boldsymbol{y=\dfrac32x+11}
Check. -\tfrac23\times\tfrac32=-1, and x=0 gives y=11 ✓.

Two questions, every time: where does m come from, and where does b come from?

4

Horizontal and Vertical Lines

Horizontal and vertical lines are special cases. Use the grid in Example 4 to work out what their slopes are, and what their equations look like.

Example 4

Horizontal and vertical lines

The grid shows two horizontal lines, L_1 and L_2, and one vertical line, L_3.

(a) State the slope and y-intercept of the horizontal line L_1.
L_1 sits at height y=3 for every x, so it rises 0 units for any run: \boldsymbol{m=0}. It crosses the y-axis at (0,\ 3), so \boldsymbol{b=3}.
(b) Use y=mx+b to determine the equation of L_1.
Substituting m=0 and b=3: y=0\cdot x+3, i.e. \boldsymbol{y=3}.
Check. (-5,\ 3) and (7,\ 3) are both on L_1, and both satisfy y=3 ✓.
(c) Predict the equation of the horizontal line L_2.
L_2 is also horizontal and crosses the y-axis at (0,\ -6), so m=0, b=-6 and \boldsymbol{y=-6}.
Check. (4,\ -6) lies on L_2 and satisfies y=-6 ✓.
(d) State the slope and y-intercept of the vertical line L_3.
Take (-3,\ -8) and (-3,\ 8) on L_3: run =-3-(-3)=0. Slope divides by the run, and division by 0 is undefined, so the slope is undefined.
L_3 never crosses the y-axis: no y-intercept.
(e) Explain why the form y=mx+b cannot be used to determine the equation of L_3.
The form y=mx+b needs a numerical value of m to substitute. The slope of a vertical line is undefined, so there is no such value and the form cannot describe L_3.
(f) Predict the equation of the vertical line L_3.
Every point of L_3 has x=-3, and L_3 crosses the x-axis at (-3,\ 0), so \boldsymbol{x=-3}.
Check. (-3,\ -8) and (-3,\ 8) both satisfy x=-3 ✓.
(d) Ask the calculator for the slope of L_3. From (-3,\ -8) to (-3,\ 8): (8−(−)8)÷((−)3−(−)3)ENTER gives an error: there is no number to give. For L_1, from (-5,\ 3) to (7,\ 3), the rise is 0 and the slope is 0.

Flatten a line: y=mx+b collapses to y=b

Keep the y-intercept and turn the slope down to 0. Watch the x term disappear.

Stand a line up: the slope and the y-intercept run away

Now keep the x-intercept and steepen the line until it is vertical. What happens to m and b?

Summary: horizontal and vertical lines.
Horizontal: y=k
Rises 0 for every unit of run, so m=0 and y=mx+b collapses to y=b. It crosses the y-axis at (0,\ k).
Vertical: x=k
Any two points have a run of 0, and division by 0 is undefined: the slope is undefined. With no m to substitute, it can’t be written as y=mx+b. It crosses the x-axis at (k,\ 0).
✓

Key ideas

Find two numbers, substitute. With the slope m and the y-intercept b, the equation is y=mx+b. A point (0,\ k) is the y-intercept: b=k. Tidy +(-5) to -5.
Off a graph. b: where the line crosses the y-axis. m: count rise over run between two lattice points (falling left to right means negative). Check with a third point.
Parallel: copy the slope triangle, equal slopes. Perpendicular: turn it 90^\circ, so rise and run swap and one sign flips: the negative reciprocal (product -1), e.g. -\tfrac23\to\tfrac32.
Horizontal lines: m=0, equation y=k. Vertical lines: slope undefined, no y-intercept, not y=mx+b; equation x=k.
