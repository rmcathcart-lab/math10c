Length, Distance, and Midpoint of a Line Segment

How long is a piece of a line, and where is its middle? A horizontal or vertical segment can be measured by counting squares or by subtracting coordinates. A slanted segment hides a right triangle, and the Pythagorean theorem becomes the distance formula. The midpoint is half the run and half the rise along.

I can…
  • find the length of a horizontal or vertical segment by counting squares, or by |x_2-x_1| and |y_2-y_1|
  • find the length of a slanted segment from the right triangle under it, as an exact radical in simplest form and as a decimal
  • use the distance formula d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2} when there is no grid to count on
  • locate a midpoint by counting, by walking half the run and half the rise, and with the midpoint formula
  • work backwards from a midpoint to a missing endpoint
RF3 Demonstrate an understanding of slope with respect to: rise and run; line segments and lines; rate of change; parallel lines; perpendicular lines.
Warm-up

Can You Get Wi-Fi?

The router at (0,\ 0) reaches up to 5 units in a straight line, in every direction. Each house sits on a grid point; 1 square = 1 unit.

1
How far is house A from the router? Does it get Wi-Fi?
A is at (-4,\ 0): a horizontal segment, 4 units. Yes.
2
House B is 3 right and 4 up. Wi-Fi?
A right triangle with legs 3 and 4: \sqrt{9+16}=\sqrt{25}=5. Yes, exactly on the edge.
3
House C is only 4 left and 4 up. Both are less than 5. Does C get Wi-Fi?
No! \sqrt{16+16}=\sqrt{32}\approx5.66>5. The straight-line distance is the hypotenuse, longer than either leg. (D at (-3,\ -4) is exactly 5: yes. E at (5,\ -3): \sqrt{34}\approx5.83, no.)
!
The big one. How many grid points are exactly 5 units from the router, right on the edge of the signal? Guess first.
A 4
B 8
C 12
D infinitely many
C: 12. We need whole numbers with x^2+y^2=25: (\pm5,\ 0), (0,\ \pm5), (\pm3,\ \pm4), (\pm4,\ \pm3). That’s 2+2+4+4=12 points (B and D are two of them). Tap All the edge points to see them.
A only goes straight up, down, left and right. B adds the “diagonal” corners but misses that (3,\ 4) and (4,\ 3) are different points. D is true for the whole circle, but it passes through only 12 grid points.
+
Early finishers. House C wants a signal booster exactly halfway between the router and the house. Where should it go?
(-2,\ 2): halfway across (2 left) and halfway up (2 up). That point is the midpoint, later today.
+
Extra challenge. How many grid points are within 5 units (on or inside the edge)?
81. Count column by column: x=0 has 11 points; x=\pm1, \pm2 and \pm3 have 9 each; x=\pm4 have 7; x=\pm5 have 1: 11+2(9+9+9+7+1)=81.

1

Horizontal and Vertical Line Segments

A line segment is the piece of a line between two endpoints. With endpoints A and B it is written \overline{AB}, and its length is written AB. Unlike a full line, a segment stops at each endpoint, so it has a definite length, and a length is never negative.

A segment is horizontal when its endpoints share the same y-coordinate, and vertical when they share the same x-coordinate. Count the grid squares, or, without a grid, subtract the coordinates that change and take the positive value.

Absolute value. |a| is the size of a with its sign dropped: |-9|=9 and |9|=9.
Horizontal (same y):
length =|x_2-x_1|
Vertical (same x):
length =|y_2-y_1|
Example 1

Horizontal and vertical segments on a grid

Segments AB and CD are graphed below.

(a) Find the length of each segment by counting grid squares.
AB=6 units, CD=7 units.
(b) State the coordinates of the endpoints of each segment.
A(-5,\ 5), B(1,\ 5); C(4,\ 3), D(4,\ -4).
(c) For AB, subtract the x-coordinates (right minus left). For CD, subtract the y-coordinates (upper minus lower).
x_B-x_A=1-(-5)=6; y_C-y_D=3-(-4)=7. Each difference matches the count in (a).
(d) Describe a rule for finding the length of a horizontal segment, and of a vertical segment, directly from the coordinates of its endpoints.
Horizontal: the positive difference of the x-coordinates, length =|x_2-x_1|. Vertical: the positive difference of the y-coordinates, length =|y_2-y_1|. The coordinate that is the same at both ends plays no part. The absolute value is there because subtracting in the other order gives the same size with the opposite sign, and a length can never be negative.
Example 2

Lengths from the coordinates

(a) Line segment PQ has endpoints P(5,\ -2) and Q(-4,\ -2). Determine the length of PQ.
Both endpoints have y=-2, so PQ is horizontal.
PQ=|x_Q-x_P|=|-4-5|=|-9|=\boldsymbol{9} units
(b) Line segment UV has endpoints U(3,\ -2) and V(3,\ -11). Determine the length of UV.
Both endpoints have x=3, so UV is vertical.
UV=|y_V-y_U|=|-11-(-2)|
=|-11+2|=|-9|=\boldsymbol{9} units
Think In (a), does it matter which endpoint you call x_1 and which you call x_2?
No. |5-(-4)|=|9|=9 and |-4-5|=|-9|=9. The order only changes the sign (which way you walked), and the absolute value drops the sign.
!
Brackets around a negative. Subtracting a negative coordinate is where most mistakes happen: -11-(-2)=-11+2=-9, not -13. Write the brackets first, then simplify.
2

Diagonal Segments and the Pythagorean Theorem

Most segments on a grid run diagonally. To find the length of a diagonal segment, build a right triangle under it (or over it: either corner works): a horizontal leg (the run), a vertical leg (the rise), and the segment itself as the hypotenuse.

Pythagorean Theorem. In a right triangle, the square of the hypotenuse c equals the sum of the squares of the two legs a and b:
a^2+b^2=c^2\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad c=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}
On a grid, the run and the rise are the legs, and solving for c gives the segment’s length.
!
Don’t add the run and the rise. Walking the two legs is the long way round. The slanted segment is the short cut, so it is always shorter than run + rise and longer than either leg (house C in the warm-up: legs 4 and 4, but the distance is about 5.66, not 8).
Example 3

Diagonal lengths by the Pythagorean theorem

Determine the length of each segment shown on the grid. Give each answer as (i) an exact value in simplest radical form, and (ii) a decimal approximation to the nearest hundredth.

(a) Segment AB
A(-5,\ -2), B(-2,\ 7). Run =|-2-(-5)|=3; rise =|7-(-2)|=9.
AB^2=3^2+9^2=9+81=90
(i) AB=\sqrt{90}=\sqrt{9\times10}=\boldsymbol{3\sqrt{10}} units
(ii) AB\approx\boldsymbol{9.49} units
(b) Segment CD
C(3,\ 6), D(7,\ -4). Run =|7-3|=4; rise =|-4-6|=10.
CD^2=4^2+10^2=16+100=116
(i) CD=\sqrt{116}=\sqrt{4\times29}=\boldsymbol{2\sqrt{29}} units
(ii) CD\approx\boldsymbol{10.77} units
Decimals of the radicals. 2ndx² is \sqrt{\phantom{x}}: √90ENTER gives 9.486832981 → 9.49. Check the simplified form gives the same decimal: 3×√10ENTER.

Exact answers: simplest radical form

The exact length is a square root. To simplify it, look for the biggest perfect square that divides the number under the root, and take its square root out front. If no perfect square (other than 1) divides it, the root is already in simplest form.

3

The Distance Formula

Counting squares stops working when the points are far apart or there is no grid. But the triangle is always there: for any two points P(x_1,\ y_1) and Q(x_2,\ y_2), the run is x_2-x_1 and the rise is y_2-y_1, so the Pythagorean theorem always produces the same pattern.

The Distance Formula. The distance d between P(x_1,\ y_1) and Q(x_2,\ y_2) is
d_{PQ}=\sqrt{(\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x_2-x_1})^2+(\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{y_2-y_1})^2}
run squared plus rise squared, then the square root: it is just a^2+b^2=c^2 written with coordinates. Because both differences are squared, either point can go first.
!
Brackets around a negative before you square it. (-12)^2=144, but the calculator reads (−)12x² as -(12^2)=-144. A distance can never come out negative or undefined; if it does, look for a missing bracket.
Squaring a negative. ((−)12)x²ENTER gives 144. Without the brackets you get −144.
Example 4

The distance formula

(a) Find the exact length of the line segment from G(-15,\ 6) to H(-9,\ -6).
x_2-x_1=-9-(-15)=6, \ y_2-y_1=-6-6=-12
GH=\sqrt{6^2+(-12)^2}=\sqrt{36+144}=\sqrt{180}=\sqrt{36\times5}=\boldsymbol{6\sqrt5}
(b) A city places a coordinate grid over a map so that emergency crews can estimate distances. One cell tower is at (-52,\ 134) and a second is at (89,\ 61), with each grid unit representing 1 km. Determine the distance between the two towers, to the nearest kilometre.
x_2-x_1=89-(-52)=141, \ y_2-y_1=61-134=-73
d=\sqrt{141^2+(-73)^2}=\sqrt{19\,881+5329}=\sqrt{25\,210}\approx158.78
The towers are 159 km apart.
All in one line. √(141x²+((−)73)x²)ENTER gives 158.7765726, so about 159 km. Close the brackets: the square root covers the whole sum.

Example 4(b) on the map

The grid is far too big to count squares on, but the triangle is still there: 141 km across and 73 km down.

4

The Midpoint of a Line Segment

The midpoint M of \overline{PQ} is the point on the segment that is the same distance from P as from Q: exactly in the centre. It splits \overline{PQ} into two equal segments, \overline{PM} and \overline{MQ}.

On a grid, a midpoint can be found by counting squares. For a horizontal or vertical segment, count in from both ends; the midpoint keeps the shared coordinate, and its other coordinate is the average of the endpoints’.

Example 5

Midpoints by counting

(a) Count squares to locate the midpoint M of horizontal segment AB. How do the endpoints’ x-coordinates combine to give M’s?
A(-5,\ 3) and B(3,\ 3) are 8 squares apart, so M is 4 squares in from either end. M keeps y=3, and its x is the average: \dfrac{-5+3}{2}=-1. \boldsymbol{M(-1,\ 3)}
(b) Oblique segment EF: average the x-coordinates and the y-coordinates separately to predict its midpoint. Check by counting squares.
E(4,\ 5), F(-2,\ -3): x_M=\dfrac{4+(-2)}{2}=1, y_M=\dfrac{5+(-3)}{2}=1, so \boldsymbol{M(1,\ 1)}. Check: from F, 3 right and 4 up reaches (1,\ 1), and the same again reaches E.

Half the run, half the rise

A slanted segment has no shared coordinate to count along. Walk the triangle instead: go across and up, but stop after half of each, and you are standing on the midpoint.

5

The Midpoint Formula

The Midpoint Formula. For a line segment joining P(x_1,\ y_1) and Q(x_2,\ y_2), the midpoint M has coordinates
M\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)
In words: the midpoint’s x-coordinate is the average of the endpoints’ x-coordinates, and its y-coordinate is the average of their y-coordinates.
!
Add, don’t subtract. The distance formula subtracts coordinates (how far apart); the midpoint formula adds them and halves (the average). Mixing them up is the most common slip in this lesson.
Example 6

Midpoints from the formula

Determine the coordinates of the midpoint of the line segment with each given pair of endpoints.

(a) P(6,\ 9),\ Q(14,\ 1)
x_M=\dfrac{6+14}{2}=\dfrac{20}{2}=10, \quad y_M=\dfrac{9+1}{2}=\dfrac{10}{2}=5
\boldsymbol{M(10,\ 5)}
(b) R(-7,\ 5),\ S(-15,\ -3)
x_M=\dfrac{-7+(-15)}{2}=\dfrac{-22}{2}=-11, \quad y_M=\dfrac{5+(-3)}{2}=\dfrac{2}{2}=1
\boldsymbol{M(-11,\ 1)}
Think Does it matter which endpoint you call P? What about in the distance formula?
No, in both. Addition doesn’t care about order (6+14=14+6), so the average is the same. In the distance formula the differences flip sign, but squaring erases the sign.
6

Working Backward: Finding a Missing Endpoint

Sometimes the midpoint (or part of it) is known and an endpoint coordinate is missing. Each coordinate of M gives an equation to solve,

x_M=\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad y_M=\dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2},

or think of the walk: the midpoint is halfway, so go the same again.

Example 7

Recovering a blotted coordinate

Devon recorded the coordinates of a trailhead P, a checkpoint M (the midpoint of the trail), and the campsite Q at the far end of the trail. Coffee spilled on the page and blotted out part of the trailhead’s coordinates and part of the checkpoint’s coordinates:

P(x_1,\ 12)
Q(-8,\ -6)
M(3,\ y_M)
(i) Determine the missing x-coordinate of the trailhead, P.
The midpoint’s x-coordinate is the average of the x-coordinates of P and Q:
\dfrac{x_1+(-8)}{2}=3\ \Longrightarrow\ x_1-8=6\ \Longrightarrow\ x_1=14
The trailhead is \boldsymbol{P(14,\ 12)}.
(ii) Determine the missing y-coordinate of the checkpoint, M.
The midpoint’s y-coordinate is the average of the y-coordinates of P and Q:
y_M=\dfrac{12+(-6)}{2}=\dfrac{6}{2}=3
The checkpoint is \boldsymbol{M(3,\ 3)}.
Check: the midpoint of P(14,\ 12) and Q(-8,\ -6) is \left(\dfrac{14-8}{2},\ \dfrac{12-6}{2}\right)=(3,\ 3) ✓
!
The midpoint is not an endpoint. To find a missing endpoint, don’t average the endpoint you know with the midpoint: that gives a point a quarter of the way along. Go past the midpoint by the same amount again.
✓

Key ideas

Horizontal and vertical: count the squares, or subtract. Horizontal (same y): |x_2-x_1|. Vertical (same x): |y_2-y_1|. The bars drop the sign: a length is never negative.
Every slanted segment hides a right triangle. The run and rise are the legs: d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}. Exact: simplest radical (\sqrt{90}=3\sqrt{10}); decimal: round at the end.
Midpoint = halfway. Count in from both ends, or walk half the run and half the rise. As a formula, average each coordinate: M\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right).
Working backward. The midpoint is halfway, so go the same again to reach the missing endpoint, or set \frac{x_1+x_2}{2}=x_M and solve. Check by finding the midpoint of your answer.
