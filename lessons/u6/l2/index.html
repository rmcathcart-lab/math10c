The Distance and Midpoint Formulas

Every segment hides a right triangle, and a point sitting exactly halfway along it. Today: measure any segment from its endpoints’ coordinates, test for right angles and straight lines, and find (or work backwards from) a midpoint.

I can…
  • turn a slanted segment into a right triangle and find its length with the Pythagorean theorem
  • use the distance formula d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}, giving exact (radical) or rounded answers
  • use side lengths to prove a right angle (the converse of the Pythagorean theorem) or that three points are collinear
  • locate a midpoint by counting, by walking half the run and half the rise, and with the midpoint formula
  • work backwards from a midpoint to a missing endpoint coordinate

Warm-up

Can You Get Wi-Fi?

The router at (0,\ 0) reaches up to 5 units in a straight line, in every direction. Each house sits on a grid point; 1 square = 1 unit.

1
How far is house A from the router? Does it get Wi-Fi?
A is at (-4,\ 0): a horizontal segment, 4 units. Yes.
2
House B is 3 right and 4 up. Wi-Fi?
A right triangle with legs 3 and 4: \sqrt{9+16}=\sqrt{25}=5. Yes, exactly on the edge.
3
House C is only 4 left and 4 up. Both are less than 5. Does C get Wi-Fi?
No! \sqrt{16+16}=\sqrt{32}\approx5.66>5. The straight-line distance is the hypotenuse, longer than either leg. (D at (-3,\ -4) is exactly 5: yes. E at (5,\ -3): \sqrt{34}\approx5.83, no.)
!
The big one. How many grid points are exactly 5 units from the router, right on the edge of the signal? Guess first.
A 4
B 8
C 12
D infinitely many
C: 12. We need whole numbers with x^2+y^2=25: (\pm5,\ 0), (0,\ \pm5), (\pm3,\ \pm4), (\pm4,\ \pm3). That’s 2+2+4+4=12 points (B and D are two of them). Tap All the edge points to see them.
A only goes straight up, down, left and right. B adds the “diagonal” corners but misses that (3,\ 4) and (4,\ 3) are different points. D is true for the whole circle, but it passes through only 12 grid points.

Early finishers. House C wants a signal booster exactly halfway between the router and the house. Where should it go?
(-2,\ 2): halfway across (2 left) and halfway up (2 up). That point is the midpoint, later today.

1

Turning a Segment into a Right Triangle

To measure the segment joining A(-4,\ -2) and B(8,\ 3), add a third point C(8,\ -2) directly below B and directly across from A. That builds a right triangle with the segment itself as the hypotenuse.

Triangle ACB has its right angle at C, so the Pythagorean theorem applies. The horizontal leg AC (the run) is the difference in x-coordinates, and the vertical leg CB (the rise) is the difference in y-coordinates.

AC=8-(-4)=12, \qquad CB=3-(-2)=5
(AB)^2=(AC)^2+(CB)^2=12^2+5^2=144+25=169\quad\Longrightarrow\quad AB=\sqrt{169}=13

The distance formula

Nothing in this depended on the points chosen. For any two points P(x_1,\ y_1) and Q(x_2,\ y_2) the horizontal leg has length x_2-x_1 and the vertical leg has length y_2-y_1, so the Pythagorean theorem always produces the same pattern.

The Distance Formula. The distance d between P(x_1,\ y_1) and Q(x_2,\ y_2) is
d_{PQ}=\sqrt{(\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x_2-x_1})^2+(\textcolor{#7b4fc4}{y_2-y_1})^2}
run squared plus rise squared, then the square root: it is just a^2+b^2=c^2 written with coordinates.

Because both differences are squared, it does not matter which point is called P and which is called Q: subtracting in the opposite order flips the sign of each difference, and squaring erases the sign either way.

!
Brackets around a negative before you square it. (-12)^2=144, but the calculator reads (−)12x² as -(12^2)=-144. A distance can never come out negative or undefined; if it does, look for a missing bracket.
Squaring a negative. ((−)12)x²ENTER gives 144. Without the brackets you get −144.
Example 1

Exact length from coordinates

Find the exact length of each line segment.

(a) P(3,\ -4) to Q(9,\ 4)
x_2-x_1=9-3=6, \ y_2-y_1=4-(-4)=8
PQ=\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=\sqrt{36+64}=\sqrt{100}=\boldsymbol{10}
(b) G(-15,\ 6) to H(-9,\ -6)
x_2-x_1=-9-(-15)=6, \ y_2-y_1=-6-6=-12
GH=\sqrt{6^2+(-12)^2}=\sqrt{36+144}=\sqrt{180}
=\sqrt{36\times5}=\boldsymbol{6\sqrt5}
Check a simplified radical. √180ENTER and 6×√5ENTER should give the same decimal. They do, so \sqrt{180}=6\sqrt5. The radical is the exact answer.
Example 2

Distance on a map grid

A city places a coordinate grid over a map so that emergency crews can estimate distances. One cell tower is at (-52,\ 134) and a second is at (89,\ 61), with each grid unit representing 1 km. Determine the distance between the two towers, to the nearest kilometre.

x_2-x_1=89-(-52)=141, \ y_2-y_1=61-134=-73
d=\sqrt{141^2+(-73)^2}=\sqrt{19\,881+5329}=\sqrt{25\,210}\approx158.78
The towers are 159 km apart.
All in one line. √(141x²+((−)73)x²)ENTER gives 158.7765726, so about 159 km. Close the brackets: the square root covers the whole sum.

Example 2 on the map

The grid is far too big to count squares on, but the triangle is still there: 141 km across and 73 km down.

Example 3

The converse of the Pythagorean theorem

(a) Explain how the lengths of the three sides of a triangle can be used to determine whether the triangle has a right angle.
Use the converse of the Pythagorean theorem. If a, b and c are the three side lengths with c the longest, and a^2+b^2=c^2, then the triangle must have a right angle, and that right angle lies opposite the longest side c (between the two shorter sides a and b). If a^2+b^2\ne c^2, the triangle has no right angle.
(b) L is the point (1,\ 1), M is (-3,\ 4), and N is (3,\ 12). Prove that \angle LMN is a right angle.
Find the three side lengths with the distance formula.
LM=\sqrt{(-3-1)^2+(4-1)^2}=\sqrt{16+9}=\sqrt{25}=5
MN=\sqrt{(3-(-3))^2+(12-4)^2}=\sqrt{36+64}=\sqrt{100}=10
LN=\sqrt{(3-1)^2+(12-1)^2}=\sqrt{4+121}=\sqrt{125}=5\sqrt5
LN is the longest side, so test LM^2+MN^2 against LN^2:
LM^2+MN^2=5^2+10^2=25+100=125 and LN^2=(5\sqrt5)^2=125.
They are equal, so the right angle lies opposite LN, at vertex M. Therefore \angle LMN is a right angle. \blacksquare
!
Test the longest side as c. The hypotenuse is always the longest side, so it is the only side that can be c. The right angle (if there is one) is at the vertex not on that side.

Squares on the sides

a^2 really is the area of a square built on side a. If the squares on the two shorter sides exactly add up to the square on the longest side, the triangle has a right angle.

Example 4

Collinear points

X is the point (-6,\ -2), Y is (-1,\ 10), and Z is (9,\ 34).

(a) Calculate the exact lengths of XY, YZ, and XZ.
XY=\sqrt{(-1-(-6))^2+(10-(-2))^2}=\sqrt{5^2+12^2}=\sqrt{169}=13
YZ=\sqrt{(9-(-1))^2+(34-10)^2}=\sqrt{10^2+24^2}=\sqrt{676}=26
XZ=\sqrt{(9-(-6))^2+(34-(-2))^2}=\sqrt{15^2+36^2}=\sqrt{1521}=39
13

XYZ
39
Notice XY+YZ=13+26=39=XZ.
(b) What do the results of part (a) tell you about the points X, Y, and Z?
Because XY+YZ=XZ exactly, Y lies directly between X and Z on one straight path: the three points are collinear, and segment XZ passes through Y. (Any detour off the line would make XY+YZ longer than XZ.)

Straight through, or a detour?

The straight path is the shortest way between two points. If going through a third point costs nothing extra, that point must be on the straight path.

2

What Is a Midpoint?

If a line segment has endpoints P and Q, its midpoint, usually labelled M, is the single point on the segment that is the same distance from P as it is from Q: the point exactly in the centre. Because M sits at the centre, it divides \overline{PQ} into two shorter segments, \overline{PM} and \overline{MQ}, of equal length.
The bar in \overline{PQ} is shorthand for “the line segment joining P and Q.”
3

Locating a Midpoint by Counting

When a segment is graphed on a grid its midpoint can often be found by counting squares. This works especially well for a horizontal or a vertical segment, since every point on such a segment already shares one coordinate with both endpoints.

Example 5

Midpoint of a horizontal and of a vertical segment

On each grid, count squares to locate the midpoint M of the segment and label it. Then record the coordinates of the endpoints and of M, and describe how the endpoints’ coordinates combine to produce the coordinate of M.

(a) Horizontal segment \boldsymbol{AB}: use the x-coordinates.
A(-5,\ 3) and B(3,\ 3). Counting gives 8 squares from A to B, so M is 4 squares in from either end: M(-1,\ 3).
A and B share the same y-coordinate, so M keeps it. The x-coordinate of M is the average of the endpoints’ x-coordinates: \dfrac{-5+3}{2}=\dfrac{-2}{2}=-1. \boldsymbol{M(-1,\ 3)}
(b) Vertical segment \boldsymbol{CD}: use the y-coordinates.
C(2,\ 5) and D(2,\ -3). Counting gives 8 squares from C down to D, so M is 4 squares below C: M(2,\ 1).
C and D share the same x-coordinate, so M keeps it. The y-coordinate of M is the average of the endpoints’ y-coordinates: \dfrac{5+(-3)}{2}=\dfrac{2}{2}=1. \boldsymbol{M(2,\ 1)}

Count from both ends

Two walkers start at the two ends and step toward each other at the same speed. Where they meet is the midpoint, and as a number it is the average of the two ends.

4

Midpoints of Oblique Segments

An oblique (slanted) segment has no shared coordinate to count along, but the pattern from Example 5 still applies: it simply has to be applied to the x-coordinates and to the y-coordinates separately.

Example 6

Midpoint of an oblique segment

Consider the oblique segment EF graphed below, with E(4,\ 5) and F(-2,\ -3).

(a) Apply the pattern from Example 5 to the x-coordinates and to the y-coordinates of E and F separately to predict the coordinates of the midpoint M of \overline{EF}. Then check your prediction by counting squares on the grid.
Average each coordinate separately:
x_M=\dfrac{4+(-2)}{2}=\dfrac{2}{2}=1, \quad y_M=\dfrac{5+(-3)}{2}=\dfrac{2}{2}=1.
Prediction \boldsymbol{M(1,\ 1)}. Counting along the segment: 3 right and 4 up from F(-2,\ -3) reaches (1,\ 1), and another 3 right and 4 up reaches E(4,\ 5). Confirmed.
(b) In words, describe a rule for finding the midpoint of any segment joining two points (x_1,\ y_1) and (x_2,\ y_2).
Rule: the midpoint’s x-coordinate is the average of the two endpoints’ x-coordinates, and the midpoint’s y-coordinate is the average of the two endpoints’ y-coordinates. It is the same averaging pattern as Example 5, applied to both coordinates at once instead of only one.

Half the run, half the rise

A walker heading from one end to the other goes across and up. Stop after half of each, and you are standing on the midpoint.

5

The Midpoint Formula

The Midpoint Formula. For a line segment joining P(x_1,\ y_1) and Q(x_2,\ y_2), the midpoint M has coordinates
M\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)
In words: the midpoint’s x-coordinate is the average of the two endpoints’ x-coordinates, and the midpoint’s y-coordinate is the average of the two endpoints’ y-coordinates.
!
Add, don’t subtract. The distance formula subtracts coordinates (how far apart); the midpoint formula adds them and halves (the average). Mixing them up is the most common slip in this lesson.
6

Applying the Midpoint Formula

With the formula in hand a grid is no longer needed: substitute directly, even when the coordinates are negative or decimals.

Example 7

Midpoints from the formula

Determine the coordinates of the midpoint of the line segment with each given pair of endpoints.

(a) P(6,\ 9),\ Q(14,\ 1)
x_M=\dfrac{6+14}{2}=\dfrac{20}{2}=10, \quad y_M=\dfrac{9+1}{2}=\dfrac{10}{2}=5
\boldsymbol{M(10,\ 5)}
(b) E(-7,\ 5),\ F(-15,\ -3)
x_M=\dfrac{-7+(-15)}{2}=\dfrac{-22}{2}=-11, \quad y_M=\dfrac{5+(-3)}{2}=\dfrac{2}{2}=1
\boldsymbol{M(-11,\ 1)}
Think Does it matter which endpoint you call P? What about in the distance formula?
No, in both. Addition doesn’t care about order (6+14=14+6), so the average is the same. In the distance formula the differences flip sign, but squaring erases the sign.
7

Working Backward: Finding a Missing Endpoint

Sometimes the midpoint (or part of it) is known and a missing endpoint coordinate has to be recovered. Since

x_M=\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad y_M=\dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2},

each coordinate of M supplies an equation that can be solved. Or think of the walk: the midpoint is halfway, so go the same again.

Example 8

Recovering a blotted coordinate

Devon recorded the coordinates of a trailhead P, a checkpoint M (the midpoint of the trail), and the campsite Q at the far end of the trail. Coffee spilled on the page and blotted out part of the trailhead’s coordinates and part of the checkpoint’s coordinates:

P(x_1,\ 12)
Q(-8,\ -6)
M(3,\ y_M)
(i) Determine the missing x-coordinate of the trailhead, P.
The midpoint’s x-coordinate is the average of the x-coordinates of P and Q:
\dfrac{x_1+(-8)}{2}=3\ \Longrightarrow\ x_1-8=6\ \Longrightarrow\ x_1=14
The trailhead is \boldsymbol{P(14,\ 12)}.
(ii) Determine the missing y-coordinate of the checkpoint, M.
The midpoint’s y-coordinate is the average of the y-coordinates of P and Q:
y_M=\dfrac{12+(-6)}{2}=\dfrac{6}{2}=3
The checkpoint is \boldsymbol{M(3,\ 3)}.
Check: the midpoint of P(14,\ 12) and Q(-8,\ -6) is \left(\dfrac{14-8}{2},\ \dfrac{12-6}{2}\right)=(3,\ 3) ✓
!
The midpoint is not an endpoint. To find a missing endpoint, don’t average the endpoint you know with the midpoint: that gives a point a quarter of the way along. Go past the midpoint by the same amount again.
✓

Key ideas

Every segment hides a right triangle. Run x_2-x_1, rise y_2-y_1, and the segment is the hypotenuse: d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}. Either point can go first. Exact answers stay as simplified radicals.
Lengths prove things. If a^2+b^2=c^2 (with c the longest side), the triangle has a right angle opposite c. If AB+BC=AC exactly, then A, B, C are collinear.
Midpoint = halfway. Count in from both ends, or walk half the run and half the rise. As a formula, average each coordinate: M\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right).
Working backward. The midpoint is halfway, so go the same again to reach the missing endpoint, or set \frac{x_1+x_2}{2}=x_M and solve. Check by finding the midpoint of your answer.
