Slope Relationships: Collinearity, Parallel Lines, and Perpendicular Lines

Turning rise and run into a formula, then using slope to test whether points are collinear and to compare two different lines: parallel lines share a slope, and perpendicular lines have slopes that are negative reciprocals.

I can…
  • calculate slope with the slope formula m=\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}, including points with negative coordinates
  • find and explain errors in a slope calculation (mixed order, run over rise)
  • prove that three points are collinear (equal slopes and a shared point) and find a missing coordinate
  • recognize parallel lines (equal slopes) and perpendicular lines (slopes with a product of -1)
  • find slopes parallel and perpendicular to a given segment, use slopes to show a right angle, and solve for an unknown slope
RF3 Demonstrate an understanding of slope with respect to: rise and run; line segments and lines; rate of change; parallel lines; perpendicular lines.

1

The Slope Formula

In Lesson 3 slope was found by counting the rise and the run on a grid. Counting needs a graph, and it is slow when the points are far apart. Example 1 shows that the rise is simply a difference of y-coordinates and the run is a difference of x-coordinates, which turns counting into a formula.

Example 1

Rise over run, and the same slope from the formula

The graph shows line segments RS and TU.

(a) Count grid squares to find the rise and run of RS, then use \dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} to calculate its slope.
From R to S the segment rises 6 squares while running 8 squares right.
\text{slope}=\dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}=\dfrac{6}{8}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{3}{4}}
(b) State the coordinates of R and S. Show that the rise equals y_S-y_R and the run equals x_S-x_R, and use these differences to confirm the slope from part (a).
R(1,\ 2), S(9,\ 8).
rise =y_S-y_R=8-2=6 and run =x_S-x_R=9-1=8: exactly the squares counted in (a).
m_{RS}=\dfrac{8-2}{9-1}=\dfrac{6}{8}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{3}{4}}, which agrees with (a).
(c) Without counting grid squares, subtract coordinates in the same way to calculate the slope of segment TU.
T(2,\ 9), U(8,\ 1). rise =y_U-y_T=1-9=-8, run =x_U-x_T=8-2=6.
m_{TU}=\dfrac{-8}{6}=\boldsymbol{-\dfrac{4}{3}}
The Slope Formula. The slope m of the line through P(x_1,\ y_1) and Q(x_2,\ y_2) is
m=\dfrac{\color{#7b4fc4}\text{rise}}{\color{#c27a0e}\text{run}}=\dfrac{\color{#7b4fc4}y_2-y_1}{\color{#c27a0e}x_2-x_1}or, with the point names,m_{PQ}=\dfrac{\color{#7b4fc4}y_Q-y_P}{\color{#c27a0e}x_Q-x_P}
Either point may be taken first, provided the coordinates are subtracted in the same order in the numerator and the denominator.
Example 2

The slope formula with negative coordinates

Find the slope of the line which passes through the points P(-3,\ 4) and Q(9,\ -6).

m_{PQ}=\dfrac{y_Q-y_P}{x_Q-x_P}=
m_{PQ}=\dfrac{-6-4}{9-(-3)}=\dfrac{-10}{12}=\boldsymbol{-\dfrac{5}{6}}
!
Brackets around a negative coordinate. x_Q-x_P=9-(-3)=9+3=12. Write the bracket every time you subtract a negative: it is the most common slip in this lesson.
The whole formula in one go. Bracket the top and the bottom: ((−)6−4)÷(9−(−)3)ENTER gives -0.8333…; then 2ndF◂▸D shows −5┘6, which means -\dfrac{5}{6}.

Same order, top and bottom

The formula works whichever point you start from, because starting from the other end flips the sign of the rise and the run. Mixing the order (one difference each way) flips only one of them.

Example 3

Finding the errors in three students’ work

Amara, Ben, and Chloe are each calculating the slope of the line segment with endpoints W(14,\ 3) and Z(-6,\ 11).

Amara
m_{WZ}=\dfrac{3-11}{-6-14}\phantom{m_{WZ}}=\dfrac{-8}{-20}m_{WZ}=\dfrac{8}{20}=\dfrac{2}{5}
Ben
m_{WZ}=\dfrac{-6-14}{11-3}\phantom{m_{WZ}}=\dfrac{-20}{8}m_{WZ}=-\dfrac{5}{2}
Chloe
m_{WZ}=\dfrac{11-3}{-6-14}\phantom{m_{WZ}}=\dfrac{8}{-20}m_{WZ}=-\dfrac{2}{5}

Since their answers do not all agree, at least two of the three have made an error. Describe every error you can find, and determine the correct slope of WZ.

Errors and the correct slope
Correct slope: m_{WZ}=\dfrac{y_Z-y_W}{x_Z-x_W}=\dfrac{11-3}{-6-14}=\dfrac{8}{-20}=\boldsymbol{-\dfrac{2}{5}}.
Amara subtracted the y-coordinates as y_W-y_Z but the x-coordinates as x_Z-x_W. Mixing the order between numerator and denominator flips the sign, giving +\dfrac{2}{5}. Her arithmetic is otherwise correct.
Ben put the run on top and the rise underneath (run over rise instead of rise over run), so his fraction is upside down: -\dfrac{5}{2}. His sign is right, since he used the same point order in both.
Chloe used the same point order top and bottom, so m_{WZ}=-\dfrac{2}{5} is correct.
2

Collinear Points

Points are collinear if one straight line passes through all of them.
Collinearity test. For three points P, Q and R, calculate m_{PQ} and m_{QR}. If the two slopes are equal and the two segments share the point Q, then P, Q and R are collinear. The shared point matters: two parallel segments also have equal slopes, but they lie on two different lines.
Example 4

Proving collinearity, and a missing coordinate

Consider the points X(-4,\ 10), Y(2,\ -2) and Z(11,\ -20).

(a) Prove that X, Y and Z are collinear.
m_{XY}=\dfrac{-2-10}{2-(-4)}=\dfrac{-12}{6}=-2
m_{YZ}=\dfrac{-20-(-2)}{11-2}=\dfrac{-18}{9}=-2
m_{XY}=m_{YZ} and the two segments share the point Y, so X, Y and Z are collinear.
(b) Find the value of w if the point W(5,\ w) lies on segment XZ.
m_{XZ}=\dfrac{-20-10}{11-(-4)}=\dfrac{-30}{15}=-2, and W must give the same slope from X:
\dfrac{w-10}{5-(-4)}=-2\ \Rightarrow\ \dfrac{w-10}{9}=-2\ \Rightarrow\ w-10=-18
\boldsymbol{w=-8}
!
Equal slopes are not enough on their own. Say both things in a collinearity proof: the slopes are equal and the segments share a point.

A missing coordinate: slide until the slope matches

A point on a line must give the line’s slope from any other point on it. So an unknown coordinate is whatever value makes the slopes equal: write the slope with the unknown in it, set it equal to the known slope, and solve.

3

Investigating Parallel Line Segments

Translating a line segment slides every point of it the same distance in the same direction, without turning or resizing it. Because the whole segment moves rigidly, a translated copy always points in exactly the same direction as the original. To translate a point 3 units right and 4 units down, add 3 to its x-coordinate and subtract 4 from its y-coordinate: (x,\ y)\to(x+3,\ y-4). On the grid, segment AB has endpoints A(-4,\ 3) and B(4,\ -1).

Tap the grid to plot points (tap a point to remove it).
(a) On the grid, draw the image of AB after each translation, and record the coordinates of the image.
i) 3 units right and 4 units down, forming CD.
C(-1, -1)   D(7, -5)
ii) 5 units left and 2 units up, forming EF.
E(-9, 5)   F(-1, 1)
C(-4+3,\ 3-4)=C(-1,\ -1), D(4+3,\ -1-4)=D(7,\ -5); E(-4-5,\ 3+2)=E(-9,\ 5), F(4-5,\ -1+2)=F(-1,\ 1).
(b) Calculate the slope of each of the three line segments.
m_{AB}=\dfrac{-1-3}{4-(-4)}=\dfrac{-4}{8}=-\dfrac{1}{2}
m_{CD}=\dfrac{-5-(-1)}{7-(-1)}=\dfrac{-4}{8}=-\dfrac{1}{2}
m_{EF}=\dfrac{1-5}{-1-(-9)}=\dfrac{-4}{8}=-\dfrac{1}{2}
(c) AB, CD and EF are parallel. Make a conjecture about the slopes of parallel line segments.
All three slopes are -\dfrac12. Conjecture: parallel line segments have equal slopes. A translation slides a segment rigidly without changing its direction, so every image points the same way as the original.
4

Investigating Perpendicular Line Segments

Now consider what happens to slope when a segment is rotated 90^\circ about the origin. A rotation of 90^\circ clockwise about the origin sends every point (x,\ y) to (y,\ -x): swap the coordinates, then change the sign of the new y-coordinate. For example, (2,\ 5)\to(5,\ -2). On the grid, point A(6,\ 3) has been joined to the origin O to form segment OA.

Tap to plot.
(a) i) Rotate OA through 90^\circ clockwise about O, draw the image on the grid, and label it OA'.   A'(3, -6)
A(6,\ 3)\to A'(3,\ -6): swap to (3,\ 6), then change the sign of the new y.
ii) Find the slopes of OA and OA', then multiply the two slopes together.
m_{OA}=\dfrac{3-0}{6-0}=\dfrac{1}{2}, m_{OA'}=\dfrac{-6-0}{3-0}=-2
m_{OA}\times m_{OA'}=\dfrac{1}{2}\times(-2)=\boldsymbol{-1}
(b) Make a conjecture about the slopes of two perpendicular line segments.
Conjecture: the slopes of two perpendicular line segments have a product of -1: each slope is the negative reciprocal of the other.
(c) Complete the following to prove your conjecture algebraically. Under a rotation of 90^\circ clockwise about O, point P(a,\ b)\to Q(b,\ -a).
m_{OP}=\dfrac{y_P-y_O}{x_P-x_O}=\dfrac{b-0}{a-0}= \dfrac{b}{a}m_{OQ}=\dfrac{y_Q-y_O}{x_Q-x_O}=\dfrac{-a-0}{b-0}= -\dfrac{a}{b}m_{OP}\times m_{OQ}= \dfrac{b}{a}\times\left(-\dfrac{a}{b}\right)=-1\quad(a,\ b\ne0)
m_{OP}=\dfrac{b}{a}, m_{OQ}=-\dfrac{a}{b}, and \dfrac{b}{a}\times\left(-\dfrac{a}{b}\right)=-\dfrac{ab}{ab}=\boldsymbol{-1} for any a,\ b\ne0: the conjecture holds for every segment, not just OA.
5

Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

Let two lines have slopes m_1 and m_2.

Parallel
the same slope
m_1=m_2
Perpendicular
product of the slopes is -1
m_1\times m_2=-1, equivalently m_1=-\dfrac{1}{m_2}
For perpendicular lines each slope is the negative reciprocal of the other, provided neither slope is 0.
Special case: a horizontal line (slope 0) and a vertical line (undefined slope) are perpendicular, even though their slopes cannot be multiplied. Two vertical lines are parallel.

Line segments are parallel (or perpendicular) when the lines containing them are, so the same tests apply to segments.

Quick reps. Tap a slope to see the parallel and perpendicular slopes.

m=\dfrac{3}{4}
∥ \dfrac34   ⊥ -\dfrac43
m=-5
∥ -5   ⊥ \dfrac15
m=-\dfrac{2}{9}
∥ -\dfrac29   ⊥ \dfrac92
m=0
∥ 0   ⊥ undefined
Example 5

Reading parallel and perpendicular from slopes alone

Line segment AC has a slope of \dfrac{5}{8}.

(a) Write the slope of line segment GH, which is parallel to AC.
Parallel lines have equal slopes: \boldsymbol{m_{GH}=\dfrac{5}{8}}
(b) Write the slope of line segment BF, which is perpendicular to AC.
Perpendicular slopes are negative reciprocals: \boldsymbol{m_{BF}=-\dfrac{8}{5}}
(Check: \dfrac58\times\left(-\dfrac85\right)=-1.)

Determine whether each pair of slopes below describes parallel lines, perpendicular lines, or neither.

(c) m_1=\dfrac{3}{10},\ m_2=\dfrac{9}{30}
\dfrac{9}{30}=\dfrac{3}{10}=m_1, so the slopes are equal: parallel.
(d) m_1=\dfrac{4}{9},\ m_2=-\dfrac{9}{4}
\dfrac{4}{9}\times\left(-\dfrac{9}{4}\right)=-1, so the slopes are negative reciprocals: perpendicular.

Parallel, perpendicular, or neither?

Simplify both slopes first. Equal means parallel; a product of -1 means perpendicular. Watch the trap: flipping the fraction without changing the sign gives neither.

Example 6

Slopes parallel and perpendicular to a given segment

P is the point (3,\ 8) and Q is the point (9,\ -4). Find the slope of a line segment

(a) parallel to PQ
m_{PQ}=\dfrac{-4-8}{9-3}=\dfrac{-12}{6}=-2
A parallel segment has the same slope: \boldsymbol{m=-2}
(b) perpendicular to PQ
A perpendicular segment has the negative reciprocal slope:
\boldsymbol{m=\dfrac{1}{2}} (Check: -2\times\dfrac12=-1.)

Using slope to find a right angle

A triangle has a right angle at a vertex when the two sides that meet there are perpendicular: their slopes multiply to -1.

Example 7

Using slope to find a right angle

\triangle DEF has coordinates D(-3,\ 1), E(-1,\ 5) and F(1,\ -1). Use slopes to show that the triangle is right-angled at D.

Show the right angle at D
The angle at D is formed by DE and DF, so test those two slopes.
m_{DE}=\dfrac{5-1}{-1-(-3)}=\dfrac{4}{2}=2,   m_{DF}=\dfrac{-1-1}{1-(-3)}=\dfrac{-2}{4}=-\dfrac{1}{2}
m_{DE}\times m_{DF}=2\times\left(-\dfrac12\right)=-1, so DE\perp DF and \triangle DEF is right-angled at D.
!
Pick the right pair of sides. The angle at D is between the two sides that contain D: DE and DF. The slope of EF plays no part.

Solving for an unknown slope

When one slope contains an unknown, write the condition as an equation: parallel: set the slopes equal; perpendicular: set the product equal to -1 (or set the unknown slope equal to the negative reciprocal).

Example 8

Solving for an unknown slope

Two lines have slopes of -\dfrac{5}{6} and \dfrac{k}{9} respectively. Find the value of k if the lines are

(a) parallel
Parallel: set the slopes equal.
\dfrac{k}{9}=-\dfrac{5}{6}\ \Rightarrow\ k=9\times\left(-\dfrac{5}{6}\right)=-\dfrac{45}{6}
\boldsymbol{k=-\dfrac{15}{2}}
(b) perpendicular
Perpendicular: set the product equal to -1.
-\dfrac{5}{6}\times\dfrac{k}{9}=-1\ \Rightarrow\ -\dfrac{5k}{54}=-1\ \Rightarrow\ 5k=54
\boldsymbol{k=\dfrac{54}{5}}
Check with fractions. (a) 9×(−)5÷6ENTER2ndF◂▸D → −15┘2. (b) 54÷5ENTER → 10.8, and \dfrac{10.8}{9}=1.2=\dfrac65: the negative reciprocal of -\dfrac56 ✓
✓

Key ideas

Slope formula. m=\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}: the rise and run read straight off the coordinates. Either point first, but the same order top and bottom; bracket negatives: 9-(-3).
Collinear. m_{PQ}=m_{QR} and the shared point Q. A missing coordinate: set its slope equal to the line’s slope and solve.
Parallel lines have equal slopes: a translated copy of the slope triangle. Simplify first: \tfrac{9}{30}=\tfrac{3}{10}.
Perpendicular: the slope triangle turned 90^\circ. Rise and run swap and one sign flips: negative reciprocals, m_1\times m_2=-1. Horizontal \perp vertical (0 and undefined).
