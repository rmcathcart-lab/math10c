Converting Square and Cubic Units: SI and Imperial Systems

Area is two dimensions and volume is three, so a linear conversion factor is squared for square units and cubed for cubic units. Today: see why 1\text{ m}^2 is 10\,000\text{ cm}^2 (not 100), convert areas and volumes in metric and imperial units, and compare objects measured in different systems.

I can…
  • build a square-unit relationship from a unit square (1\text{ m}^2=100^2=10\,000\text{ cm}^2) and a cubic-unit relationship from a unit cube (1\text{ m}^3=100^3=1\,000\,000\text{ cm}^3)
  • convert metric areas and volumes with the metric number line: n steps apart means 10^{2n} for area and 10^{3n} for volume
  • convert imperial areas and volumes (\text{ft}^2\leftrightarrow\text{in}^2, \text{yd}^3\leftrightarrow\text{ft}^3, …) by unit analysis
  • convert two measurements to a common unit so I can compare an SI object with an imperial one
M2 Apply proportional reasoning to problems that involve conversions between SI and imperial units of measure.
M3 Solve problems, using SI and imperial units, that involve the surface area and volume of 3-D objects, including: right cones; right cylinders; right prisms; right pyramids; spheres.

Converting Units of Area in the Metric System

A length conversion has one dimension, so the linear factor is applied once. An area is a product of two lengths, so both lengths are scaled and the factor is applied twice: the area conversion factor is the square of the linear one. Since 1\text{ m}=100\text{ cm}, a 1 m square is a 100 cm by 100 cm square, so 1\text{ m}^2=100^2=10\,000\text{ cm}^2, not 100\text{ cm}^2.

Why the factor squares. Each side of the square holds 100 cm, so the square holds 10\times10=100 cells (one is shaded), each 10 cm by 10 cm. One cell is 10\times10=100\text{ cm}^2, so the whole square is 100\times100=10\,000\text{ cm}^2. Counting in centimetre squares instead of ten-centimetre cells gives the same total: 100 columns \times\ 100 rows =10\,000 squares of 1\text{ cm}^2.
Metric shortcut. If two prefixes are n steps apart on the metric number line, the length factor is 10^{n}, the area factor is 10^{2n}, and the volume factor is 10^{3n}.
Millimetres to metres is 3 steps, so 1\text{ m}^2=10^{6}\text{ mm}^2 and 1\text{ m}^3=10^{9}\text{ mm}^3.
Example 1

Building the square-unit relationship from a unit square

(a) State the area of the square in \text{m}^2.
A=1\text{ m}\times1\text{ m}=\mathbf{1}\text{ m}^2
(b) Convert the side length of the square to cm, and then to mm. Use these converted side lengths to calculate the area of the same square in \text{cm}^2 and in \text{mm}^2.
1\text{ m}=100\text{ cm} and 1\text{ m}=1000\text{ mm}.
In cm: A=100\times100=\mathbf{10\,000}\text{ cm}^2.
In mm: A=1000\times1000=\mathbf{1\,000\,000}\text{ mm}^2.
(c) Complete the statement:
1\text{ m}^2= 10\,000 \text{cm}^2= 1\,000\,000 \text{mm}^2.
The linear factors are 100 and 1000; squaring them gives 100^2=\mathbf{10\,000} and 1000^2=\mathbf{1\,000\,000}.
(d) Explain why converting \text{km}^2 to \text{m}^2 requires multiplying by 1000^2=1\,000\,000, rather than by 1000.
Area is length \times width. Going from km to m multiplies the length by 1000 and the width by 1000, so the product is multiplied by 1000\times1000=1000^2=1\,000\,000. Multiplying by 1000 only once would scale a single dimension, which converts a length, not an area.
Example 2

Applying the square-unit ratio to a rectangle

A rectangle measures 7 m by 4 m.

(a) Calculate the area of the rectangle in \text{m}^2.
A=7\times4=\mathbf{28}\text{ m}^2
(b) Convert both side lengths to cm and calculate the area in \text{cm}^2. Confirm your answer using the ratio from Example 1.
Sides: 700 cm and 400 cm.
A=700\times400=\mathbf{280\,000}\text{ cm}^2
Check: 28\times10\,000=280\,000\text{ cm}^2 ✓
(c) Convert both side lengths to mm and calculate the area in \text{mm}^2. Confirm your answer using the ratio from Example 1.
Sides: 7000 mm and 4000 mm.
A=7000\times4000=\mathbf{28\,000\,000}\text{ mm}^2
Check: 28\times1\,000\,000=28\,000\,000\text{ mm}^2 ✓
Think A classmate writes 28\times100=2800\text{ cm}^2. What did they forget?
They scaled only one side. 2800\text{ cm}^2 is a strip 700 cm long and only 4 cm wide. Both sides become 100 times longer, so the area is 100^2=10\,000 times bigger.

Converting Units of Volume in the Metric System

A volume is a product of three lengths, so all three are scaled and the linear factor is applied three times: the volume conversion factor is the cube of the linear one. A 1 m cube is a 100 cm by 100 cm by 100 cm cube, so 1\text{ m}^3=100^3=1\,000\,000\text{ cm}^3.

Why the factor cubes. The cube is 10 blocks long, 10 blocks wide and 10 blocks deep, so it holds 10\times10\times10=1000 blocks. Each block is a 10 cm cube, with volume 10\times10\times10=1000\text{ cm}^3, so the whole cube is 1000\times1000=1\,000\,000\text{ cm}^3. Counting in centimetre cubes directly gives the same total: 100\times100\times100=10^{6} cubes of 1\text{ cm}^3.
!
The classic error is to apply the linear factor once and write 1\text{ m}^3=100\text{ cm}^3. A 100\text{ cm}^3 volume is a cube barely 4.6 cm on an edge: nothing like a cubic metre.
How big is 100\text{ cm}^3? Its edge is \sqrt[3]{100}: 32nd^100ENTER gives about 4.64 cm.
Example 4

Building the cubic-unit relationship from a unit cube

(a) State the volume of the cube in \text{m}^3.
V=1\times1\times1=\mathbf{1}\text{ m}^3
(b) Convert each edge of the cube to cm. Calculate the volume of the cube in \text{cm}^3.
Each edge =100\text{ cm}.
V=100\times100\times100=100^3=\mathbf{1\,000\,000}\text{ cm}^3
(c) Complete the statement: 1\text{ m}^3= 1\,000\,000 \text{cm}^3. Then, by reasoning about the conversion factor (without drawing a new diagram), determine 1\text{ m}^3 in \text{mm}^3.
1\text{ m}=1000\text{ mm}, so each of the three edges scales by 1000 and the volume scales by 1000^3=10^{9}.
1\text{ m}^3=\mathbf{1\,000\,000\,000}\text{ mm}^3\ (10^{9}\text{ mm}^3)
Example 5

Applying the cubic-unit ratio to a rectangular prism

A rectangular prism measures 5 m by 2 m by 3 m.

(a) Calculate the volume of the prism in \text{m}^3.
V=5\times2\times3=\mathbf{30}\text{ m}^3
(b) Convert all three edge lengths to cm and calculate the volume in \text{cm}^3. Confirm your answer using the ratio from Example 4.
Edges: 500 cm, 200 cm and 300 cm. V=500\times200\times300=\mathbf{30\,000\,000}\text{ cm}^3.
Check with the ratio 1\text{ m}^3=1\,000\,000\text{ cm}^3: 30\times1\,000\,000=30\,000\,000\text{ cm}^3 ✓

Example 7

Building 1\text{ ft}^2 and 1\text{ ft}^3 from 1\text{ ft}=12\text{ in}

(a) Convert the side length of the square to inches and calculate the area of the square in \text{in}^2. State the relationship 1\text{ ft}^2= 144 \text{in}^2.
Side =12\text{ in}, so A=12\times12=12^2=\mathbf{144}\text{ in}^2.
1\text{ ft}^2=\mathbf{144}\text{ in}^2: the linear factor 12, squared.
(b) Convert each edge of the cube to inches and calculate the volume of the cube in \text{in}^3. State the relationship 1\text{ ft}^3= 1728 \text{in}^3.
Each edge =12\text{ in}, so V=12\times12\times12=12^3=\mathbf{1728}\text{ in}^3.
1\text{ ft}^3=\mathbf{1728}\text{ in}^3: the linear factor 12, cubed.
Squares and cubes. 12x²ENTER gives 144, and 12^3ENTER gives 1728 (the TI-30XIIS has no cube key, so cube with ^3).

Imperial area and volume conversions by unit analysis

Convert each of the following.

(a) 8\text{ yd}^2 to \text{ft}^2
\dfrac{8\text{ yd}^2}{1}\times\dfrac{9\text{ ft}^2}{1\text{ yd}^2} (\text{yd}^2 cancels)

Applying These Conversions to Real Problems

When a problem gives measurements in two different systems, converting both to a common unit allows a direct comparison. Two questions decide every conversion: what kind of unit is it (length, area or volume), and is it going to a smaller or a bigger unit?

Example 9

Comparing a metric bin with an imperial bin

A metric storage bin measures 1.2 m by 0.9 m by 0.7 m. An imperial storage bin measures 4 ft by 3 ft by 2.5 ft. Use 2.54\text{ cm}=1\text{ in}, so 1\text{ ft}=30.48\text{ cm}.

(a) Determine, to the nearest \text{cm}^3, which bin has the greater volume, and by how much.
Bin A: V=1.2\times0.9\times0.7=0.756\text{ m}^3.
1\text{ m}^3=100^3=10^{6}\text{ cm}^3, so V=756\,000\text{ cm}^3.
Bin B: 1\text{ ft}^3=30.48^3=28\,316.846\,592\text{ cm}^3.
V=4\times3\times2.5=30\text{ ft}^3, so
V=30\times28\,316.846\,592=849\,505.4\approx\mathbf{849\,505}\text{ cm}^3.
Bin B is greater, by 849\,505-756\,000=\mathbf{93\,505}\text{ cm}^3.
(b) Determine, to the nearest \text{in}^2, which bin has the greater surface area, and by how much.
Bin A: SA=2(lw+lh+wh)
=2[(1.2)(0.9)+(1.2)(0.7)+(0.9)(0.7)]
=2(1.08+0.84+0.63)=5.10\text{ m}^2.
1\text{ m}^2\approx1550.003\,1\text{ in}^2, so SA=5.10\times1550.003\,1=7905.02\approx\mathbf{7905}\text{ in}^2.
Bin B: SA=2[(4)(3)+(4)(2.5)+(3)(2.5)]=2(29.5)=59\text{ ft}^2. Since 1\text{ ft}^2=144\text{ in}^2, SA=59\times144=\mathbf{8496}\text{ in}^2.
Bin B is greater, by 8496-7905=\mathbf{591}\text{ in}^2.
Keep full precision until the end. Cube 30.48 on the calculator instead of rounding it: 30×30.48^3ENTER gives 849 505.3978. For the surface area, put the three face areas in brackets.
✓

Key ideas

Square it for area, cube it for volume. Area has two dimensions and volume has three, so the linear factor is used twice or three times: 1\text{ m}^2=100^2=10\,000\text{ cm}^2 and 1\text{ m}^3=100^3=1\,000\,000\text{ cm}^3. Never just “×100”.
Metric shortcut. n steps apart on the metric number line: length 10^{n}, area 10^{2n}, volume 10^{3n}. To a smaller unit, multiply; to a bigger unit, divide.
Imperial. 1\text{ ft}^2=144\text{ in}^2, 1\text{ ft}^3=1728\text{ in}^3, 1\text{ yd}^2=9\text{ ft}^2, 1\text{ yd}^3=27\text{ ft}^3. In unit analysis, put the unit to cancel on the bottom of the conversion fraction.
Compare in one unit. Convert both objects to a common unit before comparing. Across systems use 1\text{ in}=2.54\text{ cm} (so 1\text{ ft}=30.48\text{ cm}), squared or cubed, and round only at the end.
