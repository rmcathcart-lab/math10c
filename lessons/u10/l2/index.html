Converting Units Within the Metric (SI) System

SI prefixes are powers of ten, so a metric conversion is a shift along a number line, a proportion, or a chain of unit-analysis fractions. Today: all three methods, and when each one shines.

I can…
  • read the metric prefix chart from kilo to milli and say what each prefix is worth
  • convert by counting steps on the metric number line and moving the decimal point one place per step
  • set up and solve a proportion two correct ways, including scales, recipes and 1:n map scales
  • convert with unit analysis, choosing each conversion factor so the unwanted unit cancels, even for rates like m/s
  • Extension use giga, mega, micro and nano, and give answers in scientific notation

!
Two different “m”s. On its own, m is the metre. In front of another unit, m means milli (a thousandth): mm, mg, mL. And capital M is mega, a million.

Method 1: Using the Metric Number Line

Arranging the prefixes from largest to smallest produces a metric number line on which each single step is one power of ten. Moving right (toward smaller prefixes) multiplies by 10 per step; moving left (toward larger prefixes) divides by 10 per step.

Kilometres to millimetres is 6 positions right (k is 3 steps left of the unit and m is 3 steps right of it), so 1\text{ km}=10^{6}\text{ mm}=1\,000\,000\text{ mm}; the reverse trip is 6 positions left, so 1\text{ mm}=10^{-6}\text{ km}.

Count steps, move the point. Each step on the line moves the decimal point one place, in the same direction you moved on the line. Right: the number gets bigger (smaller units, so you need more of them). Left: the number gets smaller.
A whole number’s decimal point is hiding at its end: 58\,400=58\,400. Fill any empty places with zeros.
Example 1

Reading a conversion off the number line

Complete each conversion. Write each answer first as a power of 10, then in standard notation.

(a) 1\text{ m}= 10^{2} cm = 100 cm
Unit \to c is 2 steps right, so multiply by 10^2: 1\text{ m}=10^{2}\text{ cm}=\boldsymbol{100}\text{ cm}
(b) 1\text{ cm}= 10^{-2} m = 0.01 m
c \to Unit is 2 steps left, so divide by 10^2: 1\text{ cm}=10^{-2}\text{ m}=\boldsymbol{0.01}\text{ m}
(c) 1\text{ kL}= 10^{3} L = 1000 L
k \to Unit is 3 steps right, so multiply by 10^3: 1\text{ kL}=10^{3}\text{ L}=\boldsymbol{1000}\text{ L}
Example 2

Counting steps to convert a measurement

Use the metric number line to convert each measure.

(a) 4.6\text{ m} to cm
m \to cm is 2 steps right: \times10^{2}.
4.6\times100=\boldsymbol{460}\text{ cm}
(b) 58\,400\text{ mg} to kg
mg \to kg is 6 steps left: \div10^{6}.
58\,400\div1\,000\,000=\boldsymbol{0.0584}\text{ kg}
(c) Extension 215 micrometres to nanometres
\mu\text{m}\to\text{nm} is 3 steps right: \times10^{3}.
215\times1000=\boldsymbol{215\,000}\text{ nm}

Large Step Counts and Scientific Notation

Extension

With the Extension prefixes a conversion can be 6, 8 or even 18 steps long. Instead of hopping one place at a time, count the steps and multiply by that power of ten all at once. Then tidy the answer into scientific notation, a\times10^{n} with 1\le a<10.

Method 2: Proportional Reasoning

A proportion is a statement that two ratios are equal. Given either a known conversion (such as 1\text{ m}=100\text{ cm}) or a ratio supplied by the question, set up

\dfrac{a}{b}=\dfrac{c}{d}

with matching units in matching positions, then cross-multiply to isolate the unknown. Scale drawings, map scales, recipes and currency exchange rates are all ratios, so each can be solved with a proportion.

Example 4

Two correct arrangements of the same proportion

Sam and Priya each convert 450\text{ cm} into metres, using 1\text{ m}=100\text{ cm}. Complete both students’ work.

Sam (cross multiplication)
\dfrac{1\text{ m}}{100\text{ cm}}=\dfrac{x}{450\text{ cm}}
(1\text{ m})(450\text{ cm})=(x)(100\text{ cm})
x= 4.5\text{ m}
x=\dfrac{(1\text{ m})(450\text{ cm})}{100\text{ cm}}=\dfrac{450}{100}=\boldsymbol{4.5}\text{ m} (cm cancels).
Priya (table of headers)

\dfrac{1}{x}=\dfrac{100}{450}
x= 4.5\text{ m}
100x=450, so x=\dfrac{450}{100}=\boldsymbol{4.5}\text{ m}.

Both methods agree: 450\text{ cm}= 4.5 m.

Ratios supplied by the question: scales and recipes

A scale (“1 cm represents 2.4 m”) or a recipe (“240 mL of yogurt to 560 mL of juice”) is a ratio that must stay the same. Whatever you do to one quantity, you do to the other.

Examples 5 and 6

Proportions from a scale and from a recipe ratio

5. A photograph of a blue whale uses a scale in which 1 cm on the photograph represents 2.4 m of actual length. The whale’s tail flukes measure 2.5 cm across in the photograph. Use proportional reasoning to determine the actual width of the tail flukes. Is your answer reasonable?
\dfrac{1\text{ cm}}{2.4\text{ m}}=\dfrac{2.5\text{ cm}}{x}
Cross multiplying: (1)(x)=(2.5)(2.4)
x=\boldsymbol{6}\text{ m}. The flukes are 6 m across: reasonable, since a blue whale’s tail flukes span roughly 6 to 7 m.
6. Dana’s smoothie recipe calls for 240 mL of yogurt and 560 mL of juice. Partway through she adds an extra 90 mL of yogurt. Use proportional reasoning to determine how much extra juice she should add to keep the same ratio.
Yogurt : juice =240:560=3:7 (divide by 80).
New yogurt: 240+90=330 mL. Then \dfrac{3}{7}=\dfrac{330}{j}, so 3j=7(330)=2310 and j=770 mL of juice in total.
Extra juice =770-560=\boldsymbol{210} mL.
Solve each proportion in one line. 5: 2.5×2.4ENTER gives 6. 6: 7×330÷3ENTER gives 770 mL in total; subtract the 560 mL already in.

Map scales written as a ratio, 1:n

Map scales. A map scale written as a ratio, 1:n, means 1 unit on the map stands for n of the same unit on the ground: on a 1:50\,000 map, 1 cm on the map is 50\,000\text{ cm}=500\text{ m} on the ground. Convert to a convenient unit only at the end.
Example 7

A map scale written as a ratio, 1:n

A map of central Alberta has a scale of 1:250\,000.

(a) Two towns are 18.4 cm apart on the map. How far apart are they on the ground, in kilometres?
\dfrac{1\text{ cm (map)}}{250\,000\text{ cm (ground)}}=\dfrac{18.4\text{ cm}}{x}
x=18.4\times250\,000=4\,600\,000\text{ cm}
4\,600\,000\text{ cm}\times\dfrac{1\text{ m}}{100\text{ cm}}\times\dfrac{1\text{ km}}{1000\text{ m}}=\boldsymbol{46}\text{ km}
(b) Two lakes are 32 km apart. How far apart are they on the map, in centimetres?
Same unit on both sides first: 32\text{ km}=32\times1000\times100=3\,200\,000\text{ cm}.
\dfrac{1}{250\,000}=\dfrac{x}{3\,200\,000}
x=\dfrac{3\,200\,000}{250\,000}=\boldsymbol{12.8}\text{ cm}
Big zeros: count them as you type. (a): 18.4×250000ENTER gives 4 600 000 cm, then ÷ 100 ÷ 1000 gives 46 km.

Method 3: Unit Analysis (Dimensional Analysis)

Proportional reasoning suits a single conversion. When several conversions must be chained, unit analysis (also called dimensional analysis) is usually faster, and it is the method used in science courses. The set-up is

\text{required}=(\text{given})\times(\text{conversion}),

with each conversion written as a fraction arranged so the unwanted unit cancels. A conversion fraction such as \dfrac{1000\text{ m}}{1\text{ km}} has the same length on top and bottom, so it is just a fancy way to write 1.

For example, to convert 640 mL to L using 1\text{ L}=1000\text{ mL},

\dfrac{640\text{ mL}}{1}\times\dfrac{1\text{ L}}{1000\text{ mL}}=\dfrac{640}{1000}\text{ L}=0.64\text{ L}\qquad(\text{mL cancels, leaving L}).
!
The unit you don’t want goes on the bottom. If a unit appears on top and on the bottom it cancels. If your answer’s units come out as something strange, like \dfrac{\text{km}^2}{\text{m}}, a conversion fraction is upside down: flip it.
Example 8

One conversion factor

Convert each measurement using unit analysis.

(a) 9 kilometres = 9000 m
\dfrac{9\text{ km}}{1}\times\dfrac{1000\text{ m}}{1\text{ km}}: km cancels, leaving m.
=9\times1000=\boldsymbol{9000}\text{ m}
(b) Extension 3140 nanometres = 3.14\times10^{-6} m
\dfrac{3140\text{ nm}}{1}\times\dfrac{1\text{ m}}{10^{9}\text{ nm}}: nm cancels, leaving m.
=\dfrac{3140}{10^{9}}=\boldsymbol{3.14\times10^{-6}}\text{ m}

Converting a rate: two units change at once

A rate like 64 m/s already is a fraction: metres on top, seconds on the bottom. Each unit that changes needs its own conversion fraction, and each one is arranged to cancel where that unit sits.

Example 10

Converting a rate (two units change at once)

Priya needs to convert 64 m/s to km/min.

(a) What conversions will Priya need in order to solve this problem?
1\text{ km}=1000\text{ m} for the distance unit, and 1\text{ min}=60\text{ s} for the time unit.
(b) Complete Priya’s work below, showing the cancellation of units.
\dfrac{64\text{ m}}{\text{s}}\times\dfrac{\phantom{00}\text{km}}{\phantom{00}\text{m}}\times\dfrac{\phantom{00}\text{s}}{\phantom{00}\text{min}}=
\dfrac{64\text{ m}}{\text{s}}\times\dfrac{1\text{ km}}{1000\text{ m}}\times\dfrac{60\text{ s}}{1\text{ min}}
m cancels and s cancels, leaving km/min.
=\dfrac{64\times60}{1000}=\dfrac{3840}{1000}=\boldsymbol{3.84}\text{ km/min}
Tops multiply, bottoms divide. 64×60÷1000ENTER gives 3.84.

Which Method Should I Use?

All three methods give the same answer for any metric conversion. Each one is fastest in a different situation.

✓

Key ideas

Prefixes are powers of ten. kilo 10^3, hecto 10^2, deca 10^1, base, deci 10^{-1}, centi 10^{-2}, milli 10^{-3} on a metre, gram or litre. Ext giga, mega, micro, nano jump by 1000.
Number line: count the steps from the given unit to the required unit. Each step moves the decimal point one place, the same way you moved: right \times10, left \div10.
Proportion: \frac{a}{b}=\frac{c}{d} with matching units in matching positions, then cross-multiply. Scales, recipes and 1:n maps are ratios that must stay the same.
Unit analysis: required = given \times conversion fractions, each arranged so the unwanted unit is on the opposite level and cancels. Chains and rates (m/s to km/min) are its specialty.
