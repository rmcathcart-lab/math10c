Measurement Unit Review

No new formulas today: every skill of the unit, assembled into one set of problems. Convert first, choose the right referent, unit or instrument, pick the right formula, carry full precision, and round once, at the end.

I can…
  • convert within SI by moving along the prefix number line, and check the direction (a smaller unit gives a bigger number)
  • choose a sensible unit, instrument or referent, and calculate with a referent
  • use an approximate conversion and a map scale, and convert between imperial and SI in applied problems
  • find the surface area and volume of prisms, cylinders, pyramids, cones, spheres and hemispheres, and work backwards to a missing dimension
  • chain several steps (a volume, trucks, a new tank, a cost comparison) at full precision
  • read a vernier caliper and a micrometer
  • Extension convert with micro- and mega-prefixes

Converting Within the SI System

From kilo through milli (kilo, hecto, deca, unit, deci, centi, milli) each step along the SI prefix ladder changes a measurement by a factor of 10; the Extension steps milli → micro → nano are each a factor of 1000, as on the Lesson 2 number line. Either way, every metric conversion is a multiplication or division by a power of ten.
!
Check the direction every time. Converting to a smaller unit must give a larger number (and to a larger unit, a smaller number). And put every length in the same unit before you add or subtract.
Example 1

Metric conversions, and judging a set of statements

(a) A rectangular banner measures 54 cm in height and 1.46 m in length. Determine the difference between these two measurements, in millimetres.
Put both lengths in millimetres first.
1.46\text{ m}=1.46\times1000=1460\text{ mm};   54\text{ cm}=54\times10=540\text{ mm}.
Difference =1460-540=\mathbf{920\ mm}.
(b) (Extension) Consider the following four statements. How many of them are false?
Statement 1: 1\text{ cm}=0.01\text{ m}
Statement 2: 1\text{ mm}=0.001\text{ m}
Statement 3: 1\ \mu\text{m}=0.01\text{ mm}
Statement 4: 1\text{ km}=1000\text{ m}
Statement 1: 1\text{ cm}=10^{-2}\text{ m}=0.01\text{ m}: true.   Statement 2: 1\text{ mm}=10^{-3}\text{ m}=0.001\text{ m}: true.
Statement 3: 1\ \mu\text{m}=10^{-6}\text{ m} and 1\text{ mm}=10^{-3}\text{ m}, so 1\ \mu\text{m}=0.001\text{ mm}, not 0.01\text{ mm}: false.
Statement 4: 1\text{ km}=10^{3}\text{ m}=1000\text{ m}: true.   Exactly one statement is false.
Same unit first, then subtract. 1.46×1000−54×10ENTER gives 920 mm.

Choosing Referents, Units, and Instruments

Choosing a unit means matching the size of the unit to the size of the object; choosing an instrument means matching the precision the job needs to a tool that can deliver it. A referent is a familiar object (often a part of the body) whose size is known, used to estimate a measurement with no tool in hand.
Example 2

Units, instruments, and a referent-based calculation

(a) State the most suitable imperial unit for measuring each of the following.
the diameter of a coin: inches
the length of a soccer field: yards
the driving distance between two towns: miles
coin: inches   soccer field: yards   driving distance: miles. Each unit is chosen so the measurement is a convenient-sized number: a coin is about 1 in across, a field about 100 yd long, a drive tens of miles.
(b) State the measuring instrument that would be easiest to use for each of the following.
the thickness of a credit card: micrometer
the width of a doorway: tape measure
the length of a running track: trundle wheel
credit card: micrometer (well under a millimetre, and 0.01 mm precision is needed)   doorway: tape measure   running track: trundle wheel (a long distance, walked out).
(c) The height of a horse is measured in hands. Using the width of an adult hand as a referent for 4 inches, determine the height, in feet, of a horse measuring 14\tfrac14 hands.
Each hand is the 4 in referent, so 14\tfrac14\text{ hands}=14.25\times4=57\text{ in}.
1\text{ ft}=12\text{ in}, so 57\text{ in}\times\dfrac{1\text{ ft}}{12\text{ in}}=\dfrac{57}{12}=\mathbf{4\tfrac34\ feet} (inches cancel) =4\text{ ft }9\text{ in}.

Reading a Micrometer

A micrometer measures small lengths to a hundredth of a millimetre by combining two scales: a fixed sleeve scale, marked in whole and half millimetres, giving the coarse reading; and a rotating thimble scale, marked in hundredths of a millimetre, giving the fine reading. The two are added:
\text{reading}=(\text{sleeve scale value})+(\text{thimble scale value})
Example 3

Combining the sleeve and thimble readings

The micrometer below has a measuring range of 0–25 mm and is accurate to 0.01 mm. The sleeve has a mark every 0.5 mm; the thimble has divisions of 0.01 mm. Determine the sleeve (coarse) reading and the thimble (fine) reading separately, then combine them to state the full reading, in millimetres.

Sleeve reading 8.5 mm   +   thimble reading 0.27 mm   =   reading 8.77 mm
Sleeve (coarse) reading: the last fully visible sleeve mark left of the thimble is the 8.5 mm half-millimetre mark, so sleeve =8.5 mm.
Thimble (fine) reading: the index line is on thimble mark 27, so thimble =27\times0.01=0.27 mm.
Reading =8.5+0.27=\mathbf{8.77\ mm}, stated to the instrument’s own 0.01 mm precision.

Converting Between Systems, and Using Scale

An approximate conversion such as 5\text{ miles}=8\text{ km} (the Lesson 2 benchmark 1\text{ mi}\approx1.6\text{ km}) switches between the systems by a single ratio. A map scale written 1:n (Lesson 2, Example 2) means one unit of distance on the map represents n of the same unit in reality, so the map distance is the real distance divided by n, with both expressed in the same unit first.
Example 4

An approximate conversion and a map scale

(a) A section of highway is 1032 km long. Using the approximate conversion 5\text{ miles}=8\text{ km}, determine this distance in miles.
Unit analysis with the ratio 5\text{ mi}=8\text{ km}, arranged so km cancels:
1032\text{ km}\times\dfrac{5\text{ mi}}{8\text{ km}}=\dfrac{1032}{8}\times5=129\times5=\mathbf{645\ miles}.
Miles are longer than kilometres, so the number should be smaller. ✓
(b) The scale on a map is 1:1\,500\,000. If the actual distance between two towns is 540 km, determine the distance between them on the map, in centimetres.
Put the real distance in centimetres: 540\text{ km}=540\,000\text{ m}=54\,000\,000\text{ cm}.
A scale of 1:1\,500\,000 means 1 cm on the map for every 1\,500\,000 cm in reality, so divide:
map distance =\dfrac{54\,000\,000}{1\,500\,000}=\mathbf{36\ cm}.
(a) 1032×5÷8ENTER gives 645. (b) Big numbers are easy to mistype; EE keeps the zeros straight: 542ndEE6÷1.52ndEE6ENTER gives 36.

Applied Conversion Problems

Most applied problems give measurements in mixed units. Convert every quantity to the same unit before any arithmetic is attempted.
Example 5

Tiling a floor, and dosing a tank

(a) A rectangular hallway floor is 7 yards long and 3\tfrac12 yards wide. The tiles chosen to cover it are 10\tfrac12 inches by 10\tfrac12 inches. Determine the number of tiles needed.
Convert the floor to inches, the unit the tiles are given in: 1\text{ yd}=3\text{ ft}=36\text{ in}.
Length: 7\times36=252 in.   Width: 3\tfrac12\times36=126 in.
Tiles along the length: 252\div10.5=24.   Along the width: 126\div10.5=12.
Total =24\times12=\mathbf{288\ tiles}.
(b) An aquarium has dimensions 55 cm by 40 cm by 25 cm. The directions on a bottle of fish medicine are to add 4 drops per 10 litres of water. Determine the number of drops needed to completely fill the aquarium with treated water.
Volume =55\times40\times25=55\,000\text{ cm}^3.
1\text{ cm}^3=1\text{ mL}, so 55\,000\text{ cm}^3=55\,000\text{ mL}=55\text{ L}.
At 4 drops per 10 L: \dfrac{55}{10}\times4=5.5\times4=\mathbf{22\ drops}.
(a) Tiles along each side, multiplied: (7×36÷10.5)×(3.5×36÷10.5)ENTER gives 288. (b) 55×40×25÷1000ENTER gives 55 L; then 55÷10×4ENTER gives 22.

Comparing Imperial and Metric Measurements

Two lengths given in different systems can be compared without a full conversion of both, as long as each is placed on the same number line. Lesson 2’s bridge, 1\text{ cm}=0.3937\text{ in}, does it in either direction: 1\text{ mm}=0.03937\text{ in}, and 1\text{ in}=2.54\text{ cm}=25.4\text{ mm}.

\tfrac14=0.250 (too small), \tfrac{9}{32}=0.281 (too small), \tfrac38=0.375 (fits), \tfrac{7}{16}=0.438 (too large).

Every formula of Lessons 3, 4 and 5 is collected below. Throughout, h is the perpendicular height, s is the slant height, A_{\text{Base}} is the area of the base, and r is the radius: halve a diameter before substituting it.

Prismsum of all faces =2A_{\text{Base}}+(\text{perimeter})hV=A_{\text{Base}}\times h
CylinderSA=2\pi r^{2}+2\pi rh=2\pi r(r+h)V=\pi r^{2}h

The Measurement You Need First

A cone’s surface area needs the slant height s, not the perpendicular height h: find it with the Pythagorean theorem on the cone’s cross-section. A sphere’s formulas need the radius: halve the diameter first.

Example 7

A cone (slant height first) and a sphere (radius first)

(a) A conical paperweight has a base radius of 8 cm and a height of 15 cm. Determine the surface area of the paperweight, in terms of \pi.
Surface area of a cone needs the slant height s, not the perpendicular height h:
s^{2}=h^{2}+r^{2}=15^{2}+8^{2}=225+64=289, so s=\sqrt{289}=17 cm (an 8–15–17 triangle).
SA=\pi r^{2}+\pi rs=\pi(8)^{2}+\pi(8)(17)=64\pi+136\pi=\mathbf{200\pi\ cm^{2}}.
(b) A spherical balloon has a diameter of 18 inches. Determine its surface area, to the nearest square inch.
A sphere’s surface area needs the radius: r=\dfrac{18}{2}=9 in.
SA=4\pi r^{2}=4\pi(9)^{2}=4\pi(81)=324\pi=1017.876\ldots
SA\approx\mathbf{1018\ in^{2}}. (Using the diameter by mistake would give four times too much.)
(a) The slant height: √15x²+8x²)ENTER gives 17. (b) Use the π key, never 3.14: 4×π×9x²ENTER gives 1017.876…

Volume of 3-D Solids: A Review

A pyramid or cone holds one third of the prism or cylinder on the same base, so V=\tfrac13 A_{\text{Base}}h uses the perpendicular height, never the slant height. A volume formula can also be rearranged and solved for a missing dimension: V=\tfrac23\pi r^{3} gives r=\sqrt[3]{\dfrac{3V}{2\pi}}.
Example 8

A pyramid’s volume, and a hemisphere’s radius from its volume

(a) A sculpture is shaped like a triangular pyramid. Its base is an equilateral triangle with sides of 8 m, and the pyramid’s height is 15 m. Determine the volume of the sculpture, as an exact value in simplest radical form and to one decimal place.
The base is an equilateral triangle of side 8 m, so A_{\text{Base}}=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{4}(8)^{2}=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{4}(64)=16\sqrt3\ \text{m}^2.
V=\tfrac13 A_{\text{Base}}h=\tfrac13(16\sqrt3)(15)=\mathbf{80\sqrt3\ m^{3}} (exact).
80\sqrt3=80(1.7320508\ldots)=138.564\ldots\approx\mathbf{138.6\ m^{3}}.
(b) A hemispherical storage dome has a volume of 1526.8\ \text{m}^{3}. Determine the radius of the dome, to the nearest tenth of a metre.
Solve V=\tfrac23\pi r^{3} for r: r^{3}=\dfrac{3V}{2\pi}, so r=\sqrt[3]{\dfrac{3V}{2\pi}}.
r^{3}=\dfrac{3(1526.8)}{2\pi}=\dfrac{4580.4}{6.283185\ldots}=728.99\ldots
r=\sqrt[3]{728.99\ldots}=8.99997\ldots\approx\mathbf{9.0\ m}.

Cumulative Applied Problem

One problem can chain the volume of a cone, the volume of a cylinder, a volume formula solved for a missing dimension, and a cost comparison that needs both a unit conversion and a currency conversion. As everywhere in this unit, carry each value forward at full calculator precision and round only the answer a part asks for.
Example 9

A conical tank, tanker trucks, a second tank, and two bids

A specialty fertilizer concentrate is stored in a conical tank in Calgary whose diameter is three times its height. The height of the tank is 5.4 m.

(a) Determine the volume of concentrate the tank holds, correct to one decimal place.
The diameter is three times the height: d=3(5.4)=16.2 m, so r=\dfrac{16.2}{2}=8.1 m.
V=\tfrac13\pi r^{2}h=\tfrac13\pi(8.1)^{2}(5.4)=\tfrac13\pi(65.61)(5.4)=\tfrac13\pi(354.294)=118.098\pi
V=371.0158\ldots\approx\mathbf{371.0\ m^{3}}.
(b) The concentrate is transferred using cylindrical tanker trucks, each with a radius of 1.8 m and a length of 9 m. Using the volume from part (a), determine the number of trucks needed.
Each truck is a cylinder lying on its side, so its length is the height of the cylinder:
V_{\text{truck}}=\pi r^{2}h=\pi(1.8)^{2}(9)=\pi(3.24)(9)=29.16\pi\approx91.6088\ \text{m}^3.
Number of trucks =\dfrac{118.098\pi}{29.16\pi}=4.05 (the unrounded volume from (a); \pi cancels, so this is exact).
Four trucks would leave concentrate behind, so 5 trucks are needed (always round up).
(c) At the distributor, the concentrate is poured into a new conical tank with a circular base of diameter 12 m. Using the total volume from part (a), determine the height of this tank, to one decimal place.
The new cone has r=\dfrac{12}{2}=6 m, and holds the same 118.098\pi\ \text{m}^3 (the unrounded volume from (a)).
\tfrac13\pi(6)^{2}h=118.098\pi\ \Rightarrow\ 12\pi h=118.098\pi\ \Rightarrow\ h=\dfrac{118.098}{12}
h=9.8415\approx\mathbf{9.8\ m}.
(d) Two shipping companies bid on the transport job. Prairie Freight charges C$150 per truck plus C$4.80 per \text{m}^{3} of concentrate. Northern Cargo charges US$110 per truck plus US$0.12 per \text{ft}^{3} of concentrate. Using C$1 = US$0.92 and 2.54 cm = 1 inch, and using the volume and number of trucks from parts (a) and (b), determine which company is cheaper.
Prairie Freight (already in C$): 150(5)+4.80(371.0158\ldots)=750+1780.8758\ldots=\mathbf{C\$2530.88}.
Northern Cargo charges per \text{ft}^3, so convert the volume first. 1\text{ in}=2.54\text{ cm} gives 1\text{ ft}=12(2.54)=30.48\text{ cm}=0.3048\text{ m}, and a cubic conversion cubes that factor: 1\text{ m}^{3}=\left(\dfrac{1}{0.3048}\right)^{3}=35.3146\ldots\ \text{ft}^{3}, so 371.0158\ldots\times35.3146\ldots=13\,102.2996\ldots\ \text{ft}^{3}.
Cost =110(5)+0.12(13\,102.2996\ldots)=550+1572.2759\ldots=\text{US\$}2122.2759\ldots
Converting to Canadian dollars (\div0.92): \dfrac{2122.2759\ldots}{0.92}\approx\mathbf{C\$2306.82}.
Since C$2306.82 < C$2530.88, Northern Cargo is cheaper, by about C$224.05. (Full precision carried throughout; each dollar amount rounded once.)

21. Written Response

Golden Plains Dairy stores fresh milk in a cylindrical vat with a radius of 2.2 m and a height of 4.5 m, filled completely.

(a) Determine the volume of milk in the vat, correct to one decimal place.
V=\pi r^{2}h=\pi(2.2)^{2}(4.5)=\pi(4.84)(4.5)=21.78\pi=68.4238\ldots\approx\mathbf{68.4\ m^{3}}.
(b) The milk is transported to a processing plant using rectangular tanker trucks with interior dimensions 2.5 m by 2 m by 8 m. Using the volume from (a), determine the number of trucks needed.
Each truck is a rectangular prism: V_{\text{truck}}=2.5\times2\times8=40\ \text{m}^3. \dfrac{68.4238\ldots}{40}=1.7106\ldots (the unrounded volume from (a)), and a part-load still needs a whole truck, so 2 trucks.
(c) At the plant, the milk is poured into a hemispherical storage tank with a diameter of 10 m. Using the total volume from (a), determine what percentage of the hemispherical tank’s capacity is filled, to the nearest tenth of a percent.
The tank’s radius is 5 m, so its capacity is V_{\text{hemi}}=\tfrac23\pi r^{3}=\tfrac23\pi(125)=\tfrac{250}{3}\pi=261.7993\ldots\ \text{m}^3.
Percentage =\dfrac{21.78\pi}{\tfrac{250}{3}\pi}\times100\%=\dfrac{65.34}{250}\times100\%=26.136\%\approx\mathbf{26.1\%} (\pi cancels, so no rounding enters at all).
(d) Two trucking companies bid for the job. Foothills Transport charges C$200 per truck plus C$3.50 per \text{m}^{3} of milk. Rocky Mountain Haulers charges US$150 per truck plus US$0.10 per \text{ft}^{3} of milk. Using C$1 = US$0.93 and 2.54 cm = 1 inch, and using the volume and number of trucks from (a) and (b), determine which company is cheaper.
Foothills Transport: 200(2)+3.50(68.4238\ldots)=400+239.4836\ldots=\mathbf{C\$639.48}.
Rocky Mountain Haulers charges per \text{ft}^3: 1\text{ m}^{3}=\left(\tfrac{1}{0.3048}\right)^{3}=35.3146\ldots\ \text{ft}^{3}, so 68.4238\ldots\times35.3146\ldots=2416.3668\ldots\ \text{ft}^{3}. Cost =150(2)+0.10(2416.3668\ldots)=300+241.6366\ldots=\text{US\$}541.6366\ldots; in Canadian dollars, \dfrac{541.6366\ldots}{0.93}\approx\mathbf{C\$582.41}.
Since C$582.41 < C$639.48, Rocky Mountain Haulers is cheaper, by about C$57.08.

23. (Written Response) A chocolate bar is a right triangular prism. Its triangular ends are right triangles with legs of 6 cm and 8 cm and a hypotenuse of 10 cm, and the bar is 15 cm long.
(a) Determine the volume of the bar.
For a prism, V=A_{\text{Base}}\times h, where the base is the triangular end and h is the 15 cm length. A_{\text{Base}}=\tfrac12(6)(8)=24\ \text{cm}^2; V=24\times15=\mathbf{360\ cm^{3}}.
(b) Determine the surface area of the foil wrapper that covers the bar exactly.
Two triangular ends plus three rectangles. Ends: 2(24)=48\ \text{cm}^2. The three rectangles together form one rectangle of width (perimeter of the triangle) and length 15 cm: (6+8+10)(15)=360\ \text{cm}^2. SA=48+360=\mathbf{408\ cm^{2}}: an area, so cm², not cm³.
(c) Melted, the chocolate is poured into a measuring cup. State its volume in millilitres and in litres.
1\text{ cm}^3=1\text{ mL}, so 360\text{ cm}^3=\mathbf{360\ mL}; 1000\text{ mL}=1\text{ L}, so 360\text{ mL}=\mathbf{0.36\ L}.
24. (Written Response) A monument is a square-based pyramid. Each side of the base is 10 cm and the pyramid’s perpendicular height is 12 cm.
(a) Determine the slant height s of a triangular face.
The slant height runs from the apex to the midpoint of a base edge, so its foot is half a side from the centre: 5 cm. s^2=12^2+5^2=144+25=169, so s=\mathbf{13\ cm}.
(b) Determine the total surface area of the monument, including its base.
Surface area uses the slant height s=13 cm. Base: 10\times10=100\ \text{cm}^2. Four triangular faces: 4\left(\tfrac12 bs\right)=4\left(\tfrac12\right)(10)(13)=260\ \text{cm}^2. SA=100+260=\mathbf{360\ cm^{2}}.
(c) Determine the volume of the monument.
Volume uses the perpendicular height h=12 cm. A_{\text{Base}}=100\ \text{cm}^2; V=\tfrac13 A_{\text{Base}}h=\tfrac13(100)(12)=\mathbf{400\ cm^{3}}.

Key ideas

Convert first. SI to SI: slide along the prefix line (\times10 per step to the right). Anything else: unit analysis with a conversion fraction that cancels the unwanted unit. Squared or cubed units: square or cube the factor. Smaller unit, bigger number.
Match the tool to the job. Units: a convenient-sized number (in, yd, mi). Instruments: the precision the job needs (micrometer, caliper, ruler, tape, trundle wheel). Referents: count how many fit, then convert.
Pick the formula from the shape. Flat top: V=A_{\text{Base}}h. Pointed: \tfrac13 of that, and s (not h) for surface area. Round: 4\pi r^2, \tfrac43\pi r^3. Always the radius.
Chain carefully. Carry full precision, round once at the end; round up for whole trucks or tiles; work backwards by undoing the formula (\sqrt{\ }, \sqrt[3]{\ }); compare costs in one currency.
