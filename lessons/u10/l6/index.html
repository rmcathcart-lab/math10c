Surface Area and Volume of Prisms and Cylinders

Every right prism and every right cylinder is a base pushed straight back. Its volume is the base area times the height (layers of the base, stacked up), and its surface area is the area of every face added together, read off the solid or off its net.

I can…
  • name the two congruent bases and the rectangular side faces of a right prism, and treat a right cylinder as a prism with a circular base
  • find the volume with V_{\text{Prism}}=A_{\text{Base}}\times h and V_{\text{Cylinder}}=\pi r^{2}h, and report it as a capacity too (1\ \text{cm}^3=1\ \text{mL})
  • find the surface area by adding the area of every face, reading each face off the net, including SA_{\text{Cylinder}}=2\pi r^{2}+2\pi rh
  • halve a diameter first, keep exact \pi, and round only the final answer, with the right units (\text{cm}^2 or \text{cm}^3)
  • find the curved surface only, and work backwards from a volume or a surface area to a missing side, length or height
M3 Solve problems, using SI and imperial units, that involve the surface area and volume of 3-D objects, including: right cones; right cylinders; right prisms; right pyramids; spheres.
Warm-up

Roll It Two Ways

Roll one sheet of paper (21.6\ \text{cm}\times27.9\ \text{cm}) into a tube. Tape the edges together, no overlap. Roll it the tall way, or the short way.

1
Which tube uses more paper?
Neither. Both curved surfaces are the whole sheet: 21.6\times27.9\approx603\ \text{cm}^2.
2
How tall is each tube? How far is it around each tube?
Tall: height 27.9 cm, around 21.6 cm. Short: height 21.6 cm, around 27.9 cm. The two sides just swap jobs.
3
Which tube has the bigger base?
The short tube (radius \approx4.4 cm vs 3.4 cm). The tall tube has the bigger height. Who wins? In V=\pi r^{2}h the radius gets squared; the height doesn’t.
!
The big one. Fill both tubes with rice. Which one holds more? Guess first.
A the tall tube
B the short tube
C the same (same sheet of paper!)
B: the short tube, about 30% more. Tall: r=\frac{21.6}{2\pi}\approx3.44 cm, V=\pi r^{2}h\approx1036\ \text{cm}^3. Short: r=\frac{27.9}{2\pi}\approx4.44 cm, V\approx1338\ \text{cm}^3. That is about 1.0 L against 1.3 L.
C is the trap: same paper means the same surface, not the same space inside. A is the “taller looks bigger” instinct (think of tall, skinny glasses).

Try it: stand the tall tube inside the short one, fill it with rice, then lift it out. How full is the short tube?

+
Early finishers. Fold the sheet into a square tube the tall way instead (each side 21.6\div4=5.4 cm). Does it hold more or less than the tall cylinder?
Less. 5.4^{2}\times27.9\approx814\ \text{cm}^3, against about 1036\ \text{cm}^3. For a fixed distance around, a circle encloses the most area.

Talk to your neighbour: what would you change about the sheet to make the tube hold even more?

1

Right Prisms

A prism has two congruent, parallel bases, with every side face a parallelogram. A right prism is a prism whose side faces are all rectangles: each side face meets the base at a right angle. In the three right prisms below the two congruent bases are the front and back ends.
Right Triangular Prism
Two triangular ends, three rectangular sides.
Right Rectangular Prism
Two rectangular ends, four rectangular sides.
Cube
Two square ends, four square sides: six square faces.
Formulas for any right prism.
V_{\text{Prism}}=A_{\text{Base}}\times h
where A_{\text{Base}} is the area of one base and h is the distance between the two bases (often called the length of a triangular prism). For surface area, add together the areas of every face: the two congruent bases plus every rectangular side face.
2

Volume and Surface Area of a Right Triangular Prism

Volume counts the space inside (cubic units); surface area counts the covering (square units). The same prism gives both.

Example 1

Base area, then volume, then every face

Consider the right triangular prism shown, whose triangular base is a right triangle.

(a) Determine the area of the triangular base of the prism.
The base is a right triangle with legs 9 cm and 12 cm (15 cm is the hypotenuse).
A_{\text{Base}}=\tfrac12(9)(12)=\boldsymbol{54\ \text{cm}^{2}}: an area, so \text{cm}^2.
(b) Determine the volume of the prism.
The length of the prism, the distance between the two triangular bases, is 11 cm.
V=A_{\text{Base}}\times h=54\times11=\boldsymbol{594\ \text{cm}^{3}}: a volume, so \text{cm}^3.
(c) Determine the surface area of the prism.
Two triangular bases plus three rectangular side faces. Each side face is 11 cm long and as wide as one side of the triangle, so together they come to the triangle’s perimeter times 11:
perimeter =9+12+15=36 cm, \quad lateral area =36\times11=396\ \text{cm}^2.
SA=2(54)+396=108+396=\boldsymbol{504\ \text{cm}^{2}}
Volume and surface area in one line each. 0.5×9×12×11ENTER gives 594, and 2×54+36×11ENTER gives 504.
!
The 15 cm side is not a height. The base area uses the two legs that meet at the right angle (9 and 12). The hypotenuse only matters for the surface area: it is the width of the slanted side face.

Volume: layers of the base

Cut the prism into slices 1 unit thick, parallel to the base. Every slice is a copy of the base, holding A_{\text{Base}} cubic units. There are h slices, so V=A_{\text{Base}}\times h.

Surface area: unfold the net

Cut along some edges and fold the prism flat. The flat pattern is its net: every face appears exactly once, so the surface area is the total of the net’s areas.

3

Working With Volume and Capacity Units

The volume of a solid is measured in \text{cm}^3, \text{m}^3, \text{in}^3, \text{ft}^3, and so on. The capacity of a container, how much liquid it holds, is measured in litres, millilitres or gallons.

VolumeSolidLiquid
Metric\text{cm}^3,\ \text{m}^3,\ \text{km}^3, etc.litres (L), millilitres (mL), etc.
Imperial\text{in}^3,\ \text{ft}^3,\ \text{yd}^3, etc.gallons, pints, etc.
Conversions.
1\ \text{cm}^3=1\ \text{mL} 1000\ \text{mL}=1\ \text{L} 1\ \text{m}^3=1000\ \text{L} 1\ \text{US gallon}=3.7854\ \text{L} 1\ \text{imperial (UK) gallon}=4.5461\ \text{L} 1\ \text{gallon}=8\ \text{pints}
Between systems, cube the linear factor of Lesson 4B: from 1\ \text{ft}=0.3048\ \text{m} come 1\ \text{ft}^3=0.028\,316\,8\ \text{m}^3 and 1\ \text{m}^3=35.3147\ \text{ft}^3; squaring it gives 1\ \text{ft}^2=0.092\,903\ \text{m}^2.
Example 2

The same box as a volume and as a capacity

Vault Boxes Inc. manufactures containers in the shape of right rectangular prisms. Nova Blocks Ltd needs a cardboard container 20 cm long, 15 cm wide and 12 cm high to pack 1 cm building blocks. AquaGlow Tanks needs a small glass aquarium with the same dimensions, measured on the inside of the glass.

(a) Calculate the volume of the container for Nova Blocks Ltd. Is \text{cm}^3 an appropriate way to report this volume?
V=l\times w\times h=20\times15\times12=\boldsymbol{3\,600\ \text{cm}^{3}}
Yes. Every dimension is given in centimetres, so \text{cm}^3 is the natural unit here (and it is exactly the number of 1 cm blocks that fit).
(b) The AquaGlow aquarium will be filled with water, and its capacity is advertised in litres and in US gallons. Determine the capacity of one aquarium in both units, to two decimal places.
The dimensions are inside measurements, so the glass thickness does not reduce the space for water. 1\ \text{cm}^3=1\ \text{mL}, so 3\,600\ \text{cm}^3=3\,600\ \text{mL}, and 3\,600\div1000=\boldsymbol{3.60\ \text{L}}.
In US gallons, using 1\ \text{US gal}=3.7854\ \text{L}: 3.6\div3.7854=0.95102\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{0.95\ \text{US gal}}
Volume, then litres, then US gallons. 20×15×12ENTER gives 3600; divide by 1000 for 3.6 L; then 3.6÷3.7854ENTER gives 0.951022349, so 0.95 US gal.
!
Divide by the gallon, don’t multiply. A gallon is bigger than a litre, so there are fewer gallons than litres: 3.6\div3.7854\approx0.95, not 3.6\times3.7854\approx13.6.
4

Volume and Surface Area of a Right Cylinder

A right cylinder behaves exactly like a right prism whose base is a circle instead of a polygon: two congruent, parallel circular bases joined by a curved lateral surface. Unrolling the curved surface gives a flat net made of two circles and one rectangle, and that net is the key to the surface area formula.

Example 3

Reading the surface area off the net

The net of a right cylinder with base radius r and height h is shown below: the circular top and bottom, and the rectangle formed by unrolling the curved side. (Tap a blank to reveal it.)

(a) Fill in the four blanks in the diagram, in terms of r and h.
Top and bottom are circles of radius r, so each has area \pi r^{2}.
The rectangle wraps once around the circle, so its width is the circumference 2\pi r and its height is h:
A_{\text{rect}}=l\times w=(2\pi r)(h)=2\pi rh
(b) Use the diagram to show that the surface area of a right cylinder is SA_{\text{Cylinder}}=2\pi r^{2}+2\pi rh.
Add the areas of the three pieces of the net: top \pi r^{2}, bottom \pi r^{2}, rectangle 2\pi rh.
SA_{\text{Cylinder}}=\pi r^{2}+\pi r^{2}+2\pi rh
=2\pi r^{2}+2\pi rh\ \checkmark
(c) Use the general formula for the volume of a right prism to write the formula for the volume of a cylinder of radius r and height h.
The general prism formula is V=A_{\text{Base}}\times h. A cylinder’s base is a circle of area \pi r^{2}, so
V_{\text{Cylinder}}=\pi r^{2}\times h=\pi r^{2}h\ \checkmark
Formulas for a right cylinder.
V_{\text{Cylinder}}=\pi r^{2}h\qquad\qquad SA_{\text{Cylinder}}=2\pi r^{2}+2\pi rh=2\pi r(r+h)
Example 4

A canister, from a diameter

A cylindrical storage canister has a height of 12.6 cm and a base diameter of 8.4 cm. Determine the volume and the surface area of the canister, each to the nearest tenth.

The diagram gives the diameter, so first halve it: r=8.4\div2=4.2 cm, and h=12.6 cm.
V=\pi r^{2}h=\pi(4.2)^{2}(12.6)=\pi(17.64)(12.6)=222.264\pi
=698.2629\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{698.3\ \text{cm}^{3}}
SA=2\pi r^{2}+2\pi rh=2\pi(17.64)+2\pi(4.2)(12.6)
=35.28\pi+105.84\pi=141.12\pi=443.3415\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{443.3\ \text{cm}^{2}}
Exact \pi is carried to the last step, and the units differ: \text{cm}^3 for the volume, \text{cm}^2 for the surface area.
Use the π key, and round once. π×4.2x²×12.6ENTER gives 698.2629496. For the surface area, 2×π×4.2x²+2×π×4.2×12.6ENTER gives 443.3415553.
!
Halve the diameter first. Using 8.4 as the radius makes the base four times too big, because the radius is squared.

Radius first, round last

Two habits keep cylinder answers right: halve a diameter before it goes into a formula, and keep every digit (and exact \pi) until the very last step.

5

Solving for an Unknown Dimension Given the Volume

When the volume is already known and a dimension is asked for, substitute every known value into the appropriate volume formula and solve the resulting equation for the unknown.

Example 5

A cube, working backwards from its volume

A commercial recycling bin in the shape of a cube has a volume of 74.5\ \text{m}^3. Determine the side length s of the bin, to the nearest cm.

For a cube V=s^{3}, so s=\sqrt[3]{V}:
s=\sqrt[3]{74.5}=4.207770\ldots\ \text{m}
The answer is wanted in centimetres: 4.207770\ldots\times100=420.777\ldots\ \text{cm}\approx\boldsymbol{421\ \text{cm}}
Check: 4.20777^{3}=74.5\ \checkmark
Cube root on the TI-30XIIS. Type the root index first: 32nd^ (ˣ√)74.5ENTER gives 4.207770949 m. Check with x³: 4.20777x³ is about 74.5.
Example 6

A triangular prism, working backwards from its volume

The box for a chocolate bar is shaped like a right triangular prism. The triangular ends have a base of 4.2 cm and a height of 3.6 cm. If the empty space inside the box has a volume of 145.3\ \text{cm}^3, determine the length L of the box, to the nearest tenth of a cm.

A_{\text{Base}}=\tfrac12(4.2)(3.6)=7.56\ \text{cm}^2
Since V=A_{\text{Base}}\times L,
L=\dfrac{V}{A_{\text{Base}}}=\dfrac{145.3}{7.56}=19.21957\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{19.2\ \text{cm}}
Check: 7.56\times19.2=145.2\ \text{cm}^3\ \checkmark
Divide by the whole base area. 145.3÷(0.5×4.2×3.6)ENTER gives 19.21957672. Without the brackets the calculator divides by 0.5 only, then multiplies.

Working backwards

Undo the formula. A cube cubed its side, so take the cube root. A prism multiplied its base area by the length, so divide: how many layers of the base fit in the volume?

6

Curved Surface Area Only

Some questions ask only for the area of the curved lateral surface of a cylinder: a label wrapped around a can, for instance. That area is the circumference of the base times the height, without the area of either circular end.

Example 7

The label on a poster tube

A shipping company packs its poster tubes in cylindrical cardboard containers with a height of 21.5 in and a base circumference of 3.75 in. Every container is wrapped with a paper label that covers the entire curved surface. Calculate, to the nearest square inch, the area of the label.

The label covers the curved surface only (neither circular end is included), so its area is circumference \times height:
Curved SA=3.75\times21.5=80.625\approx\boldsymbol{81\ \text{in}^{2}}
(No \pi is needed here: the circumference is given directly.)
Length times width of the unrolled label. 3.75×21.5ENTER gives 80.625, so 81 in².

Peel off the label

Unwrap the curved surface and it lies flat as a rectangle: one side is the distance around (the circumference), the other is the height. The circular ends stay behind on the tube.

7

Solving for an Unknown Dimension Given the Surface Area

Working backwards from a surface area is the same idea as Section 5: write the surface-area formula, substitute everything you know, and solve for the one unknown. For a cylinder, the 2\pi r^{2} part becomes a number, so the height is left in a one-step linear equation.

Example 8

The height of a can from its surface area

A closed cylindrical can has a surface area of 250\ \text{cm}^2 and a radius of 3.5 cm. Determine its height, to the nearest tenth of a centimetre.

SA=2\pi r^{2}+2\pi rh
250=2\pi(3.5)^{2}+2\pi(3.5)h=76.969\ldots+21.991\ldots h
21.991\ldots h=250-76.969\ldots=173.030\ldots
h=\dfrac{173.030\ldots}{21.991\ldots}=7.868\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{7.9}\ \text{cm}
Check: 2\pi(3.5)^{2}+2\pi(3.5)(7.868)=250.0\ \checkmark
Subtract the circles, then divide by 2\pi r. (250−2×π×3.5x²)÷(2×π×3.5)ENTER gives 7.868210221, so 7.9 cm.

What is left for the rectangle?

The two circles take their share of the 250\ \text{cm}^2 first. Whatever is left must be the curved rectangle, and a rectangle’s height is its area divided by its width.

✓

Key ideas

Volume = layers of the base. V_{\text{Prism}}=A_{\text{Base}}\times h for every right prism; a cylinder’s base is a circle, so V_{\text{Cylinder}}=\pi r^{2}h. Cubic units: \text{cm}^3, \text{m}^3, \text{in}^3.
Surface area = every face. Unfold the net and add: two bases plus the side faces (together, perimeter \times length). Cylinder: SA=2\pi r^{2}+2\pi rh. Curved surface only: circumference \times height. Square units.
Capacity. 1\ \text{cm}^3=1\ \text{mL}, 1000\ \text{mL}=1\ \text{L}, 1\ \text{m}^3=1000\ \text{L}, 1\ \text{US gal}=3.7854\ \text{L}. Between systems, cube the linear factor for volume, square it for area.
Careful habits. Halve a diameter first, use the π key, round only at the end. Working backwards: cube root for a cube, V\div A_{\text{Base}} for a length, subtract the circles then \div2\pi r for a height.
