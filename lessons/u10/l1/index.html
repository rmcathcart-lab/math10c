Systems of Measurement, Referents, and Measuring Instruments

No ruler? You carry a whole set of them around with you. Today: the two systems of measurement, imperial and SI, and how to estimate a length: pick a referent close in size to the object, count how many times it fits, then multiply the count by the referent’s length. When “about” isn’t good enough, read a vernier caliper or a micrometer: a coarse reading plus a fine reading.

I can…
  • name the imperial units of length (inch, foot, yard, mile) and the SI units (millimetre, centimetre, metre, kilometre) and state how they are related
  • explain what a referent is, and give a personal referent for common units in both systems
  • choose a referent that is close in size to the object being measured, and justify the choice
  • estimate a length, a perimeter or a circumference by laying a referent end to end, counting, and multiplying
  • measure a scale diagram in both systems and use the scale to find the actual length
  • build a referent for a large unit out of a referent for a smaller one
  • read a vernier caliper (to 0.05 mm) and a micrometer (to 0.01 mm) by adding a coarse and a fine reading
M1 Solve problems that involve linear measurement, using: SI and imperial units of measure; estimation strategies; measurement strategies.

1

The Imperial System of Measurement

Before measuring instruments existed, an unknown length was compared to a familiar object or body part: a referent. The need for consistent, repeatable measurements produced formal systems of measurement. The imperial system, developed in England and still in everyday use in the United States, measures length in inches, feet, yards and miles.

Imperial units of length
1\text{ foot}=12\text{ inches}\qquad 1\text{ yard}=3\text{ feet}\qquad 1\text{ mile}=1\,760\text{ yards}

Many imperial units began life as referents:

Yard Shortly after the Norman Conquest of 1066, legend holds that King Henry I decreed it to be the distance from the tip of his nose to the end of his outstretched thumb.
Furlong Used by Saxon farmers before 1066 for how far a team of oxen could plow before resting. It still states the distance of horse races: 8 furlongs =1 mile.
Rod A pole about 11 cubits long.
League Several personal referents, the two most common being the distance a person or a horse could walk in one hour: roughly 3 miles. No longer in common use.
Chain The length of a cricket pitch, about 4 rods, and 10 chains =1 furlong.
Hand The width of a clenched fist, 4 inches. It still states the height of a horse or pony.
2

The SI System of Measurement

The other major system is the SI system (Système International d’Unités), commonly called the metric system. As early as 1670 Gabriel Mouton and Jean Picard proposed a base-10 system to replace the tangle of older, inconsistent units, and in 1790 the French Academy of Sciences was asked to design a scientific standard: a unit of length based on a fraction of the Earth’s circumference, with every related unit scaled by a power of 10. Canada officially adopted SI in the 1970s, by which time most of the world had already switched. The common SI unit of length is the metre.

SI units of length
1\text{ centimetre}=10\text{ millimetres}\qquad 1\text{ metre}=100\text{ centimetres}\qquad 1\text{ kilometre}=1\,000\text{ metres}

The United States uses SI units widely in science but still relies on the imperial system for most everyday purposes, so both systems are worth knowing well.

Think Which system is easier to move between units in? Why?
SI. Every SI step is a power of 10 (\times10,\ \times100,\ \times1\,000), so changing units just moves the decimal point. The imperial steps (12,\ 3,\ 1\,760) are all different.

Referents: Measuring Without a Tool

A referent is a familiar object, very often a part of your own body, used as a stand-in point of reference when no measuring tool is available. A referent taken from a person’s immediate surroundings (their own body, or an object they always carry) is a personal referent.
Body-based referents are the oldest and most convenient, since they are always at hand. The five below are drawn roughly to scale relative to one another.
1
Pick a referent close in size to the object.
2
Count how many times it fits, end to end.
3
Multiply the count by the referent’s length.
!
A pace is a normal walking step, about 90 cm \approx1 yd. Walking heel-to-toe measures something else: foot lengths of about 1 ft.
Example 1

Matching the size of the referent to the size of the object

For each object, state one personal referent (from the diagram above, or from the imperial units it represents) that would be reasonable to use to estimate it, and justify the choice.

(a) the length of a hockey rink
A standard NHL rink is 200 ft long, far too big for a small referent. Use the pace (\approx90 cm \approx1 yd): walk the length with normal walking paces, counting, then multiply the count by about 1 yard.
200\text{ ft}\div3\text{ ft per pace}\approx\boldsymbol{67} paces, a countable number.
A thumb width would need 200\times12=2\,400 repetitions: far too many to count without losing track.
(b) the length of a USB flash drive
A flash drive is only an inch or two long, so a pace or a cubit is far too large to resolve it. Use the width of a thumb across the joint (\approx2.5 cm \approx1 in): lay the drive beside your thumb and compare directly.
About 2 thumb widths \Rightarrow about 2 in (\approx5 cm).
The referent should be close in size to the object.
(c) the height of a pony
Horse and pony heights are traditionally given in hands, and 1 hand =4 in \approx10 cm is exactly the width of a clenched fist. Stack fists from the ground to the withers (the top of the shoulders) and count.
A 12-hand pony is 12\times4=48 in = 4 ft (\approx122 cm).
Both the most natural referent and the standard unit for the object.
How many paces? 200÷3ENTER gives 66.67, so about 67 paces. The pony: 12×4ENTER =48 in.
Math HistoryEgypt · c. 2700 BCE

A forearm made official

A cubit is the length from your elbow to your fingertip, a personal referent. Ancient Egypt was building with it by the time of the Step Pyramid of Djoser, around 2700 BCE.

Arms come in different lengths, so Egypt fixed one royal cubit of 7 palms, each palm 4 fingers wide (28 fingers in all), and marked it on rods of wood and stone. Rods that survive measure about 52.4 to 52.9 cm.

Say your palm (four fingers side by side) is about 7.5 cm. How long is your 7-palm cubit? About how many royal cubits tall is a 2 m doorway?
7\times7.5=\boldsymbol{52.5} cm, right in the range of the real rods. Doorway: 200\div52.5\approx3.8, so about 4 royal cubits.

Example 2

One object, one referent from each system

Consider the task of estimating the length of a bath towel.

(a) Identify a referent that could be used to estimate the length in SI units.
A bath towel is roughly 1–1.5 m long, so a mid-sized SI referent fits best: the width of this workbook, \approx20 cm.
It takes about 100\div20=5 to 150\div20=7.5 workbook widths: a small, easy count.
(A pinky fingernail, \approx1 cm, would need 100–150 repetitions.)
(b) Identify a referent that could be used to estimate the length in imperial units.
The adult pace, \approx90 cm \approx1 yd.
A towel of 1–1.5 m is about 1 to 1\tfrac{1}{2} paces long, so the count stays small and the leftover fraction is easy to judge by eye.
(The thumb, \approx1 in, would need 40–60 repetitions; the adult foot, \approx1 ft, about 3–5.)
(c) Explain the process you would use to estimate the length with your imperial referent from part (b).
Lay the towel out flat. Starting at one end, walk along it with normal paces, counting full paces and estimating any leftover fraction. Then multiply by the referent’s length:
\text{estimated length}=(\text{number of paces})\times1\text{ yd}
E.g. 1\tfrac{1}{2} paces gives 1.5\text{ yd}=1.5\times3=\boldsymbol{4.5\text{ ft}} (\approx1.4 m).
The estimate is only as good as the referent: treat it as approximate, not exact.
Example 3

Strategies for a perimeter and a circumference

(a) Describe and explain a personal strategy that could be used to estimate the perimeter of the base of a rectangular tool shed, using one of the referents above.
Use the adult foot (\approx1 ft): walk heel-to-toe along each side of the shed’s base, counting foot lengths. Multiply each count by about 1 ft, then add:
P\approx(\text{count}_1+\text{count}_2+\text{count}_3+\text{count}_4)\times1\text{ ft}
Because the base is a rectangle, only two adjacent sides need to be counted: P\approx2(\ell+w). A shed measuring 8 heel-to-toe steps by 12 gives P\approx2(8+12)=\boldsymbol{40\text{ ft}}.
For a large shed the pace (\approx1 yd) keeps the counts smaller.
(b) Describe and explain a personal strategy that could be used to estimate the circumference of a bicycle wheel, using one of the referents above.
A rim is curved, so no straight referent can be laid along it. Wrap a string once around the outside of the tire, mark where it meets itself, then lay the string out straight.
Measure the string with a small SI referent, the width of a pinky fingernail (\approx1 cm), counting how many fit. The count is the circumference in centimetres.
A 700 mm road wheel has circumference \pi(70)\approx220 cm, so expect roughly 220 fingernail widths.
Circumference of the wheel. C=\pi d with d=700 mm =70 cm: π×70ENTER gives 219.911\ldots\approx220 cm.
Example 4

Measuring a scale diagram in both systems

Shown below is a scale diagram of a sample access card, drawn at a scale of 1:3. Measure the printed diagram itself: it is reproduced at full size.

sample card
In the booklet this diagram is printed at its true size, 3.0 cm long. On a screen it is not, so measure the booklet’s copy, or use the on-screen ruler in the explainer below.
(a) Measure the length of the scale diagram above, in both SI units and imperial units.
A ruler laid along the longer edge reads 3.0 cm.
Converting with 1\text{ in}=2.54 cm:
3.0\text{ cm}\div2.54\text{ cm/in}=1.1811\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{1.18\text{ in}}
(On a ruler marked in sixteenths, this is just short of 1\tfrac{3}{16} in.)
(b) Use your answer from part (a) to determine the actual length of the access card.
The scale is 1:3, so every 1 cm on the diagram stands for 3 cm on the real card.
3.0\text{ cm}\times3=\boldsymbol{9.0\text{ cm}}
In imperial units: 9.0\div2.54=3.5433\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{3.54\text{ in}}, the same as 1.18\times3.
The length is multiplied by the scale factor; the unit does not change.
(c) Could the length of this access card be used as a referent in either system of measurement? Explain.
Yes. Access cards of this type are manufactured to a fixed, standard size, so the length is the same from card to card: exactly the consistency a referent needs. A card kept in a wallet is always at hand, so it is a personal referent.
In SI it stands for \approx9 cm; in imperial units for \approx3\tfrac12 in.
(A Canadian bank note works for the same reason.)
Centimetres to inches: divide by 2.54. 3÷2.54ENTER gives 1.1811\ldots and 9÷2.54ENTER gives 3.5433\ldots
Example 5

Building a big referent out of a smaller one

Marcus was asked to give a referent for 1 kilometre. He could not think of anything that was actually 1 kilometre long, so instead he built his referent for the kilometre out of a referent for a smaller unit. Suggest how he might have done this.

Build the large referent by repeating a smaller one whose length he already knows. Since 1 km =1\,000 m, and an Olympic swimming pool is 50 m:
1\,000\text{ m}\div50\text{ m per pool length}=\boldsymbol{20}
So 1 kilometre is about 20 pool lengths laid end to end.
The same method works from any smaller referent (with an adult pace, \approx1 yd \approx0.9 m: 1\,000\div0.9\approx1\,111 paces), but 20 pool lengths is by far the easier count to picture.
How many referents make a kilometre? 1000÷50ENTER =20 pool lengths; 1000÷.9ENTER =1\,111.1\ldots paces.

Measuring Instruments: the Vernier Caliper and the Micrometer

A referent is good to “about”. When a job needs more, pick an instrument that suits the size and the precision: a trundle wheel for long distances, a tape measure for rooms, a ruler for the desk (to 1 mm), a vernier caliper for small objects to 0.05 mm, a micrometer for very small ones to 0.01 mm. Both precision tools give a coarse reading on a fixed scale plus a fine reading on a moving scale, stated to the tool’s own precision: 25.30 mm, not 25.3 mm.

Vernier caliper. Coarse: the last main-scale mark (whole mm) at or left of the index, the vernier’s 0. Fine: the vernier mark that lines up best with a main-scale mark, \times0.05 mm.
Micrometer. Coarse: the last sleeve mark showing left of the thimble (a mark every 0.5 mm; halves are below the line). Fine: the thimble mark on the index line, \times0.01 mm.
\text{caliper: }54\text{ mm}+13\times0.05\text{ mm}=54.65\text{ mm}\qquad\text{micrometer: }8.5\text{ mm}+27\times0.01\text{ mm}=8.77\text{ mm}
Example 6

Reading a vernier caliper and a micrometer

Determine each reading, in millimetres, to the precision of the instrument.

(a) a vernier caliper
Coarse (main scale): the index is just past 42 mm. Fine (vernier): mark 13 lines up, so 13\times0.05=0.65 mm.
42+0.65=\boldsymbol{42.65\text{ mm}} (to the caliper’s 0.05 mm).
(b) a micrometer
Coarse (sleeve): the half mark below the line shows, so 11.5 mm. Fine (thimble): index line on 16, so 16\times0.01=0.16 mm.
11.5+0.16=\boldsymbol{11.66\text{ mm}}

Key ideas

Two systems. Imperial: 1 ft =12 in, 1 yd =3 ft, 1 mi =1\,760 yd. SI: 1 cm =10 mm, 1 m =100 cm, 1 km =1\,000 m: every step a power of 10.
Referents. A familiar object (often a body part) used as a stand-in when there is no measuring tool. Thumb \approx1 in, adult foot \approx1 ft, pace \approx1 yd; pinky nail \approx1 cm, workbook \approx20 cm, 4-year-old \approx1 m.
Pick · count · multiply. Choose a referent close in size to the object, lay it end to end: estimate = count \times referent length. Rectangle: P\approx2(\ell+w). Curve: wrap a string, straighten it, count. Scale diagram: measure, multiply by the scale. Big unit: repeat a smaller referent.
Instruments: coarse + fine. Caliper: main-scale mark at or left of the index, plus the vernier mark that lines up \times0.05 mm. Micrometer: last sleeve mark (halves below the line), plus the thimble mark \times0.01 mm. State the tool’s precision: 25.30 mm.
