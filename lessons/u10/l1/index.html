Rounding and Formula Substitution

Every measurement in this unit ends the same way: put numbers into a formula, let the calculator work, then round the answer sensibly. Today: round to any place value (and see why the rule works), keep every digit until the end, and substitute into expressions and formulas without sign slips.

I can…
  • round a number to any place value, and explain why it snaps to the closer neighbour
  • keep every digit through a calculation and round only once, at the end
  • Extension write a number in scientific notation and re-normalize a calculated coefficient
  • substitute values, each in brackets, into an expression and evaluate it with the order of operations
  • substitute into a measurement formula and solve for the unknown, either substituting first or solving first
M1 Solve problems that involve linear measurement, using: SI and imperial units of measure; estimation strategies; measurement strategies.

A skills lesson for the whole unit: the rounding and formula work here is used in every lesson that follows, for length (M1), conversions (M2), and surface area and volume (M3).

Rounding to a Given Place Value

A calculation often produces far more digits than are useful: a non-terminating decimal, or a value with many decimal places. Rounding expresses such an answer to a sensible degree of accuracy, and gives a quick way to check whether an answer is reasonable.

The Rounding Rule. To round a number to a given place value:
  1. Find the digit in that place: the place-value digit.
  2. Look at the digit immediately to its right: the test digit.
  3. If the test digit is 0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3 or 4, leave the place-value digit as it is (round down), and drop every digit to its right (replacing them with zeros if they lie to the left of the decimal point).
  4. If the test digit is 5,\ 6,\ 7,\ 8 or 9, increase the place-value digit by one (round up), and drop every digit to its right the same way.

To round 52\,148.673 to the nearest ten, the place-value digit is the 4 and the test digit is the 8; since 8\ge5 we round up, giving \mathbf{52\,150}. For a negative number, rounding up means increasing the magnitude (moving further from 0), so -138.4562 rounds to -138.5 at the tenths place, not -138.4.

Zoom in: which neighbour is closer?

Rounding to a place value means: of the two numbers on either side at that place, which one is closer? Zoom in on the number line and the number sits between two neighbours. Past the halfway mark, it snaps to the upper one. That is all the test digit is checking.

!
Exactly halfway goes up. A test digit of 5 means the number is at (or past) the halfway mark, and the rule says round up. For a negative number, “up” means away from 0: -2.86\to-2.9, because -2.9 really is the closer neighbour.

The rule on the digits

The same rule, done on the digits themselves: find the place, read the test digit, roll the place-value digit up (or not), then drop everything to its right. A 9 that rolls up carries into the next place, like a car’s odometer.

Rounding with a calculator

Two features do this automatically. Setting a fixed number of decimal places in the mode/format menu rounds every displayed result to that many places: useful when every answer in a problem needs the same precision, such as money to the nearest cent. A round( function in the Math or numeric menu (on graphing calculators) rounds one calculation on demand, as round(expression, number of decimal places): useful when different questions call for different precision.

On the TI-30XIIS. Press 2ndFIX (FIX is above the decimal-point key) to get the menu F 0 1 2 3 … 9, arrow to the number of decimal places and press ENTER (FIX shows on the screen). Choose F (floating) to go back to every digit. The calculator on this site works the same way. It has no round( key, so for a one-off rounding, read the full display and use the rounding rule.

Whichever you use, the calculator must still hold every digit while it works. Round once, at the very end, never part-way through.

Scientific Notation

Extension
ExtensionThis section is enrichment: the Math 10C measurement outcomes do not require scientific notation (you met it in Unit 2). Sections 1, 3 and 4 are the core of this lesson.

Some measurements are extremely large (the distance between planets) or extremely small (the diameter of a cell), and standard notation is unwieldy for them. Scientific notation writes such a number as

a\times10^{n},\qquad 1\le a<10,\ \ n\text{ an integer,}

where a is the coefficient. Neptune’s average distance from the sun, about 4\,500\,000\,000 km, becomes

4\,500\,000\,000=\underbrace{4.5}_{1\,\le\,a\,<\,10}\times\underbrace{10^{9}}_{\text{power of ten}}.
Example 3

Standard notation to scientific notation

Extension

Express each number in scientific notation, rounded to the nearest tenth.

(a) 68\,400\,000
Move the decimal point so the coefficient lands in [1,\ 10): from 68\,400\,000 it moves 7 places left, so n=7.
68\,400\,000=6.84\times10^{7}
Round the coefficient to the nearest tenth: test digit 4<5, round down: \mathbf{6.8\times10^{7}}
(b) 0.000\,051\,27
The decimal point moves 5 places right to reach 5.127, so n=-5.
0.000\,051\,27=5.127\times10^{-5}
Coefficient to the nearest tenth: test digit 2<5, round down: \mathbf{5.1\times10^{-5}}

Re-normalizing: keep the coefficient between 1 and 10

Extension

Multiplying or dividing the coefficients can land outside 1\le a<10. Hop the decimal point one place left (\div10) and raise the exponent by one (\times10): the two changes cancel, so the value stays the same.

Substitution in Algebraic Expressions

To evaluate an expression for given values of its variables, replace each variable with its value, always enclosed in brackets, then simplify using the order of operations. The brackets prevent sign errors when a value is negative. A calculator’s variable-store feature (STO▸) substitutes automatically once the values are stored, and applies the brackets for you.

!
The square needs the brackets. (-3)^{2}=(-3)(-3)=9, but -3^{2}=-(3\times3)=-9: without brackets the square only touches the 3. The TI-30XIIS reads (−)3x² as -9 too.
Brackets on the calculator. ((−)3)x²ENTER gives 9; (−)3x²ENTER gives −9. Or store the value: (−)3STO▸, choose a letter, ENTER, then use RCL to put it in an expression.

Substitution in Formulas

A measurement formula relates several quantities, and two tasks recur throughout this unit: substitute the known values directly and solve for the one remaining unknown, or first solve the formula in general for the unknown variable and then substitute. Both approaches are used below, on formulas you will meet again later in the unit.

Example 6

Substitute first, then solve

In each case: (i) substitute the given value into the formula, then (ii) solve for the indicated variable, rounding to the nearest tenth where necessary.

(a) The distance formula is d=vt. If d=84 and v=12, calculate t.
Substitute into d=vt:
84=(12)t
t=\dfrac{84}{12}=\mathbf{7}
(Exact, so no rounding is needed.)
(b) The circumference of a circle is C=2\pi r. If C=100, calculate r.
Substitute into C=2\pi r:
100=2\pi r
r=\dfrac{100}{2\pi}=\dfrac{100}{6.28318\ldots}=15.9154\ldots
\approx\mathbf{15.9}
Check: 2\pi(15.9155)=100.0 ✓
(c) The volume of a sphere is V=\dfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}. If V=150, calculate r.
Substitute into V=\frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}:
150=\frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}
r^{3}=\dfrac{3(150)}{4\pi}=\dfrac{450}{12.56637\ldots}=35.8098\ldots
r=\sqrt[3]{35.8098\ldots}=3.2961\ldots\approx\mathbf{3.3}
Bracket the whole denominator, and use the π key. (b) 100÷(2×π)ENTER gives 15.91549431. (c) The cube root is 32nd^ (\sqrt[x]{\ }): 3ˣ√(3×150÷(4×π))ENTER gives 3.296103825.

Substitute, then undo

Once the numbers are in, the unknown is wrapped in operations. Undo them, one at a time, doing the same thing to both sides. Keep every digit, and round at the end.

Example 7

Solve for the variable first, then substitute

In each case: (i) solve the formula in general for the indicated variable, then (ii) substitute the given values and calculate, rounding to the nearest tenth where necessary.

(a) The area of a circle is A=\pi r^{2}, where r>0. Solve for r, then calculate r when A=60.
Solve first: A=\pi r^{2}\Rightarrow r^{2}=\dfrac{A}{\pi}\Rightarrow r=\sqrt{\dfrac{A}{\pi}} (positive root, since r>0).
r^{2}=\dfrac{60}{\pi}=19.0986\ldots
r=\sqrt{19.0986\ldots}=4.3702\ldots\approx\mathbf{4.4}
(b) The volume of a cylinder is V=\pi r^{2}h. Solve for r, then calculate r when V=250 and h=5.
Solve first: V=\pi r^{2}h\Rightarrow r^{2}=\dfrac{V}{\pi h}\Rightarrow r=\sqrt{\dfrac{V}{\pi h}}.
r^{2}=\dfrac{250}{\pi(5)}=\dfrac{250}{15.7079\ldots}=15.9154\ldots
r=\sqrt{15.9154\ldots}=3.9894\ldots\approx\mathbf{4.0}
(c) The volume of a cone is V=\dfrac{1}{3}\pi r^{2}h. Solve for h, then calculate h when V=90 and r=6.
Solve first: V=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^{2}h\Rightarrow 3V=\pi r^{2}h\Rightarrow h=\dfrac{3V}{\pi r^{2}}.
h=\dfrac{3(90)}{\pi(6)^{2}}=\dfrac{270}{113.0973\ldots}=2.3873\ldots
\approx\mathbf{2.4}
Type the solved formula in one line. (a) √60÷π)ENTER gives 4.370193722 (√ opens its own bracket). (b) √250÷(π×5))ENTER gives 3.989422804. (c) 3×90÷(π×6x²)ENTER gives 2.387324146.

Solve first: run the formula backwards

A formula is a recipe: it says what to do to the unknown to get the answer. To solve for the unknown, run the recipe backwards, undoing the last step first.

Think In Example 7(a), why do we keep only the positive square root?
A radius is a length, so r>0. r=-4.37\ldots also makes \pi r^{2}=60, but it isn’t a real radius.
✓

Key ideas

Rounding = the closer neighbour. Find the place-value digit, read the test digit to its right: 0–4 round down, 5–9 round up (a 9 carries). Drop the digits to its right; zeros hold the places left of the decimal point.
Round once, at the end. Keep every digit (and the π key, never 3.14) through the whole calculation. Rounding part-way through drifts the final answer.
Substitute in brackets. x=-3 goes in as (-3), so x^{2}=(-3)^{2}=9. Then powers, multiply/divide, add/subtract. Ext a\times10^{n} needs 1\le a<10.
Formulas: two routes. Substitute first and undo, or solve for the unknown first and then substitute. Undo the last operation first. Answers keep their units.
