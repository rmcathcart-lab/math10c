Reading Precision Measuring Instruments

Every precision instrument gives a coarse reading on a fixed scale and a fine reading on a moving scale. Add them, and state the result to the instrument’s own precision.

I can…
  • choose a trundle wheel, tape measure, ruler, vernier caliper or micrometer to suit the size of an object and the precision needed
  • read a vernier caliper: main-scale coarse reading plus the vernier mark that lines up, at 0.05 mm per division
  • read a caliper’s inch scale in sixteenths plus 128ths, as a mixed fraction in lowest terms
  • Extension read a micrometer: sleeve plus thimble, at 0.01 mm per division

The Loonie Stack

One loonie is too thin to measure well with a ruler. So stack a bunch of them!

The stack of 20 loonies is 35 mm tall. How thick is one loonie?
35\div20=\boldsymbol{1.75}\text{ mm}

How tall is $100 in loonies, in cm?
100\times1.75=175\text{ mm}=\boldsymbol{17.5}\text{ cm}

Add the caliper’s two readings. Does it agree with your answer to Q1?
1\text{ mm}+0.75\text{ mm}=\boldsymbol{1.75}\text{ mm}: the same answer, from a single coin. A ruler only reads to the nearest millimetre, so one loonie would read “about 2 mm”; stacking 20 spreads that error over 20 coins. The caliper gets the same precision in one go.

Common Measuring Devices

Trundle wheel
A wheel on a handle that clicks off each rotation as it is rolled along the ground; for long distances such as a running track or a hallway.
Tape measure
A flexible strip marked in inches, centimetres, or both, that rolls up for storage and bends around corners; for room dimensions and other medium lengths.
Ruler
A rigid, short, straight-edged strip; for lengths that fit on a desk, and for drawing straight lines.
Vernier caliper
A sliding two-scale tool that reads outside dimensions, inside dimensions and hole depths to a fine fraction of a millimetre.
Micrometer
The most precise of the five; measures the thickness or outside diameter of very small objects to the nearest hundredth of a millimetre.
Precision. A micrometer typically reads to the nearest 0.01 mm; a vernier caliper reads to the nearest 0.1 mm or 0.05 mm. The exact precision is printed on the instrument, and a reading is always stated to that precision: 25.30 mm, not 25.3 mm, on a caliper good to 0.05 mm.
2

Reading a Micrometer

Extension

The Math 10C outcomes name rulers, tape measures and vernier calipers; the micrometer is enrichment. It works on the same coarse-plus-fine idea as the caliper in Section 3.

A micrometer has two scales that add together.

  • The sleeve (barrel) scale is fixed and marked every 0.5 mm. As the thimble turns, its edge slides along the sleeve and covers part of it. The coarse reading is the last fully visible sleeve mark, immediately to the left of the thimble’s edge.
  • The thimble scale rotates with the thimble, and a fixed index line crosses it. The fine reading is the thimble mark the index line lands on, at 0.01 mm per division.
\text{reading}=\text{coarse reading (sleeve)}+\text{fine reading (thimble)}

The last fully visible sleeve mark to the left of the thimble body is 8.5 mm, so the coarse reading is 8.5 mm. The index line lines up with thimble mark 27, so the fine reading is 27\times0.01=0.27 mm.

8.5\text{ mm}+0.27\text{ mm}=8.77\text{ mm}

Both parts are needed: the sleeve alone is only good to 0.5 mm.

!
Look below the line. Whole millimetres are marked above the sleeve’s line and half millimetres below it. If a half mark shows past the last whole millimetre, the coarse reading ends in .5.

Reading a Vernier Caliper

A vernier caliper also combines two readings.

  • The main scale is the fixed ruler along the body, marked in whole millimetres. The coarse reading is the last main-scale mark at or to the left of the index, the vernier’s own 0 mark.
  • The vernier scale slides along the main scale. The fine reading is the vernier mark that lines up most closely with a main-scale mark; look along the whole vernier to find it. On a metric caliper reading to the nearest 0.05 mm, each vernier division is worth 0.05 mm.

The index (the vernier’s 0) sits just past the 54 mm mark, so the coarse reading is 54 mm. Vernier mark 13 lines up exactly with a main-scale mark (the 67 mm mark), so the fine reading is 13\times0.05=0.65 mm:

54\text{ mm}+0.65\text{ mm}=54.65\text{ mm}.

Why the vernier works

The vernier’s 20 divisions span only 19 mm of the main scale, so each one is a little shorter than a millimetre. That small difference is what the fine reading counts.

Think Why can a caliper that reads to the nearest 0.05 mm never give a reading of 54.62 mm?
The fine reading is always a whole number of 0.05 mm steps (n\times0.05), so the hundredths digit of a reading is always 0 or 5: 54.60 or 54.65, never 54.62.

The inch scale

Many calipers carry an inch scale as well, read the same way. Its main scale is marked every \tfrac{1}{16} in (=\tfrac{8}{128} in), and its vernier has 8 divisions, each worth \tfrac{1}{128} in, so the fine reading is always between \tfrac{0}{128} and \tfrac{7}{128} in. Add the coarse and fine readings in either system; an imperial reading is left as a mixed fraction in lowest terms.

The same jaw opening (54.65 mm) on the caliper’s inch scale is shown below. The index sits just past the 2\tfrac{2}{16}=2\tfrac{1}{8} in mark (coarse reading), and vernier mark 3 lines up with a main-scale mark (fine reading \tfrac{3}{128} in):

2\tfrac{1}{8}\text{ in}+\tfrac{3}{128}\text{ in}=2\tfrac{16}{128}\text{ in}+\tfrac{3}{128}\text{ in}=2\tfrac{19}{128}\text{ in}.

Check: 2\tfrac{19}{128} in \approx2.148 in and 54.65\div25.4\approx2.152 in, the same to the nearest \tfrac{1}{128} in.

Extension
(a) A machinist must measure the diameter of a small steel ball bearing to the nearest 0.01 mm. Which measuring device should be used, and why is a ruler not suitable here?
A micrometer: it is the only device here that reads to the nearest 0.01 mm, which is exactly the precision asked for.
A ruler’s smallest divisions are 1 mm apart, 100 times coarser than 0.01 mm, and the gap between two marks cannot be split into hundredths by eye. A ruler also cannot be closed squarely onto a sphere, so it would not find the true diameter.
(b) The machinist’s instrument reads as shown. State the diameter of the ball bearing, in millimetres.
Coarse (sleeve): last fully visible mark left of the thimble body =4.5 mm.
Fine (thimble): index line on mark 13, so 13\times0.01=0.13 mm.
4.5+0.13=\boldsymbol{4.63\text{ mm}}
✓

Key ideas

Pick the device by size and precision. Trundle wheel for long distances, tape measure for rooms, ruler for the desk (to 1 mm), vernier caliper to 0.05 mm, micrometer to 0.01 mm.
Coarse + fine. Every precision instrument gives a coarse reading on a fixed scale and a fine reading on a moving scale. Add them, and state the result to the instrument’s precision (25.30 mm, not 25.3 mm).
Vernier caliper. Coarse: the last main-scale mark at or left of the index (whole mm). Fine: the vernier mark that lines up, \times0.05 mm. Inch scale: sixteenths plus \tfrac{n}{128} in, as a mixed fraction in lowest terms.
Extension Micrometer. Coarse: the last sleeve mark showing (every 0.5 mm; look below the line for halves). Fine: the thimble mark on the index line, \times0.01 mm.
