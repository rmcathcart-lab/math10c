Surface Area and Volume of Pyramids and Cones

A pyramid or cone holds exactly one third of the prism or cylinder on the same base with the same height. Surface area uses the slant height s; volume uses the perpendicular height h, and a right triangle hidden inside the solid connects the two.

I can…
  • explain why a pyramid (or cone) holds exactly \tfrac13 of the prism (or cylinder) with the same base and height
  • tell the height h from the slant height s, and find one from the other with the Pythagorean theorem on the cross-section
  • calculate the surface area of a pyramid (base + triangular faces) and of a cone, SA=\pi r^2+\pi rs, with or without the base
  • calculate volumes with V=\tfrac13A_{\text{Base}}h and V=\tfrac13\pi r^2h, including a hexagonal base in exact form
  • predict how a cone’s volume changes when its height or radius is scaled, without the formula
  • find the volume and surface area of a composite object, leaving out the hidden faces
M3 Solve problems, using SI and imperial units, that involve the surface area and volume of 3-D objects, including: right cones; right cylinders; right prisms; right pyramids; spheres.

1

Right Pyramids

A pyramid has a polygon base and triangular side faces that meet at a single point, the apex. A right pyramid is a pyramid whose apex is directly above the centre of its base. If the base is a regular polygon, the side faces are congruent isosceles triangles; if the base is a rectangle, opposite side faces match in pairs. Two measurements matter:

  • h = the height: the perpendicular distance from the apex straight down to the centre of the base.
  • s = the slant height: the distance from the apex down along a triangular face to the midpoint of a base edge.

The base can be any polygon (rectangular, triangular, square, pentagonal, hexagonal, …); the figure shows a regular pentagonal base. Unless stated otherwise, “pyramid” in this course always means right pyramid.

h, s and the distance from the centre of the base to the midpoint of an edge (half the side, for a square base) are the three sides of a right triangle standing inside the pyramid, with s as the hypotenuse. So s is always longer than h.
2

Volume of a Right Pyramid

In a woodworking class, Mr. Halden’s students built a rectangular planter box (a right prism) and a pyramid-shaped funnel on an identical rectangular base and with the same height. A student filled the funnel with rice and emptied it into the planter box, then repeated this twice more. On the third emptying the box was filled exactly to the top: no rice left over, no room left.

The experiment illustrates a general fact: a pyramid’s volume is always exactly \tfrac13 of the volume of a prism sharing the same base and the same height, whatever shape the base is. The figure shows a right pyramid (heavy lines) sharing its base and height with the surrounding prism (light lines).

Discuss How does the volume of a pyramid compare with the volume of a prism that has an identical base and the same height?
The pyramid holds exactly one third of the prism. Three funnels filled the box exactly, so 3V_{\text{pyramid}}=V_{\text{prism}}, giving V_{\text{pyramid}}=\tfrac13V_{\text{prism}}=\tfrac13A_{\text{Base}}h.
All that is needed is that the two solids share a base and share a height; the shape of the base is irrelevant, so the same \tfrac13 holds for a triangular, pentagonal or hexagonal base.
Example 1

Square-based pyramid: volume from h, surface area from s

A glass pyramid-shaped skylight sits above a museum atrium. It has a square base with sides measuring 16 m and a height of 6 m.

(a) Make a sketch of the pyramid, labelling the given measurements, and calculate its volume to the nearest cubic metre.
Volume uses the perpendicular height h=6 m; the slant height plays no part here.
Base area A_{\text{Base}}=16^2=256 m2, so
V=\tfrac13A_{\text{Base}}h=\tfrac13(256)(6)=\tfrac13(1\,536)=\boldsymbol{512} m3.
Check: the prism on the same base holds 256\times6=1\,536 m3, and 512 is exactly one third of that.
(b) Make a sketch of the net of the pyramid and calculate the surface area to the nearest square metre. Explain why the base is not included in this calculation.
Surface area uses the slant height, so find it first. Half a base side is 8 m, and h, that half-side and s form a right triangle:
s=\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=\sqrt{36+64}=\sqrt{100}=10 m.
The net is the 16\times16 base square with four congruent triangles folded out from its edges, each of base 16 m and height s=10 m.
The base is not counted: it sits on the roof of the atrium, is never seen and is not glazed, so it contributes no glass area.
Each triangular face: \tfrac12(16)(10)=80 m2, so SA=4(80)=\boldsymbol{320} m2.
Volume: 16x²×6÷3ENTER gives 512. Slant height: √(6x²+8x²)ENTER gives 10. Close the bracket: the root covers the whole sum.

Example 1: unfold the skylight

The surface area is the area of the net: fold the four glass triangles down flat around the base and add up what is really there.

3

Right Circular Cones

A right circular cone has a circular base and an apex directly above the centre of that base. As with a pyramid, h is the perpendicular height and s is the slant height: here the straight-line distance along the curved surface from the apex to any point on the edge of the base circle. The base radius is r. Since h, r and s are the two legs and the hypotenuse of a right triangle,

s^2=h^2+r^2.

Unless stated otherwise, “cone” in this course always means right circular cone.

4

Volume of a Right Circular Cone

A cone and a cylinder built on an identical circular base with the same height behave exactly as the pyramid and prism did: filling the cone with water and pouring it into the cylinder, three full cones are needed to fill the cylinder exactly. A cone’s volume is always exactly \tfrac13 of the volume of a cylinder sharing its base and height; the same \tfrac13 relationship holds for every radius and height.

Discuss How does the volume of a cone compare with the volume of a cylinder that has an identical base and the same height?
The cone holds exactly one third of the cylinder. Three coneloads fill the cylinder, so V_{\text{cone}}=\tfrac13V_{\text{cylinder}}=\tfrac13\pi r^2h.
As with the pyramid and prism, only a shared base and a shared height are needed; the particular values of r and h do not change the ratio.
5

Formulas for Surface Area and Volume

Surface Area: uses the slant height s
SA_{\text{pyramid}}=\text{Area of Base}+\text{Area of Triangular Faces}
SA_{\text{cone}}=\text{Area of Base}+\text{Area of Curved Surface}=\pi r^2+\pi rs
Volume: uses the perpendicular height h
V_{\text{pyramid}}=\tfrac13(\text{Volume of prism})=\tfrac13A_{\text{Base}}h
V_{\text{cone}}=\tfrac13(\text{Volume of cylinder})=\tfrac13\pi r^2h
where A_{\text{Base}} is the area of the pyramid’s base.

Why the curved surface of a cone is \pi rs

Cut the cone along one slant edge and unroll it. The curved surface flattens into a sector (a “pizza slice”) of a circle whose radius is the slant height s. The arc of the sector is the edge that wrapped around the base, so its length is the base circumference, 2\pi r.

The sector is the fraction \dfrac{\text{arc}}{\text{full circumference}}=\dfrac{2\pi r}{2\pi s}=\dfrac{r}{s} of the full circle of radius s, whose area is \pi s^2. So

\text{curved area}=\dfrac{r}{s}\times\pi s^2=\pi rs.

Adding the circular base gives SA_{\text{cone}}=\pi r^2+\pi rs.

!
Which height? Volume takes the perpendicular height h; surface area takes the slant height s. If the one you need isn’t given, find it with s^2=h^2+r^2 (for a square pyramid, use half the side instead of r). And check whether you were given the diameter: the formulas use the radius.
Example 2

Cone: h given, s found by Pythagoras

A solid wooden cone (a closed solid, so its base counts) has a height of 12 cm and a base diameter of 10 cm. Determine the volume and the surface area of the cone, each to one decimal place.

The diameter is 10 cm, so r=5 cm. Volume wants h=12 cm; surface area wants s, which is not given, so get it from Pythagoras:
s=\sqrt{r^2+h^2}=\sqrt{5^2+12^2}=\sqrt{25+144}=\sqrt{169}=13 cm.
V=\tfrac13\pi r^2h=\tfrac13\pi(25)(12)=100\pi=314.159\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{314.2} cm3 (uses h).
SA=\pi r^2+\pi rs=\pi(25)+\pi(5)(13)=25\pi+65\pi=90\pi=282.743\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{282.7} cm2 (uses s).
The cone is solid, so the base is included. An open paper cone of the same size would need only the curved surface, \pi rs=65\pi\approx204.2 cm2.
Volume: π×5x²×12÷3ENTER gives 314.1592654. Surface area: π×5x²+π×5×13ENTER gives 282.7433388. Round only at the end.
Example 3

Hexagonal base: exact area, then volume

A right pyramid has a height of 12 cm and a regular hexagonal base with sides measuring 6 cm.

(a) Make a sketch of the hexagonal base and determine its area as an exact value (in simplest radical form) and to the nearest square centimetre.
The centre-to-vertex distance of a regular hexagon equals its side length, so the hexagon splits into 6 equilateral triangles of side 6 cm meeting at the centre.
Each triangle: height \sqrt{6^2-3^2}=\sqrt{27}=3\sqrt3, so area \tfrac12(6)(3\sqrt3)=9\sqrt3 cm2 (the same as \tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}(6)^2=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}(36)).
A_{\text{hex}}=6(9\sqrt3)=\boldsymbol{54\sqrt3} cm2 exactly, and 54\sqrt3=93.53\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{94} cm2.
(b) The pyramid is filled completely with water. Determine the volume of water it holds, to the nearest millilitre.
Volume uses the perpendicular height h=12 cm (no slant height is involved), and the exact base area from (a) so nothing is lost to early rounding.
V=\tfrac13A_{\text{Base}}h=\tfrac13(54\sqrt3)(12)=216\sqrt3=374.122\ldots cm3.
Since 1 cm3 =1 mL, the pyramid holds 374 mL.

Example 4

Scaling a cone’s volume without the formula

Use the information below to answer both parts.

Problem 1: Two cones have radii of equal length. The first cone has a height of 6 cm and a volume of 15 cm3. State the volume of the second cone if its height is 24 cm.
Problem 2: Two cones have the same height. The first cone has a radius of 4 cm and a volume of 20 cm3. State the volume of the second cone if its radius is 12 cm.
(a) Explain how Problem 1 can be solved using mental math (without the volume formula), and state the answer.
In V=\tfrac13\pi r^2h the radius, and so r^2, is unchanged, which leaves V directly proportional to h alone.
The height goes from 6 cm to 24 cm, a factor of 24\div6=4, so the volume is multiplied by the same factor of 4:
V_2=4(15)=\boldsymbol{60} cm3. The formula is never evaluated.
(b) Explain how Problem 2 can be solved using mental math (without the volume formula), and state the answer.
Here the height is unchanged, so V is proportional to r^2: the radius enters squared.
The radius goes from 4 cm to 12 cm, a factor of 12\div4=3, so the volume is multiplied by 3^2=9:
V_2=9(20)=\boldsymbol{180} cm3. Note the factor is 9, not 3.
6

Composite Objects

A composite object is built from two or more simple solids. Its volume is the sum of the volumes of the parts. For its surface area, add the outside surfaces of the parts, but leave out any face where two parts are joined: that shared face is hidden inside the object, so it is removed from both parts.
Example 5

A shed: rectangular prism + square pyramid roof

A garden shed is a rectangular prism with a 3 m by 3 m floor and walls 2.4 m high, topped by a right square pyramid roof on the same 3 m by 3 m square, with a height of 1.2 m.

(a) Determine the volume of the shed.
Prism: V=A_{\text{Base}}h=(3)(3)(2.4)=21.6 m3.
Pyramid: V=\tfrac13A_{\text{Base}}h=\tfrac13(9)(1.2)=3.6 m3.
Total V=21.6+3.6=\boldsymbol{25.2} m3.
(b) Determine the surface area of the shed (including the floor), to the nearest tenth of a square metre. Which face is not counted, and why?
The top of the prism and the base of the roof are the same 3\times3 square, hidden inside the shed, so neither is counted.
Prism part: floor 3\times3=9 m2 plus four walls 4(3)(2.4)=28.8 m2.
Roof slant height: the horizontal leg is half a side, 1.5 m, so s=\sqrt{1.2^2+1.5^2}=\sqrt{3.69}=1.9209\ldots m.
Four triangles: 4\cdot\tfrac12(3)(1.9209\ldots)=11.5256\ldots m2.
SA=9+28.8+11.5256\ldots=49.3256\ldots\approx\boldsymbol{49.3} m2.
Keep the slant height in the calculator. √(1.2x²+1.5x²)ENTER gives 1.920937271. Then the whole surface area in one line: 9+28.8+4×3×√3.69)÷2ENTER gives 49.32562463.
✓

Key ideas

One third. A pyramid holds \tfrac13 of the prism, and a cone \tfrac13 of the cylinder, with the same base and height: V=\tfrac13A_{\text{Base}}h, V=\tfrac13\pi r^2h. Scale the height by k: volume \times k. Scale the radius by k: volume \times k^2.
h for volume, s for surface area. Inside the solid, h, r (or half the side) and s make a right triangle: s^2=h^2+r^2. The slant height is the hypotenuse, so it is always the longer one.
Surface area = the net. Pyramid: base + triangles \tfrac12(\text{side})(s). Cone: \pi r^2+\pi rs; the curved part unrolls to a sector, the fraction \tfrac rs of a circle of radius s. Leave out a base that isn’t there.
Composite objects. Volume: add the parts. Surface area: add the outside surfaces only; a face where two parts join is hidden, so it comes off both parts. Use exact values (\pi, \sqrt3) and round once at the end.
