Where Graphs Meet the Axes

Two places on any graph deserve a closer look: where it meets the horizontal axis and where it meets the vertical axis. Today: what those points are called, how to find them from an equation (substitute y=0, substitute x=0), and what they mean in a real situation.

I can…
  • identify the x- and y-intercepts of a graph, and tell an intercept (a value) from an intercept point (an ordered pair)
  • determine intercepts algebraically: substitute y=0 for the x-intercept(s) and substitute x=0 for the y-intercept(s), whatever form the equation is in
  • find the intercepts of lines, circles and parabolas (factoring when I need to)
  • explain why a line can have one, two or infinitely many intercept points
  • interpret intercepts in context, and tell interpolation from extrapolation
RF1 Interpret and explain the relationships among data, graphs and situations.
RF5 Determine the characteristics of the graphs of linear relations, including the intercepts, slope, domain and range.
Warm-up

The High Dive

A diver jumps off a tower. The graph shows her height above the water.

1
How high is the diving platform?
10 m. The graph starts on the vertical axis at (0,\ 10).
2
After how many seconds does she hit the water?
2 s. The graph meets the horizontal axis (the water) at (2,\ 0).
3
Fill in the blanks. (tap a blank)
At the splash, height = 0 m.
At the jump, time = 0 s.
Both blanks are 0. That is the whole lesson in two blanks: where a graph meets one axis, the other coordinate is zero.
!
The big one. The graph’s rule is h=10+5t-5t^2. Setting h=0 gives two answers: t=2 and t=-1. What does t=-1 mean for the dive? Guess first.
A She goes 1 m under water.
B She splashes 1 s early.
C Nothing: it’s before the jump.
C: nothing. 0=10+5t-5t^2=-5(t^2-t-2)=-5(t-2)(t+1), so t=2 or t=-1. But t=-1 is one second before she jumped. She was standing on the platform, so the rule doesn’t describe her then: the model only fits 0\le t\le 2.
A mixes up the variables: -1 is a time, not a height. B reads the minus sign as “early”, but she can’t splash before she jumps.
Bonus trap: the highest point is not an intercept. She rises to 11.25 m at t=0.5 s before falling.
+
Early finishers. A 3 m springboard diver follows h=3+2t-5t^2. Find both intercepts and say what each one means.
t=0: h=3, the board. h=0: 0=-(5t+3)(t-1), so t=1 s is the splash; t=-0.6 has no meaning.

Act it out with your hand: platform, arc, splash! Point to where the graph meets each axis.

1

Inputs, Outputs, and Graphs

Every ordered pair (x,\ y) of a relation has a first coordinate, the input (a value of the independent variable, usually x), and a second coordinate, the output (a value of the dependent variable, usually y, because its value depends on the input chosen). Graphing all the ordered pairs traces out a curve (a line, a parabola, a circle), and two places on that curve are worth special attention: where it crosses the horizontal axis, and where it crosses the vertical axis.

The line crosses the x-axis at (-2.5,\ 0) and the y-axis at (0,\ 1.5). These points are called intercept points.
2

Definitions and the Algebraic Method

x-intercept: the x-coordinate of a point where a graph meets the x-axis. It may cross the axis or just touch it (the parabola y=x^2 touches the x-axis at the origin). At every such point the y-coordinate equals zero.
y-intercept: the y-coordinate of a point where a graph meets (crosses or touches) the y-axis. At every such point the x-coordinate equals zero.
Value or point? In this unit the intercept is the value and the intercept point is the ordered pair: for the line in Section 1, the x-intercept is -2.5 and the x-intercept point is (-2.5,\ 0).

To determine intercepts algebraically, given the equation of a relation:
x-intercept(s)
substitute \boldsymbol{y=0}
and solve for x
the point is (\,?,\ 0)
y-intercept(s)
substitute \boldsymbol{x=0}
and solve for y
the point is (0,\ ?\,)

The equation of a relation can be written in several equivalent forms: y=\tfrac{4}{3}x+8 and 4x-3y+24=0 describe the very same line. Whatever the form, the same substitution strategy finds the intercepts.

!
The zero goes in the other spot. An x-intercept of 5 is the point (5,\ 0), not (0,\ 5). The point is on the x-axis, so its y-coordinate is the zero.
Example 1

A line with both variables on one side

A line has equation 4x-3y+24=0. Algebraically determine the x-intercept and the y-intercept of the line. Write each intercept point as an ordered pair.

x-intercept
Substitute y=0:
4x-3(0)+24=0
4x=-24
x=-6
x-intercept \boldsymbol{-6}; point \boldsymbol{(-6,\ 0)}
y-intercept
Substitute x=0:
4(0)-3y+24=0
-3y=-24
y=8
y-intercept \boldsymbol{8}; point \boldsymbol{(0,\ 8)}

Same line, two forms

Example 1’s equation 4x-3y+24=0 and y=\tfrac{4}{3}x+8 are the same line written two ways. Rearranging, a table of values and the graph all agree, so either form gives the same intercepts.

Example 2

A circle has two of each

The equation of a circle is x^2+y^2=36. Calculate the x-intercept(s) and y-intercept(s) of its graph. Write each intercept point as an ordered pair.

x-intercept(s)
Substitute y=0:
x^2+0^2=36
x^2=36, so x=\pm6.
x-intercepts \boldsymbol{\pm6}; points \boldsymbol{(6,\ 0)} and \boldsymbol{(-6,\ 0)}
y-intercept(s)
Substitute x=0:
0^2+y^2=36
y^2=36, so y=\pm6.
y-intercepts \boldsymbol{\pm6}; points \boldsymbol{(0,\ 6)} and \boldsymbol{(0,\ -6)}
!
Two square roots. x^2=36 has two solutions, 6 and -6, because (-6)^2=36 too. Forget the \pm and you lose half the intercepts of the circle.
Example 3

A parabola: factor to find the x-intercepts

A parabola has equation y=x^2-2x-24. Algebraically determine the x-intercept(s) and the y-intercept of the parabola. Write each intercept point as an ordered pair.

x-intercept(s)
Substitute y=0 and factor (Unit 4):
0=x^2-2x-24=(x-6)(x+4)
x=6 or x=-4.
x-intercepts \boldsymbol{6} and \boldsymbol{-4}; points \boldsymbol{(6,\ 0)} and \boldsymbol{(-4,\ 0)}
y-intercept
Substitute x=0:
y=0^2-2(0)-24=-24
y-intercept \boldsymbol{-24}; point \boldsymbol{(0,\ -24)}
Think The parabola has two x-intercepts but only one y-intercept. Why can’t it have two y-intercepts?
Substituting x=0 into y=x^2-2x-24 gives exactly one output, -24: one input, one output. Substituting y=0 asks the question backwards, and two different inputs (6 and −4) can give the same output 0.
Check an intercept: it must give y=0. Put x=-4 into x^2-2x-24, with brackets around the negative: ((−)4)x²−2×(−)4−24ENTER gives 0 ✓

Meets means crosses or touches

How many x-intercepts a graph has depends on how it sits against the axis. Slide a parabola down (or a circle across) and watch the count change. Substituting tells the same story: no real solution means the graph misses the axis.

3

Lines With One, Two, or Infinitely Many Intercepts

Most lines cross both axes, so they have two intercept points. Some lines do not.

Example 4

Horizontal, vertical, and through-the-origin lines

For each line, state the x-intercept(s) and the y-intercept(s), if any, and the number of intercept points. (Line (c), y=0, is the x-axis itself.)

(a) y=3
y=0 gives 0=3: impossible, so no x-intercept (parallel to the x-axis). x=0 gives y=3: y-intercept 3. One intercept point, (0,\ 3).
(b) x=-2
y=0 gives x=-2: x-intercept -2. x=0 gives 0=-2: impossible, so no y-intercept. One intercept point, (-2,\ 0).
(c) y=0
Every point of this line has y=0, so every point is on the x-axis: infinitely many x-intercepts. x=0 gives y=0: y-intercept 0.
(d) y=2x
y=0 gives x=0, and x=0 gives y=0: both intercepts are 0, at the same point. One intercept point, the origin (0,\ 0).
Summary. A line can have two intercept points; exactly one (a horizontal or vertical line that is not an axis, or a slanted line through the origin); or infinitely many (the line is the x-axis, y=0, or the y-axis, x=0).
Think Can a line have no intercepts at all?
No. A line that misses the x-axis must be parallel to it, so it is horizontal, and every horizontal line crosses the y-axis. Every line has at least one intercept point.
4

Interpreting Intercepts in Applied Situations

When a relation models a real situation its intercepts carry meaning tied to the context. Usually the vertical-axis intercept is the start (the input is 0) and the horizontal-axis intercept is where the output runs out (reaches 0).

Interpolation: reading a value off a graph when it lies between two plotted points.
Extrapolation: reading a value when the graph must be extended beyond the plotted points.
Substituting into the formula always gives an exact value. Reading from a graph gives only an estimate.
!
Check every answer against the story. Algebra can produce an intercept with no meaning, like t=-1 for the diver: a time before the story starts. Say so, and reject it.
Example 5

A descending hot-air balloon

A hot-air balloon begins a steady descent. Its height above the ground, h metres, after t minutes (t\ge0) is given by h=800-40t.

(a) Complete the table of values, then plot the ordered pairs on the grid and connect them with a straight line.
h(0)=800, h(5)=800-200=600, h(10)=800-400=400, h(15)=800-600=200 (tap the table cells). The line is extended to the extra point (20,\ 0), where it meets the t-axis.
(b) What does the ordered pair (0,\ 800) represent in the context of this problem?
At t=0 no time has passed, so (0,\ 800) says the balloon is 800 m above the ground at the instant the descent begins. It is the h-intercept point of the graph.
(c) Determine the t-intercept of the graph algebraically. What does the t-intercept represent in the context of this problem?
Substitute h=0: 0=800-40t\Rightarrow 40t=800\Rightarrow t=20. The t-intercept is \boldsymbol{20} (the point (20,\ 0)): the balloon reaches the ground 20 minutes after the descent begins.
(d) Use the formula to calculate the exact height of the balloon after 12 minutes.
h=800-40(12)
=800-480
=\boldsymbol{320} m
(e) Use the formula to determine how many minutes it takes the balloon to reach a height of 160 metres.
160=800-40t
40t=640
t=\boldsymbol{16} minutes
(f) The table stops at t=15. Is finding the height at t=12 in (d) an example of interpolation or extrapolation? What about finding the time in (e)?
(d) t=12 lies between the plotted points t=10 and t=15: interpolation. (e) The answer t=16 lies beyond the last plotted point (t=15): extrapolation.
(d) on the calculator. 800−40×12ENTER gives 320 (the calculator multiplies first). For (e), undo the steps: t=(800-160)\div40.

Interpolation or extrapolation?

The plotted points of Example 5 run from t=0 to t=15. Inside that stretch we interpolate; outside it we extrapolate, and extrapolating too far can leave the story altogether.

✓

Key ideas

Where a graph meets the axes. On the x-axis, y=0; on the y-axis, x=0. The intercept is the value (-6); the intercept point is the pair ((-6,\ 0)).
Algebraically: x-intercept(s): substitute y=0. y-intercept(s): substitute x=0. Any form of the equation works. Squares give \pm; trinomials factor.
Lines: two intercept points, exactly one (horizontal or vertical, not an axis, or through the origin), or infinitely many (the line is an axis).
In context: the vertical intercept is usually the start, the horizontal one where it runs out. Reject answers outside the story. Between plotted points: interpolation; beyond: extrapolation. Formula = exact, graph = estimate.
