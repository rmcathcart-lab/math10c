Exploring Real-World Relations With a Graphing Calculator

A formula from real life (a kayak losing value, a stunt performer flying through the air) graphs as a line or a parabola that goes on forever. The calculator gives the math answer; the situation decides which part of it makes sense. Today: choose a window that fits, read the intercepts, trace, intersect and the vertex, and trim the domain and range to the story.

I can…
  • choose a viewing window (Xmin, Xmax, Ymin, Ymax) that fits the quantities in a situation
  • find the intercepts of a real-world graph and explain what each one means (usually the start or the end of the story)
  • use trace (or 1:value) for the output at a given input, and intersect for the input that gives a given output
  • find the vertex of a parabola with 4:maximum, a landing time with 2:zero, and use the axis of symmetry
  • state a domain and range that make sense in context, and reject math answers that don’t (negative time, negative height)
RF1 Interpret and explain the relationships among data, graphs and situations.
RF5 Determine the characteristics of the graphs of linear relations, including the intercepts, slope, domain and range.
Warm-up

Stopping Distance

Two cars slam on the brakes at the same line. How far until they stop?

Braking-distance model (dry road): \boldsymbol{d=0.006\,v^{2}}  d in metres, v in km/h
1
Use the model: how far does the 50 km/h car skid? Check the ruler.
d=0.006(50)^2=0.006(2500)=15 m. Yes: the skid marks end at 15 m on the ruler.
2
How far does a car skid from 30 km/h (a school zone)?
d=0.006(30)^2=0.006(900)=5.4 m.
3
Graphing d=0.006v^2 on a graphing calculator, should Xmin be negative? Why?
No. A speed can’t be negative, so Xmin=0. A sensible window: x{:}\,[0,\,120,\,10], y{:}\,[0,\,100,\,10].
!
The big one. The orange car goes twice as fast. About how far does it skid before it stops? Guess first.
A about 30 m
B about 40 m
C about 60 m
D about 100 m
C: about 60 m. d=0.006(100)^2=0.006(10\,000)=60 m, which is 4 times the 15 m at 50 km/h. Why: the speed is squared. Doubling v multiplies v^2 by 2^2=4.
A (30 m) is the “double the speed, double the distance” trap: it treats the relation as a straight line. B is a “bit more than double” hedge. D overshoots, usually from “100 km/h means 100 m”.
A simplified dry-road model: real braking distances depend on the car, tires and road, but they do grow roughly with the square of the speed. Total stopping distance also adds the reaction distance before the brakes go on.
+
Early finishers. What speed gives a braking distance of 24 m?
0.006v^2=24\Rightarrow v^2=4000\Rightarrow v\approx63 km/h.

Hands up for A, B, C or D. Then find someone who voted differently and convince them.

Setting a Useful Window

Today’s keystrokes are for the graphing calculator (TI-83/84). The keys look like Y= WINDOW 2ND TRACE, and the screens on this page are drawn like its screen. Your TI-30XIIS can’t graph, but it can still check any answer by substitution (the grey “Show me” tips).

The viewing window is the visible span along each axis, set by Xmin, Xmax, Ymin and Ymax. Adjust it until the part of the graph that matters for the situation is on screen. A ZoomFit-style feature (ZOOM 0) can suggest a starting window, but nudge it so the bounds suit the quantities involved: if the input is time in seconds, a negative Xmin rarely makes sense.

A window is written x{:}\,[\text{Xmin},\,\text{Xmax},\,\text{Xscl}], y{:}\,[\text{Ymin},\,\text{Ymax},\,\text{Yscl}]; Xscl and Yscl are the spacing of the tick marks. In context, start each axis at 0 unless the quantity can really be negative, and stop a little past the biggest value that matters.
Graphing calculator. Y= type the rule (the input is always X) · ZOOM 6 standard window x{:}\,[-10,10,1], y{:}\,[-10,10,1] · ZOOM 0 ZoomFit · WINDOW type your own values, then GRAPH.

Intercepts: the start and the end of the story

Locate the intercepts, where the graph crosses each axis, right away. For a relation with input t and output y, the t-intercept occurs where y=0 and the y-intercept occurs where t=0. In context these are renamed to match the variables in the problem (a V-intercept, an h-intercept), and each usually represents the starting point or the ending point of the scenario.

On the output axis
the input is 0: substitute t=0
usually the start: the value when new, the launch height
On the input axis
the output is 0: substitute V=0 and solve
often the end: worth $0, landing on the ground

Given the input, or given the output?

The calculator tools are the ones from Lesson 5. Trace (or 1:value, or the table) reads the output at a given input. Use intersect (with Y_2 set to the target value) for a given output: it answers “when does the quantity reach this value?”

Forwards: input → output
TRACE, type the input, ENTER
or 2ND TRACE 1:value
Backwards: output → input
Y= Y_2= the target, then 2ND TRACE 5:intersect
First curve? ENTER Second curve? ENTER Guess? ENTER
No graphing calculator? Check by substituting (TI-30XIIS). The kayak after 4 years: 3000−200×4ENTER gives 2200. Check the intersect answer the same way: t=7.5 should give 1500.

The math answer vs the answer that makes sense

An equation doesn’t know what it describes. V=3000-200t graphs as a whole line, for every real t: that is the math answer. The situation only uses a piece of it, from the start of the story to its end. That piece decides a domain and range that make sense.

Math answer
Makes sense in context
the whole line: t can be any real number, even -3 or 40
only from purchase (t=0) to worth $0 (t=15): no negative ages, no negative values
!
Dots or a line? Age and value change continuously (any decimal makes sense), so the graph is a solid segment and we write t\in\mathbb{R}. If the input could only be whole numbers (jerseys, tickets), the graph would be separate dots.
Example 1

A depreciating value: intercepts, trace and intersect

An outfitter purchases a new kayak for $3000. The value of the kayak depreciates according to the formula V=3000-200t, where V is the value of the kayak in dollars and t is its age in years.

(a) Graph V=3000-200t using a graphing calculator. State a window setting appropriate for this scenario, and sketch the graph on the grid below.
Xmin=Xmax=Ymin=Ymax=
t is an age in years and V a value in dollars, so both are non-negative, and the value never exceeds the $3000 purchase price:
Xmin=0, Xmax=16, Ymin=0, Ymax=3200
(windows may vary: anything that shows both intercepts and no negatives works)
(b) Determine the t-intercept of the graph, and describe what it represents in the context of this scenario.
The t-intercept occurs where V=0:
0=3000-200t\Rightarrow 200t=3000\Rightarrow t=15
The t-intercept is \boldsymbol{(15,\ 0)}: after 15 years this model predicts the kayak has depreciated to a value of $0.
(c) Determine the V-intercept of the graph, and describe what it represents in the context of this scenario.
The V-intercept occurs where t=0:
V=3000-200(0)=3000
The V-intercept is \boldsymbol{(0,\ 3000)}: the kayak’s original purchase price was $3000.
(d) Use the trace feature (or direct substitution) to determine the value of the kayak after 4 years.
Trace to t=4 (or substitute):
V=3000-200(4)=3000-800=\boldsymbol{\$2200}
(e) Use the intersect feature (or algebra) to determine after how many years the kayak will be worth half of its original price.
Half the original price is 3000\div2=1500. With Y_2=1500, 5:intersect gives (7.5,\ 1500).
Algebraically: 1500=3000-200t\Rightarrow 200t=1500\Rightarrow t=\boldsymbol{7.5} years.
(f) State an appropriate domain and range for this relation.
The model is meaningful from the purchase date, t=0, until the value reaches $0 at t=15, and both quantities vary continuously:
D=\{t\mid 0\le t\le15,\ t\in\mathbb{R}\},    R=\{V\mid 0\le V\le3000,\ V\in\mathbb{R}\}
Think The equation gives V=3000-200(17)=-400. What does that tell you about the kayak?
Nothing real: a kayak can’t be worth -\$400. It’s a math answer from a part of the line the situation doesn’t use. The model stops making sense at t=15, which is why the domain stops there.

Quadratic Relations: Vertex, Maximum, and Symmetry

Many real quantities (the height of a thrown or launched object, for example) change according to a quadratic relation, whose graph is a parabola. A parabola has a single turning point, the vertex, which is either the highest point (a maximum) or the lowest point (a minimum) on the graph.

The parabola is symmetric about a vertical line through the vertex, the axis of symmetry. That’s why two different inputs on either side of the vertex produce the same output.

On the graphing calculator: maximum and zero

The intercepts, a value found by trace, and an appropriate domain and range are all still meaningful. The vertex, located with 4:maximum or 3:minimum exactly as in Lesson 5, Section 9, now answers a question of its own, such as “what is the greatest height reached?” A landing time is an x-intercept: use 2:zero.

Graphing calculator. 2ND TRACE opens CALC. Choose 4:maximum (or 2:zero), then answer three questions: Left Bound? move left of the point, ENTER · Right Bound? move right of it, ENTER · Guess? ENTER.
!
The calculator doesn’t know the story. 8+10t-t^2=0 has two solutions, t\approx-0.74 and t\approx10.74. Only the second makes sense: t\approx-0.74 is before Blaze was launched. Always ask “does this answer fit the situation?”
Check the vertex (TI-30XIIS). 8+10×5−5x²ENTER gives 33. At the landing time, h(10.74) should be very close to 0.
Example 2

A launched stunt performer: vertex, landing time and symmetry

A stunt performer named Blaze is launched from a spring-loaded platform on top of a tower. Blaze’s height above the ground, in metres, can be modelled by h=8+10t-t^2, where t is the time in seconds after launch. Blaze lands in an airbag whose top surface is level with the ground.

(a) Graph h=8+10t-t^2 using a graphing calculator. State a window setting appropriate for this scenario, and sketch the graph on the grid below.
Xmin=Xmax=Ymin=Ymax=
Time and height are both non-negative, and the graph only matters until Blaze lands:
Xmin=0, Xmax=11, Ymin=0, Ymax=35
(windows may vary)
(b) What is the height of the platform above the ground?
The platform height is the height at t=0 (the h-intercept):
h=8+10(0)-(0)^2=\boldsymbol{8} m.
(c) What is the maximum height Blaze reaches above the ground, and how many seconds after launch does this occur?
4:maximum (2ND TRACE), with bounds on either side of the top, gives t\approx5, h=33.
Check: h(5)=8+10(5)-5^2=8+50-25=33 ✓
Maximum height 33 m, reached 5 s after launch.
(d) To the nearest hundredth of a second, how long does it take Blaze to land in the airbag?
Blaze lands when h=0: the t-intercept. 2:zero, with bounds on either side of the right-hand crossing, gives t\approx10.7446, so t\approx\boldsymbol{10.74} s.
Check: h(10.74)=8+107.4-115.35\approx0.05\approx0 ✓
(The other zero, t\approx-0.74, is before launch: rejected.)
(e) How high above the ground is Blaze 2 seconds after launch? At what other time is Blaze at this same height again?
At t=2: h=8+20-4=\boldsymbol{24} m.
Solve 8+10t-t^2=24: t^2-10t+16=0\Rightarrow(t-2)(t-8)=0, so t=2 or t=8.
Blaze is back at 24 m at t=8 s: the two times are symmetric about the vertex at t=5.
(f) State an appropriate domain and range for this relation.
Using the landing time from (d), with time and height both continuous:
D=\{t\mid 0\le t\le10.74,\ t\in\mathbb{R}\},    R=\{h\mid 0\le h\le33,\ h\in\mathbb{R}\}
Think Blaze starts at 8 m. Why does the range start at 0 and not at 8?
The range is every height Blaze actually reaches, lowest to highest. After the top (33 m), Blaze falls past the platform height all the way down to the airbag at ground level, 0 m. The starting value isn’t the smallest value.

Which calculator tool answers the question?

Read what the question gives and what it asks for. An input given means trace or value; an output given means intersect; “zero”, “lands” or “worth $0” means zero; “greatest” or “least” means maximum or minimum.

✓

Key ideas

A window that fits: no negative time or height unless it’s real, and stop just past the biggest value that matters. ZoomFit is a start; then nudge. Kayak: x{:}\,[0,16], y{:}\,[0,3200].
Intercepts tell the story: set the other variable to 0. V-intercept (0,\ 3000), the purchase price (start); t-intercept (15,\ 0), worth $0 (end). A landing time is a zero.
Pick the tool: given an input → TRACE / 1:value; given an output → Y_2 + 5:intersect; output 0 → 2:zero; highest → 4:maximum. A parabola is symmetric about its vertex: h(2)=h(8)=24.
Math answer vs makes sense: the equation goes on forever; keep the piece the situation uses. D=\{t\mid 0\le t\le10.74,\ t\in\mathbb{R}\}, R=\{h\mid 0\le h\le33,\ h\in\mathbb{R}\}, lowest to highest output. Reject t\approx-0.74.
