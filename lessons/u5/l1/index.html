The Cartesian Plane, Growing Patterns, and Types of Variables

Two numbers are enough to pin down any point on a flat surface. Today: the Cartesian plane and its four quadrants, a matchstick pattern described three ways (a table, a graph and an equation), and the question behind every graph in this unit: join the dots, or not?

The Cartesian Coordinate System

The word “Cartesian” honours the French mathematician and philosopher René Descartes (1596–1650), who in 1637 introduced the idea of locating a point on a flat surface using two perpendicular reference lines.

A Cartesian coordinate system is two number lines, called axes, that cross at right angles. The horizontal axis is the x-axis and the vertical axis is the y-axis; together they form the xy-plane, also called the Cartesian plane. The axes cross at the origin, usually labelled O.
Every point has a unique ordered pair of numbers, its coordinates. The first number, the x-coordinate, tells how far to move horizontally from the origin; the second, the y-coordinate, tells how far to move vertically. The point P(5,\ 2) is 5 units right and 2 units up from the origin.
The axes divide the plane into four quadrants, numbered I to IV, starting at the top right and moving counter-clockwise.

Plotting a point: two moves from the origin

Read an ordered pair as directions. Start at the origin, move across by the x-coordinate (right if positive, left if negative), then move up or down by the y-coordinate.

!
Across first, then up or down. (2,\ 5) and (5,\ 2) are different points: that is why it’s called an ordered pair. A coordinate of 0 means no move in that direction: (0,\ -3) sits on the y-axis.

The four quadrants and their signs

The quadrant a point lands in depends only on the signs of its coordinates: right means a positive x, up means a positive y.

Describing a Growing Pattern With a Relation

Many real situations produce a pattern of numbers that grows by the same amount at every step. A pattern like this can always be described in three equivalent ways: a table of values, a graph, and an equation.

A single square is built using 4 matchsticks, one for each side. Each additional square is attached along one full side of the previous square, so it needs only 3 new matchsticks to complete its remaining three sides. The pattern continues in this way, forming a row of adjoining squares.

Which number goes first? We choose the number of squares; the number of matchsticks depends on that choice. So s comes first in the ordered pair and goes on the horizontal axis, and M goes on the vertical axis. Lesson 2 names these the independent and dependent variables.

From the pattern to an equation

The table and the graph only go as far as we draw them. An equation works for any number of squares, including ones too big to draw. Counting the matchsticks in a clever way gives the equation.

Forwards and backwards. Forwards (squares → matchsticks): multiply by 3, then add 1. Backwards (matchsticks → squares): undo in reverse order: subtract 1, then divide by 3. (91−1)÷3ENTER

Continuous Data and Linear Relations

Not every relation between two variables behaves like the matchstick pattern. Some relations involve variables that can take on any value in between two given values. Their graphs are made of connected points, a line or a curve, rather than separate dots.

Example 4

Cost of gasoline

A gas station sells gasoline for a fixed price per litre. The table below shows the cost, C dollars, of purchasing V litres of gasoline.

Volume, V (L)246810
Cost, C ($)2.905.808.7011.6014.50
(a) Represent the data from the table on the grid.
Plot (2,\ 2.90), (4,\ 5.80), …, (10,\ 14.50) and join them (see (b)).
(b) Does it make sense to join the points together with a straight line? Explain.
Yes. You can buy any volume of gas, for example 2.37 L, so the variables are continuous and every in-between value has meaning. Join the points with a straight line.
(c) Use the graph to estimate the cost of purchasing 5 L of gasoline.
V=5 is halfway between V=4 ($5.80) and V=6 ($8.70), so C\approx\boldsymbol{\$7.25}
(d) Use the graph to estimate the volume of gasoline purchased if the cost was $10.15.
$10.15 is between $8.70 (V=6) and $11.60 (V=8), so V\approx\boldsymbol{7} L
(e) Write an equation expressing C in terms of V.
Cost per litre: 2.90\div2=1.45, 5.80\div4=1.45, …
\boldsymbol{C=1.45V}
(f) Use the equation to check the accuracy of your estimates in (c) and (d).
V=5: C=1.45(5)=7.25 ✓
C=10.15: V=\dfrac{10.15}{1.45}=7 ✓
Both estimates were exact.
(g) In the context of the question, what does the value 1.45 represent in your equation?
The price per litre of gasoline: $1.45 is the cost of 1 L.

Reading a graph forwards and backwards

A graph answers questions in both directions. Forwards: from a value on the horizontal axis, go up to the line, then across. Backwards: from a value on the vertical axis, go across to the line, then down. The equation then checks the estimate.

Check (c) and (d) with the equation. Forwards: 1.45×5ENTER gives 7.25. Backwards, divide: 10.15÷1.45ENTER gives 7.

Where the equation comes from: the price per litre

For gasoline, every litre costs the same. Dividing each cost by its volume finds that price, and the price becomes the number in front of V.

4

Discrete and Continuous Variables

Continuous variables can take any value: between any two values there is always another. In Example 4 you can buy 2.37 L of gasoline, so volume and cost are continuous. Graph them with the points connected by a line or curve: every in-between value has meaning.
Discrete variables take only separate, countable values with gaps between them. In Examples 2 and 3 there is no “square number 2.5”, so the number of squares and the number of matchsticks are discrete. Graph them as separate dots: the in-between values mean nothing.

A discrete variable need not have a largest value (a book can have any whole number of pages), but between two allowed values there are gaps.

Count it or measure it?

A quick test: if you get the value by counting, the variable is usually discrete. If you get it by measuring (time, length, mass, volume), it is continuous.

Example 5

Classifying variables

Classify each of the following variables as discrete or continuous.

(a) number of pages in a book
A whole number of pages, nothing in between: discrete.
(b) time taken to run a marathon
Time can be any value, down to fractions of a second (3 h 17 min 42.6 s): continuous.
(c) number of siblings a person has
A whole-number count; no one has 2.5 siblings: discrete.
(d) volume of water in a bathtub
The volume can be any value in a range (73.4 L): continuous.
✓

Key ideas

Ordered pairs: (x,\ y) means move across by x, then up or down by y, starting at the origin O(0,\ 0). Order matters: (2,\ 5)\ne(5,\ 2).
Quadrants I to IV, counter-clockwise from the top right: (+,+), (-,+), (-,-), (+,-). A point on an axis is in no quadrant.
Three descriptions: table (M goes up 3 each time), graph (the points), equation M=3s+1. Use the equation forwards (substitute) and backwards (solve).
Discrete (count it): separate dots, never joined. Continuous (measure it): join the points, because every in-between value means something.
