Interpreting and Sketching Real-World Graphs

Every real-world graph tells a story. Today we read that story piece by piece: where the quantity rises, falls or holds steady, and how fast it changes. Then we run it backwards: hear a story and sketch its graph.

I can…
  • describe what is happening during each interval of a real-world graph: increasing, decreasing or constant
  • find a rate of change from a graph, with units, and explain why steeper means faster
  • explain why a graph of a quantity against time must pass the vertical line test
  • match a description to the shape of a graph (constant rate, slowing down, speeding up, periodic) and suggest a story for a graph
  • sketch a graph with no scale from a written description
RF1 Interpret and explain the relationships among data, graphs and situations.
Warm-up

The Bathtub Graph

Sam takes a bath. The graph shows the water’s depth the whole time.

1
Which segment shows the tap filling the tub?
Segment 1. The depth increases steadily from 0 to 20 cm over 8 minutes.
2
How deep is the water when the tap is turned off?
20 cm. Segment 2 is flat at 20: the tap is off and the depth is constant.
3
Which is faster, filling 1 or draining 6? How do you know?
Draining is faster: segment 6 is steeper. It drops 20 cm in 4.5 min, about 4.4 cm/min. Filling rises 20 cm in 8 min, 2.5 cm/min.
!
The big one. Which segment shows the moment Sam gets into the tub? Guess first.
A segment 1
B segment 3
C segment 4
D segment 5
B, segment 3. Sam’s body pushes the water up (it is displaced), so the depth jumps from 20 to 28 cm almost instantly. Segment 4 (constant at 28 cm) is Sam soaking, segment 5 (a sudden drop back to 20) is Sam getting out, and segment 6 is the plug pulled.
A mixes up Sam and the tap: filling is slow and steady, while getting in is almost vertical. C is Sam staying in, not getting in. D: the water level doesn’t drop as you sink in; it rises.
+
Challenge a neighbour. Sam’s dog jumps in mid-soak, then leaps out. Sketch the new graph.
During segment 4, add a sudden jump up (dog in), a flat piece, then a sudden drop back to 28 cm (dog out). A smaller dog means a smaller jump. (Watch it in the first explainer below: choose “Dog jumps in”.)

With a neighbour: tell the whole bath story, one segment at a time.

1

Increasing, Decreasing, and Constant Behaviour

A real-world graph plots a changing quantity (vertical axis) against time or another variable (horizontal axis). Every such graph breaks into intervals where the quantity is:

increasing
the graph rises left to right
decreasing
the graph falls left to right
constant
the graph is flat

Read a graph like a book: left to right, the way time moves. Watch the bath from the warm-up play out with its graph.

Sorting the pieces

To read any graph’s story, find its pieces first. Every piece that rises is a time when the quantity was increasing; every flat piece is a time when nothing changed.

How fast? The rate of change

The steepness of a segment measures how quickly the quantity changes: its rate of change.

\text{rate of change}=\dfrac{\text{change in the quantity (vertical)}}{\text{change in time (horizontal)}}
A larger magnitude of steepness, positive or negative, means a faster rate of change; a flat segment has a rate of change of zero. The units are “quantity per time”: cm/min, cars/h, km/h.
!
Faster means steeper, not higher. A falling segment can be the fastest one on the graph. Compare the size of the rates and ignore the sign: draining at -4.4 cm/min beats filling at 2.5 cm/min.
The drain rate in the warm-up. The depth changes by -20 cm in 4.5 min: (−)20÷4.5ENTER gives about -4.4 cm/min. Filling: 20÷8ENTER gives 2.5 cm/min.
Think Segment A rises 30 cm in 6 min. Segment B falls 30 cm in 3 min. Which shows the faster change, and which is steeper?
A: \dfrac{30}{6}=5 cm/min. B: \dfrac{-30}{3}=-10 cm/min. B is faster and steeper: its magnitude, 10, is bigger than 5. It covers the same change in half the time.
Example 1

Reading the story of a parking-garage graph

A downtown parking garage opens empty at 7:00 and closes for the night at 18:00. The graph shows the number of cars parked in the garage through the day. It is made of six line segments meeting at P,Q,R,S,T,U,V.

(a) With reference to the parking garage, explain what is happening during each interval.

(i) P to Q
P(7{:}00,\ 0) to Q(9{:}00,\ 160): the count climbs from 0 to 160 cars in 2 hours. This is the morning rush: commuters arriving and parking.
(ii) R to S
R(11{:}00,\ 160) to S(12{:}00,\ 180): a gentle rise from 160 to 180 cars in 1 hour. A small wave of lunchtime visitors arrives.
(iii) T to U
T(13{:}00,\ 120) to U(17{:}00,\ 120): flat at 120 cars for 4 hours. Cars enter and leave at the same rate (or not at all): a quiet afternoon.
(b) Determine the rate of change, in cars per hour, between S and T.
From S(12,\ 180) to T(13,\ 120):
\text{rate}=\dfrac{120-180}{13-12}=\dfrac{-60}{1}=\boldsymbol{-60} cars/h.
The garage loses 60 cars in that hour as the lunch crowd leaves.
(c) Which single segment shows the fastest rate of change, and what does this tell you about the garage at that time?
Compare the magnitude of the rate on every sloped segment: PQ:\ 160\div2=80; RS:\ 20\div1=20; ST:\ 60\div1=60; UV:\ 120\div1=120.
The steepest is U to V, at 120 cars/h (decreasing): the evening exodus, almost everyone leaving in the hour before closing.
(d) For how many hours in total was the number of cars in the garage increasing?
Increasing on P\to Q (7:00 to 9:00, 2 h) and on R\to S (11:00 to 12:00, 1 h). Total =\boldsymbol{3} hours.

The vertical line test, revisited

A graph represents a quantity as a function of time only if every vertical line crosses it at most once; otherwise a single instant would give more than one value of the quantity, which is impossible.

The circle at right fails the test: a vertical line through its centre meets the curve twice, so it cannot show y as a function of x.

Every graph in this lesson that plots a quantity against time must pass this test: an object can only be in one state at any one instant.
Think Could a graph of water depth against time ever have a perfectly vertical segment? What about a very steep one?
Never vertical: a vertical piece would give many depths at one instant, which fails the vertical line test. A very steep piece is fine: a fast change (like Sam getting in) still takes a little time, so the segment leans slightly right.

3

Sketching a Graph From a Description

To sketch a graph with no numerical scale, focus only on its overall shape: where it rises, falls, stays flat, or repeats, and how sharply it does so. Exact numbers do not matter; getting the pattern of behaviour right does.

1Read the axes. What is the quantity? Is the horizontal axis time or something else?
2Split the story into phrases: one piece of graph for each.
3Each piece: up, down or flat? Sharply or gently? Straight or curving?
4Check the ends: where does it start? Does it ever reach zero?
Example 3

From a story to a shape

Sketch a graph with no scale for each situation.

(a) The temperature inside a kiln while pottery is fired: the kiln heats up steadily, holds at a high temperature for a long soak, then is turned off and cools back toward room temperature.
Three pieces, in order: a straight increase (steady heating), a constant flat stretch (the long soak), then a decrease (cooling once switched off). The fall stops above the axis: the kiln cools to room temperature, not to 0^\circ\text{C}.
(b) The speed of a cyclist on a flat trail with one steep hill in the middle: she rides at a steady speed, slows sharply climbing the hill, nearly stops at the crest, then speeds up quickly down the far side before levelling off.
Constant on the flat trail, a sharp decrease to just above zero at the crest, a sharp increase down the far side, then constant again. The horizontal axis is distance, not time, and the speed never reaches 0: she nearly stops.
Think In part (b) the horizontal axis is distance. Could her graph show her stopping at the crest for a minute to enjoy the view?
No. While she is stopped, her distance doesn’t change, so the graph can’t move to the right at all: the speed would just touch 0 at that one distance, and the minute’s rest itself is invisible on a speed–distance graph. The low flat piece means she kept riding slowly over that stretch of trail.
✓

Key ideas

Read left to right. A piece that rises is increasing, one that falls is decreasing, a flat piece is constant. Each piece is one part of the story.
Rate of change =\dfrac{\text{change in quantity}}{\text{change in time}}, with units like cars/h. Steeper means faster (compare sizes, ignore the sign); flat means a rate of 0.
Shapes: straight = constant rate; curving and levelling off = slowing down; curving upward more and more = speeding up; a repeating pattern = periodic.
Sketching: read the axes, one piece per phrase, then check the ends (does it ever reach 0?). A quantity against time must pass the vertical line test.
