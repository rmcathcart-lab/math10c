Simplifying Expressions with Several Exponent Laws

Real expressions rarely need just one law. (2x^3)^4 needs two, and a fraction with brackets on top and bottom can need four. Today we chain the laws together in a sensible order, one base at a time, then deal with negative bases and with exponents that are whole expressions.

I can…
  • explain a simplification by writing each power in expanded factor form
  • simplify a product or quotient: the coefficients first, then one base at a time
  • choose and apply two or more exponent laws, in the right order, in one chain
  • tell (-a)^n from -a^n and keep track of the sign
  • add and subtract exponents that are algebraic expressions (extension)
AN3 Demonstrate an understanding of powers with integral and rational exponents.

Recap: Expanding Into Factors

Lesson 1 built each law by expanding powers into factors. The same check works when there are coefficients: write each term in expanded factor form.

Multiplying
\begin{aligned}&3x^2\times2x^4\\&=(3\cdot x\cdot x)\times(2\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x)\\&=(3\times2)\cdot(x\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x)\\&=6x^6\end{aligned}
Dividing
\begin{aligned}&12a^5\div3a^2\\&=\dfrac{12\cdot a\cdot a\cdot a\cdot a\cdot a}{3\cdot a\cdot a}\\&=\dfrac{12}{3}\cdot a\cdot a\cdot a\\&=4a^3\end{aligned}
Coefficients combine like ordinary numbers. The variable factors stack up (Product Law) or cancel (Quotient Law).
Example 1

Three students, three answers

Three students are asked to simplify 5x^2\times4x^4. Their answers are shown below.

Devon
20x^8
Farah
9x^6
Priya
20x^6

Use expanded factor form to explain which student’s answer is correct, and describe the mistake made by each of the other two.

(5\cdot x\cdot x)\times(4\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x)=(5\times4)\cdot(x\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x)=\boldsymbol{20x^6}, so Priya is correct.
Devon multiplied the exponents (2\times4=8) instead of adding them: there are only 2+4=6 factors of x.
Farah added the coefficients (5+4=9) instead of multiplying them: the 5 and the 4 multiply to 20.

Dividing: the factors cancel in pairs

Each a on the bottom cancels one a on the top, because \dfrac{a}{a}=1. Whatever is left on top is the answer’s power.

Example 2

Justifying the quotient law with factors

Use expanded factor form to explain why 10a^8\div5a^3=2a^5.

10a^8=10\cdot(a\cdot a\cdot a\cdot a\cdot a\cdot a\cdot a\cdot a) has 8 factors of a; 5a^3=5\cdot(a\cdot a\cdot a) has 3.
The coefficients reduce, 10\div5=2, and 3 of the 8 factors of a cancel, leaving 8-3=5.
So 10a^8\div5a^3=\boldsymbol{2a^5}, just as the quotient law predicts. ✓

A Strategy for Combining Several Laws

With several laws at once, work in this order:

  1. Step 1
    Raise each inner bracket to its power (Power of a Product / Power of a Quotient / Power of a Power): every factor inside, coefficient included. A bracket around the whole expression waits for Step 4.
  2. Step 2
    Multiply or divide the coefficients like ordinary numbers.
  3. Step 3
    Add or subtract exponents, one base at a time (Product and Quotient Laws).
  4. Step 4
    If the whole expression sits inside an outer bracket, simplify inside first and apply that outer power last.
  5. Step 5
    Rewrite any negative exponent as a positive one (Lesson 3).
The laws from Lesson 1
Producta^m\cdot a^n=a^{m+n}
Quotient\dfrac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n}
Power of a Power(a^m)^n=a^{mn}
Power of a Product(ab)^m=a^mb^m
Power of a Quotient\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^n=\dfrac{a^n}{b^n}
Zero Exponenta^0=1

One base at a time

Sort the pieces into columns: the numbers in one, each letter in its own. Then each column is a small, one-law problem.

Example 3

One base at a time

Simplify the following.

(a) 6x^4\times3x^5
6\times3=18; x^{4+5}=x^9
\boldsymbol{18x^9}
(b) (-8a^6)(5a^9)
(-8)(5)=-40; a^{6+9}=a^{15}
\boldsymbol{-40a^{15}}
(c) \dfrac{24y^{18}}{8y^6}
24\div8=3; y^{18-6}=y^{12}
\boldsymbol{3y^{12}}
(d) \dfrac{40b^{15}}{48b^9}
\tfrac{40}{48}=\tfrac{5}{6}; b^{15-9}=b^6
\boldsymbol{\tfrac{5}{6}b^6}
(e) (2a^5)(a^6)(4a^2)
2\times1\times4=8; a^{5+6+2}=a^{13}
\boldsymbol{8a^{13}}
(f) (-20n^7)\div(-4n)
(-20)\div(-4)=5; n^{7-1}=n^6
\boldsymbol{5n^6}
(g) a^6b^9\times a^2b^3
a^{6+2}=a^8; b^{9+3}=b^{12}
\boldsymbol{a^8b^{12}}
(h) \dfrac{x^7y^5}{x^3y^2}
x^{7-3}=x^4; y^{5-2}=y^3
\boldsymbol{x^4y^3}
!
A letter with no exponent has exponent 1. In (f), n=n^1, so n^7\div n=n^{7-1}=n^6, not n^7. And a term with no number in front, like a^6 in (e), has coefficient 1.

Two or more laws in one chain

Follow the strategy one line at a time. Each line uses one or two laws; the badges show which law just did the work.

!
Every factor inside the bracket gets the power, the number too. (2x^3)^4=2^4x^{12}=16x^{12}, not 2x^{12}. A negative number inside gets it as well: (-3)^3=-27.
Example 4

Two or more laws in one chain

Simplify the following. Each part uses two or more of the exponent laws.

(a) (2x^3)^4
Power of a product: 2^4=16, x^{3\times4}=x^{12}
\boldsymbol{16x^{12}}
(b) (-3a^2b^4)^3
(-3)^3=-27 (odd power); a^{2\times3}=a^6, b^{4\times3}=b^{12}
\boldsymbol{-27a^6b^{12}}
(c) \dfrac{x^4\cdot x^6}{x^3\cdot x^2}
Top x^{10}, bottom x^5; then x^{10-5}
\boldsymbol{x^5}
(d) \left(-\dfrac{3a}{b^2}\right)^3
(-3)^3=-27 and a^3 on top; b^{2\times3}=b^6 on the bottom
\boldsymbol{-\dfrac{27a^3}{b^6}}
(e) \left(\dfrac{6a^3\times2a^5}{4a^2}\right)^3
Inside first: \dfrac{12a^8}{4a^2}=3a^6. Outer power last: (3a^6)^3=3^3a^{18}
\boldsymbol{27a^{18}}
(f) \dfrac{54(a^3b^4)^2}{(3a^2)^3}
\dfrac{54a^6b^8}{27a^6}=2a^{6-6}b^8=2a^0b^8
\boldsymbol{2b^8}
(g) (3a^4b)^2(2a^3b^5)
(3a^4b)^2=9a^8b^2; then 9\times2=18, a^{8+3}, b^{2+5}
\boldsymbol{18a^{11}b^7}
Think Kai wrote (2x^3)^4=2x^{12}, and Mia wrote (2x^3)^4=8x^{12}. What did each of them do wrong?
Kai never raised the 2 to the 4th: it’s a factor inside the bracket, so it gets the power too. Mia multiplied 2\times4 instead of working out 2^4=16. Correct: \boldsymbol{16x^{12}}.

Is my answer right? Check it with a number

An expression and its simplified form are equal for every value of the variable. So substitute a number into both: if the values don’t match, there’s a mistake.

Check Example 4(a) at x=2. The original: (2×2^3)^4ENTER. The answer: 16×2^12ENTER. Both give 65 536 ✓.
!
Don’t test with 0 or 1. 1^8 and 1^6 are both 1, so a wrong exponent slips right past. Use 2 or 3.

Negative Bases: (-a)^n versus -a^n

(-a)^n The negative sign is part of the base, so it gets raised to the power too.
(-a)^n=a^n when n is even  ·  (-a)^n=-a^n when n is odd
-a^n Only a is the base. The exponent applies to a alone; the negative sign out front is applied afterward.
Always the opposite sign of a^n:  -a^n=-(a^n)
The calculator agrees. The TI-30XIIS does powers before negation: (−)2x²ENTER gives −4, which is -2^2. For (-2)^2 you need the brackets: ((−)2)x²ENTER gives 4.
! These are NOT exponent laws: common traps (tap “Test it” to try numbers)
(a+b)^2\neq a^2+b^2
The exponent does not distribute over +.
(2+3)^2=5^2=25, but 2^2+3^2=4+9=13.
2^3\cdot3^2\neq6^5
Different bases: the Product Law needs the same base.
2^3\cdot3^2=8\times9=72, but 6^5=7\,776.
a^m+a^n\neq a^{m+n}
Adding powers is not multiplying them.
2^3+2^2=8+4=12, but 2^5=32.
Coming in Lesson 3: 4x^{-2}\neq\dfrac{1}{4x^2}. An exponent belongs only to the base it touches.
Example 5

Negative bases

Write in simplest form.

(a) (-a)^8\div(-a)^5
Same base (-a): (-a)^{8-5}=(-a)^3; odd exponent, so negative
\boldsymbol{-a^3}
(b) -a^8\div(-a)^5
\dfrac{-a^8}{-a^5}: the two negatives cancel, so a^{8-5}
\boldsymbol{a^3}
(c) -a^9\div(-a)^4
(-a)^4=a^4 (even), so \dfrac{-a^9}{a^4}=-a^{9-4}
\boldsymbol{-a^5}
(d) \dfrac{100x^6y^9(-z^2)^3}{5(2x^2y)^3z^2y^2}
Powers first: (-z^2)^3=-z^6 (odd) and (2x^2y)^3=8x^6y^3. Top =-100x^6y^9z^6, bottom =40x^6y^5z^2.
One base at a time: -\tfrac{100}{40}\cdot x^{6-6}\cdot y^{9-5}\cdot z^{6-2}=-\tfrac{5}{2}x^0y^4z^4=\boldsymbol{-\dfrac{5}{2}y^4z^4}

Extension: Variable Exponents

The laws still apply when the exponents are algebraic expressions: add or subtract the exponent expressions instead of single numbers. The base never changes; all the work happens up in the exponent.
!
Subtract the whole bottom exponent: use brackets. Forget them and 5x+2y-x-3y=4x-y, which is wrong. With brackets: (5x+2y)-(x-3y)=4x+5y.
Example 6

Variable exponents

Simplify each expression.

(a) \dfrac{b^{5x+2y}}{b^{x-3y}}
(5x+2y)-(x-3y)=5x+2y-x+3y=4x+5y
\boldsymbol{b^{4x+5y}}
(b) \dfrac{x^{4a+9b}\cdot x^{2a+b}}{x^a\cdot x^{3a-4b}}
Top 6a+10b; bottom 4a-4b
(6a+10b)-(4a-4b)=2a+14b
\boldsymbol{x^{2a+14b}}
(c) \dfrac{m^{2x+5}}{m^6}
(2x+5)-6=2x-1
\boldsymbol{m^{2x-1}}
✓

Key ideas

Expanded factor form explains every law: 5x^2\times4x^4=(5\times4)(x\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x)=20x^6. Coefficients multiply; exponents add.
Brackets first (every factor inside, the number too), then the coefficients, then one base at a time. An outer bracket around everything goes last.
(-a)^n: the sign is in the base, so it’s positive for even n, negative for odd. -a^n is always the opposite of a^n.
Variable exponents: add or subtract the exponent expressions, with brackets: b^{5x+2y}\div b^{x-3y}=b^{4x+5y}. Check any answer by substituting 2 or 3.
