Simplifying with Rational Exponents

In Lesson 5A, a^{m/n}=\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^{m} turned rational exponents into radicals and back. Now the exponent laws from Lessons 1–3 do the simplifying. They work exactly the same way with fractional exponents: you just add, subtract or multiply fractions.

I can…
  • use the exponent laws with fractional exponents, adding, subtracting and multiplying the fractions
  • write a simplified power with a positive exponent, and then in radical form
  • write a root of a root (or a root in a denominator) as a single power, and evaluate it
  • write a radical in the form ax^n by splitting off and evaluating the numerical factor
  • simplify multi-law chains with rational and negative exponents, one base at a time
AN3 Demonstrate an understanding of powers with integral and rational exponents.

The Earthquake Scale

One step up the magnitude scale: 10 times the shaking, about 32 times the energy.

1
An M5 quake shakes the ground how many times as much as an M4?
10 times (one step up).
2
An M7 shakes the ground how many times as much as an M5?
10\times10= 100 times (two steps up).
3
“About 32” is really 10^{3/2}. Fill in: (tap the box)
10^{3/2}\times10^{3/2}=10^{\frac32+\frac32}=10^{6/2}=10^{3}. That’s the Product Law, with fractions.
!
The big one. An M7 earthquake releases about how many times as much energy as an M5? Guess first.
A 64

B is the shaking (Question 2), not the energy. Question 3 was the Product Law and this was the Power of a Power Law: the same answer two ways.

Reviewing the Exponent Laws

Simplifying expressions with rational exponents just means reusing the laws already established for integer exponents. They hold for any rational exponents when the base is positive; with a negative base, take the root first.

Product Law
x^m\cdot x^n=x^{m+n}
Power of a Product Law
(xy)^m=x^m y^m
Quotient Law
x^m\div x^n=x^{m-n}
Power of a Quotient Law
\left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right)^m=\dfrac{x^m}{y^m},\ y\neq0
Power of a Power Law
(x^m)^n=x^{mn}
Negative Exponent Law
x^{-m}=\dfrac{1}{x^m},\ x\neq0

The fraction toolbox

With rational exponents, every law turns into a fraction calculation on the exponents. These are the only four moves you need:

Add or subtract
Get a common denominator first.
\tfrac12+\tfrac13=\tfrac36+\tfrac26=\tfrac56
Multiply
Top × top, bottom × bottom, then simplify.
\tfrac13\times\tfrac35=\tfrac{3}{15}=\tfrac15
A whole number
is a fraction over 1, or over any denominator you need.
1=\tfrac44
6\times\tfrac23=\tfrac{12}{3}=4
Minus a negative
Subtracting a negative is adding.
\tfrac45-\left(-\tfrac35\right)=\tfrac45+\tfrac35=\tfrac75
!
Always simplify the exponent. x^{6/4} is not finished: \tfrac64=\tfrac32, so write x^{3/2}. An improper fraction like \tfrac94 is fine as it is; don’t turn it into a mixed number.
2

Simplifying Expressions with Rational Exponents

The laws apply directly: there is no need to convert to radical form first. Simplify the exponent fully, write it positive, and convert to a radical only at the very end.
x^{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{m}/\textcolor{#13807a}{n}}=\sqrt[\textcolor{#13807a}{n}]{x^{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{m}}}
denominator = the index of the root
numerator = the power
!
A plain variable has exponent 1. In w^{5/4}\times w, the second w is w^1=w^{4/4}.
!
The exponent hits the number too. \left(27e^{1/2}\right)^{4/3} raises 27 to \tfrac43 as well: 27^{4/3}=81.
Check by substituting a number. If w^{5/4}\times w=w^{9/4}, they must agree for any positive w. Try w=2: 2^(5÷4)×2ENTER and 2^(9÷4)ENTER both give 4.75682846. Brackets around the fraction exponent!
Example 1

The exponent laws with fractional exponents

Simplify each expression. Write your answer as a power with a positive exponent, and then in radical form.

(a) w^{5/4}\times w
Product Law: add exponents.
w^{\frac54+1}=\boldsymbol{w^{9/4}}=\boldsymbol{\sqrt[4]{w^9}}
(b) t^{1/4}\div t^{7/4}
Quotient Law: t^{\frac14-\frac74}=t^{-6/4}=t^{-3/2}
=\boldsymbol{\tfrac{1}{t^{3/2}}}=\boldsymbol{\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{t^3}}}
(c) \left(b^{1/3}\right)^{3/5}
Power of a Power: multiply.
b^{\frac13\cdot\frac35}=b^{3/15}=\boldsymbol{b^{1/5}}=\boldsymbol{\sqrt[5]{b}}
(d) \left(\dfrac{r^2}{s}\right)^{-1/3}
Flip, then distribute:
\left(\tfrac{s}{r^2}\right)^{1/3}=\boldsymbol{\tfrac{s^{1/3}}{r^{2/3}}}=\boldsymbol{\tfrac{\sqrt[3]{s}}{\sqrt[3]{r^2}}}
(e) 6c^{5/6}\times2c^{-1/3}
6\times2=12; \frac56-\frac13=\frac56-\frac26=\frac36=\frac12
\boldsymbol{12c^{1/2}}=\boldsymbol{12\sqrt{c}}
(f) \dfrac{7d^{4/5}}{49d^{-3/5}}
\frac{7}{49}=\frac17; \frac45-\left(-\frac35\right)=\frac75
\boldsymbol{\tfrac17d^{7/5}}=\boldsymbol{\tfrac{\sqrt[5]{d^7}}{7}} (or \tfrac{d\sqrt[5]{d^2}}{7})
(g) \left(27e^{1/2}\right)^{4/3}
27^{4/3}=\left(27^{1/3}\right)^4=3^4=81; e^{\frac12\cdot\frac43}=e^{2/3}
\boldsymbol{81e^{2/3}}=\boldsymbol{81\sqrt[3]{e^2}}
3

Roots as Single Powers

Any root can be written as a single power. A “root of a root” multiplies the indices together (a square root of a cube root is a sixth root, and so on), and a root in a denominator becomes a negative exponent.
\sqrt[3]{\sqrt{x}}=\left(x^{\frac12}\right)^{\frac13}=x^{\frac12\times\frac13}=x^{1/6}=\sqrt[6]{x}
\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}=\dfrac{1}{x^{1/2}}=x^{-1/2}
Think Is \sqrt[3]{\sqrt{x}} the same as \sqrt{\sqrt[3]{x}}? Does the order of the roots matter?
Same. \tfrac12\times\tfrac13=\tfrac13\times\tfrac12=\tfrac16, so both are x^{1/6}. Multiplying fractions works in either order. Check: \sqrt[3]{\sqrt{64}}=\sqrt[3]{8}=2 and \sqrt{\sqrt[3]{64}}=\sqrt{4}=2.
On the calculator. Evaluate the single power 2401^{1/4} with brackets, 2401^(1÷4)ENTER, or with the x-th root key, 42nd^2401ENTER. For 361^{-1/2}, 2ndF◂▸D turns the decimal into a fraction.
Example 2

One power, then one evaluation

Write each expression as a single power, then evaluate.

(a) \sqrt{\sqrt{2401}}
Indices multiply: 2\times2=4, so \boldsymbol{2401^{1/4}}.
7^2=49 and 49^2=2401, so 2401=7^4 and 2401^{1/4}=\boldsymbol{7}
(b) \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{361}}
\sqrt{361}=19 since 19^2=361, so
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{361}}=\boldsymbol{361^{-1/2}}=\boldsymbol{\tfrac{1}{19}}
(c) \sqrt[3]{\sqrt{729}}
A cube root of a square root: 3\times2=6, so \boldsymbol{729^{1/6}}.
3^6=\left(3^3\right)^2=27^2=729, so 729^{1/6}=\boldsymbol{3}

Writing Radicals in the Form ax^n

When a radical contains both a perfect-power number and a variable, pull the numerical part out as an integer coefficient a, and leave the variable part as a power x^n. The root applies to both factors.
\sqrt[n]{\textcolor{#c27a0e}{c}\,\textcolor{#13807a}{x^{m}}}=\textcolor{#c27a0e}{c^{1/n}}\cdot\textcolor{#13807a}{x^{m/n}}
evaluate the number · exponent over index for the variable
a\in I,\quad n\in Q
a is an integer, n may be a fraction
!
Don’t leave the number under the root. \sqrt[3]{64x^8} is not 64x^{8/3}: the cube root applies to 64 too, and \sqrt[3]{64}=4.
Check Example 3(a) with x=2. 32nd^(64×2^8)ENTER and 4×2^(8÷3)ENTER both give 25.39841683.
Example 3

Splitting off the numerical factor

Write each expression in the form ax^n, where a\in I and n\in Q.

(a) \sqrt[3]{64x^8}
\sqrt[3]{64x^8}=64^{1/3}x^{8/3}=\boldsymbol{4x^{8/3}}, since 4^3=64
(b) \sqrt[4]{16x^6}
16^{1/4}x^{6/4}=\boldsymbol{2x^{3/2}}, since 2^4=16 and \frac64=\frac32
(c) \sqrt{400x}
\sqrt{400x}=\boldsymbol{20x^{1/2}}, since 20^2=400
(d) \left(\sqrt[3]{x^7}\right)\left(\sqrt[3]{x^2}\right)
Same base, so add exponents:
x^{7/3}\times x^{2/3}=x^{9/3}=\boldsymbol{x^3}
(e) 3\sqrt{x}\times2\sqrt[3]{x}
3\times2=6; \frac12+\frac13=\frac56
\boldsymbol{6x^{5/6}}
5

Simplifying Nested Radicals and Powers

Now the roots carry variables too. Two ways, same answer: work from the inside out, one root at a time, or multiply the indices first and take one single power of everything inside. Either way, every factor inside (number and variable) gets the power.

Example 4

Nested roots carrying variables

Write each expression using rational exponents (in the form ax^n).

(a) \sqrt{\sqrt{k^5}}
Indices multiply: 2\times2=4, so
\sqrt{\sqrt{k^5}}=\boldsymbol{k^{5/4}}
(b) \sqrt{\sqrt[3]{729w^9}}
2\times3=6: \left(729w^9\right)^{1/6}=729^{1/6}w^{9/6}
Since 3^6=729: \boldsymbol{3w^{3/2}}
(c) \left(\sqrt[4]{k^6y^{10}}\right)^{3/2}
\sqrt[4]{k^6y^{10}}=k^{6/4}y^{10/4}=k^{3/2}y^{5/2}
k^{\frac32\cdot\frac32}y^{\frac52\cdot\frac32}=\boldsymbol{k^{9/4}y^{15/4}}

Multi-Law Chains with Rational and Negative Exponents

Use the same order as in Lesson 2, now with fractions on the exponents:

1
Raise each bracket to its power: every factor, coefficient included.
2
Evaluate number powers such as 8^{2/3} by taking the root first.
3
Combine base by base: numbers with numbers, then add or subtract each variable’s exponents.
4
Finish with positive exponents: a negative exponent moves its factor across the fraction bar.

One base at a time

Give every base its own lane and its own colour. Each step works down the lanes, so nothing gets mixed up.

!
A negative exponent never makes a number negative. y^{-2}=\tfrac{1}{y^2} moves y^2 below the fraction bar; the coefficient stays where it is.
Example 5

Rational and negative exponents together

Simplify. Write each answer with positive exponents.

(a) \left(8x^{6}y^{-3}\right)^{2/3}\cdot x^{-1/2}
8^{2/3}=\left(\sqrt[3]{8}\right)^{2}=4,\ x^{6\cdot\frac23}=x^{4},\ y^{-3\cdot\frac23}=y^{-2}
4x^{4}y^{-2}\cdot x^{-1/2}=4x^{4-\frac12}y^{-2}=4x^{7/2}y^{-2}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{4x^{7/2}}{y^{2}}}
(b) \dfrac{\left(81a^{-8}b^{2}\right)^{1/4}}{3a^{-1/2}}
Top: 81^{1/4}=3,\ a^{-8\cdot\frac14}=a^{-2},\ b^{2\cdot\frac14}=b^{1/2}
\tfrac{3}{3}\,a^{-2-\left(-\frac12\right)}b^{1/2}=a^{-3/2}b^{1/2}=\boldsymbol{\dfrac{b^{1/2}}{a^{3/2}}}
Check (a) with x=4,\ y=2. The question \left(8\cdot4^{6}\cdot2^{-3}\right)^{2/3}\cdot4^{-1/2} and the answer \dfrac{4\cdot4^{7/2}}{2^{2}} both give 128. Use (−) for a negative exponent, inside its brackets.
✓

Key ideas

Same laws, fraction arithmetic. Product: add. Quotient: subtract. Power of a power: multiply. w^{5/4}\times w=w^{\frac54+\frac44}=w^{9/4}
Finish cleanly: simplify the exponent, make it positive, then convert: x^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{x^m} (denominator = index).
Roots as one power: a root of a root multiplies the indices; a root underneath becomes negative. \sqrt[3]{\sqrt{729}}=729^{1/6}=3
Chains: brackets first (every factor), numbers root first, then one base at a time, positive exponents last. \sqrt[3]{64x^8}=4x^{8/3}
