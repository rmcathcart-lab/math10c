Negative Exponents

We know a^0=1. Today we keep walking down the pattern past zero: 2^{-1}, 2^{-2}, 2^{-3}, and beyond. A negative exponent doesn’t make a number negative. It makes it small: it means one over the positive power.

I can…
  • extend the pattern of powers past a^0=1 and explain what a negative exponent means
  • use the quotient law to show why a^{-n}=\dfrac{1}{a^n}
  • rewrite an expression with positive exponents, moving only the factor the exponent belongs to
  • simplify numerical and algebraic expressions with the exponent laws, then write the answer with positive exponents
  • evaluate a fraction raised to a negative exponent by flipping it
AN3 Demonstrate an understanding of powers with integral and rational exponents.

The Negative Exponent Pattern

Recall from Lesson 1 that a^0=1\ (a\neq0). Now keep going past zero: each time the exponent of 6 drops by 1, the value is divided by 6 once more.

6^1=6 
6^0=1(6\div6)
6^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{6}(1\div6)
6^{-2}=\dfrac{1}{36}\left(\tfrac16\div6\right)
6^{-3}=\dfrac{1}{216}\left(\tfrac1{36}\div6\right)
The same “keep dividing by the base” pattern holds for any nonzero base a:
a^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{a},\qquad a^{-2}=\dfrac{1}{a^2},\qquad a^{-3}=\dfrac{1}{a^3},\qquad\dots,\qquad a^{-n}=\dfrac{1}{a^n}\quad(a\neq0)
!
Negative exponent ≠ negative number. 2^{-3}=\tfrac18. It is not -8, and it is not -6.
!
Why a\neq0? 0^{-1} would be \tfrac10, and we can never divide by zero.
Check the pattern on the calculator. For a negative exponent use the negative key (−), not the subtract key: 6^(−)3ENTER gives a decimal; 2ndF◂▸D turns it into 1┘216, which means \tfrac{1}{216}.
Example 1

Rewriting a single power with a positive exponent

Write each expression with positive exponents only.

(a) 9^{-3}
Apply a^{-n}=\tfrac{1}{a^n} directly.
9^{-3}=\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{9^3}}\ \left(=\tfrac{1}{729}\right)
(b) x^{-7}
x^{-7}=\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{x^7}}
(c) a^{-m}
a^{-m}=\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{a^m}}
Think Which is bigger, 2^{-3} or 2^{-4}? Is either one negative?
2^{-3}=\tfrac18 is bigger than 2^{-4}=\tfrac1{16}: one more step down the stairs halves it again. Neither is negative; both are small positive numbers.

Where Does the Negative Exponent Law Come From?

The quotient law confirms the pattern. It gives 6^3\div6^8=6^{3-8}=6^{-5}. Expanding the same quotient instead, three 6’s cancel from the top, leaving five on the bottom:

6^3\div6^8=\dfrac{6\times6\times6}{6\times6\times6\times6\times6\times6\times6\times6}=\dfrac{1}{6^5}

Both describe the same expression, so 6^{-5}=\dfrac{1}{6^5}. Flipped around, dividing by a negative power is the same as multiplying by the matching positive power: \dfrac{1}{6^{-5}}=6^5.

Negative Exponent Law  For any nonzero base a and positive integer n,
a^{-n}=\dfrac{1}{a^n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dfrac{1}{a^{-n}}=a^n

Move it across the fraction bar, and flip the sign

A factor with a negative exponent can move to the other side of the fraction bar, where its exponent becomes positive. But only the base the exponent touches moves. Everything else stays where it is.

NOT an exponent law (continued from Lesson 2):  4x^{-2}\neq\dfrac{1}{4x^2}. The exponent -2 belongs only to the base it touches, x:  4x^{-2}=\dfrac{4}{x^2}. Only (4x)^{-2}=\dfrac{1}{16x^2} moves the 4 as well.
Example 3

True or false? Where the negative exponent attaches

Identify each of the following statements as true or false.

(a) \dfrac{7^5}{7^{-2}}=7^7
\tfrac{7^5}{7^{-2}}=7^{5-(-2)}=7^7, which matches.
True
(b) \dfrac{7^5}{7^{-2}}=7^3
The same left side gives 7^7, and 7^7\neq7^3.
False
(c) x^{-4}=\dfrac{1}{x^4}
The negative exponent flips only x: exactly \tfrac{1}{x^4}.
True
(d) 5x^{-2}=\dfrac{1}{5x^2}
Only x carries the -2; the 5 stays on top: 5x^{-2}=\tfrac{5}{x^2}.
False

Applying the Law to Algebraic Expressions

The law works the same way for a variable base. Apply the product, quotient and power laws first, then rewrite any negative exponent using a^{-n}=\dfrac{1}{a^n}.

1Deal with brackets: a power of a product goes to every factor inside.
2Numbers with numbers: multiply, or reduce the fraction.
3Each variable on its own: exponents on top add, exponents on the bottom subtract.
4Last: move any negative exponent across the fraction bar.
Example 4

Simplifying algebraic expressions

Simplify each expression. Write the final answer with positive exponents.

(a) a^{-5}\times a^{-2}
=a^{-5+(-2)}=a^{-7}
=\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{a^7}}
(b) 8x^3\div2x^9
=\tfrac82x^{3-9}=4x^{-6}
=\mathbf{\dfrac{4}{x^6}}
(c) \dfrac{y^4}{3y^{-6}}
=\tfrac13y^{4-(-6)}=\tfrac13y^{10}
=\mathbf{\dfrac{y^{10}}{3}}
(d) (-3x)^{-3}
=\dfrac{1}{(-3x)^3}=\dfrac{1}{-27x^3}
=\mathbf{-\dfrac{1}{27x^3}}
(e) \dfrac{9a^{-4}}{3b^{-2}}
=3a^{-4}b^{2}
=\mathbf{\dfrac{3b^2}{a^4}}
(f) \dfrac{(2p)^{-2}}{7q^3}
=\dfrac{1}{(2p)^2\cdot7q^3}=\dfrac{1}{4p^2\cdot7q^3}
=\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{28p^2q^3}}
Think Why is the answer to (d) negative? What would (-3x)^{-2} be?
(-3)^3=-27: an odd power of a negative stays negative. With an even power the sign disappears: (-3x)^{-2}=\dfrac{1}{(-3x)^2}=\dfrac{1}{9x^2}.

A division chain in three variables

Simplify and write the answer with positive exponents.

4x^2y^{-5}z^{-3}\;\div\;\dfrac{12x^7y^4z^{-2}}{x^4y^{-2}z^3}

Dividing by a fraction means multiplying by its reciprocal: 4x^2y^{-5}z^{-3}\times\dfrac{x^4y^{-2}z^3}{12x^7y^4z^{-2}}
Numbers: \tfrac{4}{12}=\tfrac13.   x\!:\ 2+4-7=-1.   y\!:\ -5+(-2)-4=-11.   z\!:\ -3+3-(-2)=2.
\tfrac13x^{-1}y^{-11}z^{2}=\mathbf{\dfrac{z^2}{3xy^{11}}}

Negative Exponents with a Fractional Base

When the base is itself a fraction, the negative exponent law gives

\left(\dfrac{3}{7}\right)^{-2}=\dfrac{1}{\left(\frac{3}{7}\right)^2}=\dfrac{1}{\frac{9}{49}}=\dfrac{49}{9}=\left(\dfrac{7}{3}\right)^2

So raising a fraction to a negative power is the same as flipping the fraction and making the exponent positive.

Fractional Base Law  For nonzero a and b, and positive integer n,
\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^{-n}=\left(\dfrac{b}{a}\right)^{n}

Key ideas

The pattern: each step down divides by the base, right past zero: 2^0=1,\ 2^{-1}=\tfrac12,\ 2^{-2}=\tfrac14.
The law: a^{-n}=\dfrac{1}{a^n} and \dfrac{1}{a^{-n}}=a^n. A negative exponent means small, not negative.
Move only what the exponent touches, and flip its sign: 5x^{-2}=\dfrac{5}{x^2}, but (5x)^{-2}=\dfrac{1}{25x^2}.
Laws first, then positive exponents. A fraction to a negative power flips: \left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^{-n}=\left(\dfrac{b}{a}\right)^{n}.
