Classifying Systems: How Many Solutions?

Two lines can cross once, never, or everywhere, and their slopes and y-intercepts tell you which. In Lesson 1A every system had exactly one solution: the single point where the two lines cross. Two lines do not always cross exactly once, though. This lesson sorts systems by their number of solutions: first from their graphs, then from slope–intercept form alone.

I can…
  • describe the three ways two lines can meet (crossing, parallel, coincident) and state how many solutions each gives
  • graph a system of two linear equations and count its solutions from the graph
  • complete the classification chart: what the slopes and y-intercepts are for one solution, no solution and infinitely many solutions
  • rewrite both equations in the form y=mx+b and classify a system without graphing
RF9 Solve problems that involve systems of linear equations in two variables, graphically and algebraically.

1

Three Ways Two Lines Can Meet

When two linear equations are graphed on the same set of axes, exactly one of three things happens: the lines cross at a single point, the lines run side by side and never touch, or the lines lie perfectly on top of one another. Each outcome gives the system a different number of solutions.

The lines cross once
intersecting lines
one solution
The lines never touch
parallel (distinct) lines
no solution
The lines lie on top of each other
coincident lines (the same line)
infinitely many solutions

Watch one line turn until it is parallel to the other, then slide until it lies on top. Keep an eye on the solution count and the chart.

Think Could two different straight lines cross at exactly two points?
No. Only one straight line passes through two given points. If two lines share two points, they are the same line, and then they share every point: infinitely many solutions. So the only possible counts are 1, 0 or infinitely many.

The warm-up races as lines

Distance after t seconds = head start + speed \times\,t. That is y=mx+b: the speed is the slope and the head start is the intercept. “Side by side” means the same t and the same d: a point on both lines.

When two lines have different slopes they must cross exactly once, whatever their intercepts. When the slopes match, the lines are either always the same distance apart (parallel, no solution) or exactly on top of each other (coincident, infinitely many solutions): the intercepts decide which.

Classifying a System Without Graphing

Once both equations of a system are written in slope–intercept form, the pattern from Example 2 predicts the number of solutions directly from the numbers in the equations: no graph paper required.

Different slopes
m_1\neq m_2
one solution
Same slope, different intercepts
m_1=m_2,\ \ b_1\neq b_2
no solution
Same slope, same intercept
m_1=m_2,\ \ b_1=b_2
infinitely many solutions
To use the chart, first get y alone: move the x-term and the constant to the other side, then divide every term by the coefficient of y.
!
Rearrange before you compare. 8x-2y-12=0 and y=4x-6 look nothing alike, but they are the same line. Comparing the numbers in two different forms (the 8 and the 4) leads to the wrong answer.
Are two fraction slopes equal? Divide both on the calculator: \tfrac{10}{4} and \tfrac{5}{2} both give 2.5, so they are the same slope.
Example 3

Classifying from y=mx+b alone

Without graphing, determine whether each system has no solution, one solution, or infinitely many solutions.

(a) \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{5x+2y=20}
\textcolor{#c27a0e}{y=-\dfrac{5}{2}x}
5x+2y=20\Rightarrow 2y=-5x+20
\Rightarrow y=-\tfrac{5}{2}x+10.
The second equation is y=-\tfrac{5}{2}x+0.
Same slope -\tfrac{5}{2}, different intercepts (10 and 0).
No solution.
(b) \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{x-3y+9=0}
\textcolor{#c27a0e}{y=\dfrac{1}{3}x+3}
x-3y+9=0\Rightarrow -3y=-x-9
\Rightarrow y=\tfrac{1}{3}x+3,
which is identical to the second equation.
Same slope and same intercept.
Infinitely many solutions.
(c) \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{6x+y=11}
\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x-5y=20}
6x+y=11\Rightarrow y=-6x+11 (slope -6).
x-5y=20\Rightarrow -5y=-x+20
\Rightarrow y=\tfrac{1}{5}x-4 (slope \tfrac{1}{5}).
The slopes are different.
One solution.

Example 3 check: two questions decide it

Rearrange both, then ask: same slope? If not, one solution. If so, same intercept?

The same line in disguise

Multiplying every term of an equation by the same number changes how it looks, not which points make it true. Test points of one line in the other equation.

✓

Key ideas

Three ways to meet. Two lines cross once (one solution), are parallel and never touch (no solution), or are coincident, the same line (infinitely many solutions). Never exactly two.
From a graph. Graph both lines and count the points on both. A crossing point is the solution; check it in both equations.
The chart. Different slopes: one solution (intercepts can be anything). Equal slopes, different intercepts: no solution. Equal slopes, equal intercepts: infinitely many.
Without graphing. Rewrite both equations as y=mx+b first, then compare m, then b. Equations that look different can be the same line.
