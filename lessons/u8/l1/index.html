Graphing Systems of Linear Equations

Two equations at once: the solution is the point where the two lines cross, found by hand or with a graphing calculator.

I can…
  • explain what a system of linear equations is, and why its solution is the point of intersection of the lines
  • read the solution of a system off a graph and write it as an ordered pair (x,\ y)
  • graph both lines by hand, state the solution, and verify it in BOTH equations
  • use a system to model a real situation and explain what the solution means
  • solve a system with a graphing calculator (Y= WINDOW 2nd TRACE 5:intersect)
  • find a break-even point from cost and revenue, and read profit and loss from the graph

Warm-up

Hot Tea, Iced Tea

Poured at the same moment. Pretend each drink changes at a steady rate.

1
How hot is the hot tea after 5 min?
80-4(5)= 60 °C
2
How cold is the iced tea after 5 min?
5+1(5)= 10 °C
3
At the start the drinks are 75 °C apart. By how much does that gap shrink each minute?
5 °C per minute: the hot tea comes down 4 and the iced tea comes up 1, so they close in by 4+1. (After 5 min: 60-10=50=75-5(5).)
!
The big one. When are the two drinks the same temperature? Guess first.
A after 7.5 min, at 42.5 °C
B after 15 min, at 20 °C
C after about 19 min, at 5 °C
B: after 15 minutes, both at 20 °C. The 75 °C gap closes 5 °C a minute: 75\div5=15 min. Hot: 80-4(15)=20. Iced: 5+15=20 ✓
A is exactly halfway between 80 and 5, but that only works if both drinks change equally fast (at 7.5 min they are at 50 and 12.5). C counts only the hot tea, 75\div4\approx19, and forgets the iced tea is climbing to meet it.
✋
Hands up: two hot teas at 80 °C and 70 °C both cool 4 °C a minute. Will they ever match?
Never. Both drop 4 °C a minute, so they stay 10 °C apart forever: two lines with the same slope never cross. (More in Lesson 1.)

Each drink is a line: hot T=80-4t, iced T=5+t. Together they make a system, and the moment they match is where the lines cross.

1

What Is a System of Linear Equations?

The equation x+2y=8 is satisfied by many pairs of values: for instance x=0,\ y=4, or x=8,\ y=0, or x=4,\ y=2. A second equation, 2x-y=1, has many solutions of its own: x=0,\ y=-1, or x=1,\ y=1, or x=3,\ y=5. Requiring a pair to satisfy both equations at the same time leaves only one pair: x=2 and y=3, since 2+2(3)=2+6=8 ✓ and 2(2)-3=4-3=1 ✓.

A system of linear equations is a set of two (or more) linear equations considered together. A solution to the system is an ordered pair (x,\ y) that satisfies every equation in the system simultaneously. Graphically, the solution is the point of intersection of the lines.

On one line, or on both?

Every point on a line makes that equation true. Drag the point around the grid (or press Next for a tour): each equation gets a ✓ or a ✗. Only one point gets ✓ ✓.

Think Is (4,\ 2) a solution of the system x+2y=8, 2x-y=1?
No. 4+2(2)=8 ✓, but 2(4)-2=6\neq1 ✗. The point is on the first line only. A solution of the system must check in both equations.

Reading a Solution From a Graph

When a system has already been graphed, its solution is read directly off the picture: it is the coordinates of the point where the two lines cross.

Example 1

Reading the solution off a finished graph

The system y=x+5 (blue) and y=-2x-1 (amber) has been graphed below. The system has an integer solution.

(a) State the solution: x= -2, y= 3
Read the crossing point off the grid: it is 2 units left of the origin and 3 units up. x=-2,\ y=3.
(b) Write the solution as an ordered pair.
\boldsymbol{(-2,\ 3)}: x first, then y.
(c) Verify the solution by substituting it into both equations.
y=x+5: 3=-2+5 ✓
y=-2x-1: 3=-2(-2)-1=4-1 ✓
The point lies on both lines, so it is the solution.

Example 1 check: read it, then verify it

Drop from the crossing to each axis to read the coordinates, then substitute into both equations.

!
x first. (-2,\ 3) and (3,\ -2) are different points. The check catches a swap: (3,\ -2) fails both equations.

Solving a System by Graphing Without Technology

To solve a system by hand, graph each line (from a table of values, from the slope and y-intercept, or from the two intercepts), then read the coordinates of the point where the lines cross. Substituting those coordinates back into both original equations checks the solution: if both equations balance, the solution is confirmed.

Graphing one line from slope–intercept form (from Unit 7). To graph 2x+y=3:
  1. Rearrange into slope–intercept form: y=-2x+3, so m=-2 and b=3.
  2. Plot the y-intercept (0,\ 3).
  3. Use the slope m=\dfrac{-2}{1}: from (0,\ 3) go down 2 and right 1 to (1,\ 1), then again to (2,\ -1). Draw the line through the points.

Shortcut when both equations are already in the form y=\ldots

At the crossing point both lines have the same y, so the two expressions can be set equal. For y=x+1 and y=-2x+7: x+1=-2x+7\ \Rightarrow\ 3x=6\ \Rightarrow\ x=2, and then y=2+1=3. The solution is (2,\ 3).
Example 2

Graph both lines, read the crossing point, verify it

Consider the system of equations x-y=2 and 3x+y=-10.

(a) Graph both equations on the grid without using technology.
Rearranged for graphing: x-y=2\Rightarrow y=x-2 (slope 1, y-intercept -2), and 3x+y=-10\Rightarrow y=-3x-10 (slope -3, y-intercept -10, off this grid: start from (-4,\ 2) instead).
(b) State the solution to the system.
The lines cross at (-2,\ -4), so the solution is \boldsymbol{x=-2,\ y=-4}.
(c) Verify the solution algebraically by substituting the values into both original equations.
x-y=(-2)-(-4)=-2+4=2 ✓
3x+y=3(-2)+(-4)=-6-4=-10 ✓
Both equations balance, so (-2,\ -4) is confirmed as the solution.

Example 2 check: two lines by hand

Rearrange, graph each line, read the crossing, then check it in both original equations.

Using a System to Model a Real Situation

Many real situations involve two unknown quantities linked by two separate conditions. Writing one equation for each condition produces a system whose solution answers the original question.

Example 3

Two families, two ticket prices

The Alvarez family pays $9 in entry fees for 2 adult tickets and 1 child ticket to a charity fun run. The Bennett family pays $7 in entry fees for 1 adult ticket and 3 child tickets. Let x be the entry fee (in dollars) for an adult ticket and y the entry fee (in dollars) for a child ticket, so that the Alvarez fees are modelled by 2x+y=9 and the Bennett fees by x+3y=7.

(a) Graph both equations without technology.
Rearranged for graphing: y=9-2x and y=\tfrac{7-x}{3}. Or use the intercepts: (0,\ 9) and (4.5,\ 0); (0,\ 2\tfrac13) and (7,\ 0).
(b) State the coordinates of the point of intersection, and explain what the coordinates represent in the context of the question.
The lines cross at (4,\ 1). In context, the adult entry fee is x=\$4 and the child entry fee is y=\$1.
Check against the words: the Alvarez family’s 2 adults and 1 child cost 2(4)+1=\$9 ✓, and the Bennett family’s 1 adult and 3 children cost 4+3(1)=\$7 ✓.

Example 3 check: from the story to the crossing point

One equation for each family. Each line holds every price pair that fits one family; the crossing fits both.

Solving a System With a Graphing Calculator

A hand-drawn graph gives an exact answer only when the lines cross on grid lines. A graphing calculator finds the point of intersection to several decimal places, whatever its coordinates.

Graphing calculator steps (TI-83/84). These keys are on the graphing calculator, not the TI-30XIIS scientific calculator built into this page.
Steps (TI-84)
  1. Solve each equation for y.
  2. Press Y= and enter one equation in Y1 and the other in Y2.
  3. Press WINDOW and set Xmin, Xmax, Xscl (distance between tick marks), Ymin, Ymax, Yscl so the crossing point is on the screen. A window is written x{:}\,[\text{Xmin},\text{Xmax},\text{Xscl}], y{:}\,[\text{Ymin},\text{Ymax},\text{Yscl}], for example x{:}\,[0,350,50], y{:}\,[0,500,50]. (ZOOM 6:ZStandard gives x{:}\,[-10,10,1], y{:}\,[-10,10,1].)
  4. Press GRAPH, then 2nd TRACE (CALC) and choose 5:intersect. Press ENTER at First curve?, again at Second curve?, move the cursor close to the crossing point and press ENTER at Guess?.
  5. Read X= and Y=, round as the question asks, and verify by substituting.
Example 4

A solution that is hard to read by hand

Consider the system of equations 4x+3y=2 and 2x-y=5.

(a) Rewrite each equation in the form y=\ldots, ready for Y=.
4x+3y=2\Rightarrow 3y=-4x+2\Rightarrow y=-\tfrac43x+\tfrac23
2x-y=5\Rightarrow -y=-2x+5\Rightarrow y=2x-5
Enter Y1=(−4/3)X+2/3 and Y2=2X−5.
(c) Verify the solution by substituting into both original equations.
4(1.7)+3(-1.6)=6.8-4.8=2 ✓
2(1.7)-(-1.6)=3.4+1.6=5 ✓
Both equations balance.
(b) Graph the system in the standard window and use 5:intersect to find the solution.
The calculator shows X=1.7, Y=−1.6, so the solution is \boldsymbol{(1.7,\ -1.6)}.
(d) Why would it be hard to find this solution from a hand-drawn graph?
The point (1.7,\ -1.6) is not on grid lines, so it can only be estimated by eye. (Lesson 2 shows how to get exact answers algebraically.)
Part (c) on the TI-30XIIS. 4×1.7+3×(−)1.6ENTER gives 2, and 2×1.7−(−)1.6ENTER gives 5. Use (−) for the negative, not −.

Example 4 check: why the calculator?

By eye, the crossing looks close to a grid point. Check the guess in both equations, then zoom in.

An Application: Break-Even Analysis

A business breaks even when its cost and its revenue are equal: the point where the cost line and the revenue line intersect. Both are measured in dollars, so they share one vertical axis, and since both equations are written as “dollars =\ldots”, the break-even point can also be found by setting the two expressions equal (the shortcut from Section 3).
Loss cost line above the revenue line: R<C
Break even the lines cross: R=C
Profit revenue line above the cost line: R>C
Example 5

Cost, revenue and the break-even point

The Robotics Club is fundraising by selling custom mugs. Designing and printing the mugs has a fixed setup cost of $450, plus $3 per mug produced. The club plans to sell the mugs for $9 each. The cost C and the revenue R, both in dollars, from producing and selling n mugs are

Cost: C=450+3nRevenue: R=9n

(a) Graph each equation on the grid provided.
C=450+3n starts at $450 (the setup cost) and rises $3 per mug; R=9n starts at 0 and rises $9 per mug.
(b) How much profit or loss is made if (i) 30 mugs are sold? (ii) 90 mugs are sold?
(i) n=30: cost =450+3(30)=540; revenue =9(30)=270. 270-540=-270, a loss of $270.
(ii) n=90: cost =450+3(90)=720; revenue =9(90)=810. 810-720=90, a profit of $90.
(c) Determine algebraically the number of mugs which must be sold to break even.
At break-even, cost equals revenue, so set the expressions equal: 450+3n=9n\Rightarrow 450=6n\Rightarrow n=75. The club must sell 75 mugs, matching the graph.
(d) The break-even point is where no profit or loss is made. Mark the break-even point on the grid.
Where the two lines cross: (75,\ 675), 75 mugs and $675.
(e) Shade the region of the graph where the club makes a profit.
Profit means R>C: between the lines to the right of n=75, where the revenue line is above the cost line. To the left, the cost line is higher: a loss.
(f) If all 250 students in the school bought a mug, how much profit would the Robotics Club make?
n=250: cost =450+3(250)=1200; revenue =9(250)=2250. Profit =2250-1200= $1050.
Profit in one line. Profit = revenue − cost: 9×30−(450+3×30)ENTER gives −270: negative means a loss. Bracket the whole cost.

Example 5 check: watch the gap between the lines

At any number of mugs, the vertical gap between the lines is the profit (revenue on top) or the loss (cost on top).

Key ideas

System and solution. A system is two (or more) equations taken together. Its solution is an ordered pair (x,\ y) that satisfies every equation: the point of intersection of the lines.
Solve by graphing. Graph both lines (table of values, slope and y-intercept, or intercepts), read the crossing point, then verify in BOTH original equations. Both in y=\ldots form? Set the expressions equal.
Graphing calculator. Solve for y, Y=, choose a WINDOW that shows the crossing, GRAPH, 2nd TRACE 5:intersect, ENTER three times. Best when the crossing is not on grid lines.
In context. Say what each coordinate means (adult fee $4, child fee $1). Break-even: cost = revenue, where the lines cross. Revenue above cost is a profit, below is a loss.
