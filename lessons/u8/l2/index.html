Math 10C · Unit 8 Systems of Linear Equations · Lesson 2

Solving Systems of Equations by Substitution

An exact algebraic method: isolate one variable, substitute it into the other equation, solve, then back-substitute. Two equations in two unknowns collapse into one equation in one unknown, and the answer comes out exactly, fractions and all.

I can…
  • solve a system of two linear equations by substitution, using the four steps, and verify the solution in both equations
  • choose which equation and which variable to isolate (a coefficient of 1 or -1 avoids fractions), and get the same solution either way
  • handle a rearrangement that gives a fraction, and give the solution as exact values instead of a calculator decimal
  • expand brackets before substituting, and explain what 0=0 or 0=k means for the number of solutions
  • Extension solve three equations in three unknowns by substitution
RF9 Solve problems that involve systems of linear equations in two variables, graphically and algebraically.

The Idea Behind Substitution

Reading a solution off a grid is only as precise as the grid, and it breaks down when the solution involves fractions. If one equation can be rearranged so that a single variable stands alone on one side, that expression can be dropped directly into the other equation in place of that variable, collapsing two equations in two unknowns into one equation in a single unknown.

Step 1
Isolate
Choose whichever equation is easier to rearrange, and solve it for one variable in terms of the other.
Step 2
Substitute
Substitute that expression into the other equation. This produces one equation in a single variable.
Step 3
Solve
Solve the equation from Step 2 for the remaining variable.
Step 4
Back-substitute
Substitute the value from Step 3 back into the expression from Step 1 to find the other variable.
Substitution means “replace a variable with what it equals.” Put the expression in brackets when it goes in: the whole expression gets multiplied.
Think In Example 1, what happens if you substitute x=13-3y back into the same equation, x+3y=13?
(13-3y)+3y=13 gives 13=13. True, but useless: the y-terms cancel because the expression came from that equation. Always substitute into the other equation.

Which variable should I isolate?

Any variable in either equation works, and every choice leads to the same solution. Some choices are just tidier: look for a coefficient of 1 or -1, because then nothing has to be divided and no fractions appear.

Example 1

One system, solved two different ways

Consider the following system of equations: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{x+3y=13}\qquad\textcolor{#c27a0e}{2x-y=5}

(a) Solve the system using the method of substitution by rewriting the first equation in the form x=\ldots
Step 1: x+3y=13\Rightarrow x=13-3y
Step 2: substitute into 2x-y=5: 2(13-3y)-y=5
Step 3: 26-6y-y=5, 26-7y=5\Rightarrow -7y=-21\Rightarrow y=3
Step 4: x=13-3(3)=13-9=4
The solution is x=4,\ y=3, the point \boldsymbol{(4,\ 3)}.
(b) Solve the same system again, this time rewriting the first equation in the form y=\ldots You should arrive at the same solution.
Step 1: x+3y=13\Rightarrow 3y=13-x\Rightarrow y=\dfrac{13-x}{3}
Step 2: 2x-\dfrac{13-x}{3}=5
Step 3: multiply every term by 3: 6x-(13-x)=15, 6x-13+x=15\Rightarrow 7x=28\Rightarrow x=4
Step 4: y=\dfrac{13-4}{3}=\dfrac{9}{3}=3
Both routes give the same solution, \boldsymbol{(4,\ 3)}.
(c) Verify that your solution satisfies both original equations.
In x+3y=13: 4+3(3)=4+9=13 ✓    In 2x-y=5: 2(4)-3=8-3=5 ✓

Example 1 (c) check: a solution works in both equations

A point that works in only one equation lies on only one line. The solution of a system is the one point on both lines, so it has to balance both equations.

Example 2

A rearrangement that produces a fraction

Consider the following system of equations: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{2x+3y=0}\qquad\textcolor{#c27a0e}{4x-5y=22}

Solve the system using the method of substitution, and verify that your solution satisfies both equations. (Notice that the first equation has no constant term: rearranging it will produce a fraction, and that is perfectly normal.)

Solve and verify.
Step 1: 2x+3y=0\Rightarrow 2x=-3y\Rightarrow x=-\dfrac{3y}{2}
Step 2: substitute into 4x-5y=22: 4\left(-\dfrac{3y}{2}\right)-5y=22
Step 3: -6y-5y=22\Rightarrow -11y=22\Rightarrow y=-2
Step 4: x=-\dfrac{3(-2)}{2}=\dfrac{6}{2}=3. The solution is \boldsymbol{x=3,\ y=-2}.
Check in 2x+3y=0: 2(3)+3(-2)=6-6=0 ✓   Check in 4x-5y=22: 4(3)-5(-2)=12+10=22 ✓
Check (3,\ -2) in 4x-5y=22 on the TI-30XIIS. 4×3−5×(−)2ENTER gives 22 ✓. Use (−) for the negative, not −.

Fractions that clear

A fraction in Step 2 is nothing to fear. Either multiply every term by the denominator, or let a coefficient cancel it. Keep the brackets until the very end: a minus sign in front of a bracket changes the sign of every term inside.

!
Watch the minus in front of a fraction. -\dfrac{13-x}{3} times 3 is -(13-x)=-13+x, not -13-x.
Example 3

Exact values where a graph can only estimate

Graphed by hand, the lines \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{4x+3y=2} and \textcolor{#c27a0e}{2x-y=5} cross between grid lines, so the graph can only give an estimate of the solution (Lesson 1). Substitution gives the solution as exact values.

(a) Rewrite 2x-y=5 in the form y=\ldots
2x-y=5\Rightarrow -y=-2x+5\Rightarrow \boldsymbol{y=2x-5}
(c) Verify the solution by substituting into both original equations.
4\left(\tfrac{17}{10}\right)+3\left(-\tfrac{8}{5}\right)=\tfrac{68}{10}-\tfrac{48}{10}=\tfrac{20}{10}=2 ✓
2\left(\tfrac{17}{10}\right)-\left(-\tfrac{8}{5}\right)=\tfrac{34}{10}+\tfrac{16}{10}=\tfrac{50}{10}=5 ✓
Both equations balance, confirming the solution.
(b) Solve the system by substitution, and express the solution using exact values (fractions, not decimals).
Substitute y=2x-5 into 4x+3y=2: 4x+3(2x-5)=2\Rightarrow 4x+6x-15=2\Rightarrow 10x=17\Rightarrow x=\tfrac{17}{10}
y=2\left(\tfrac{17}{10}\right)-5=\tfrac{34}{10}-\tfrac{50}{10}=-\tfrac{16}{10}=-\tfrac{8}{5}
The exact solution is \boldsymbol{x=\tfrac{17}{10},\ y=-\tfrac{8}{5}}, that is, (1.7,\ -1.6): a point between the grid lines.
Check the fractions on the TI-30XIIS with the fraction key a b/c. In 4x+3y: 4×17a b/c10+3×(−)8a b/c5ENTER gives 2 ✓. In 2x-y: gives 5 ✓.

Exact values vs a graph’s estimate

When the lines cross between grid lines, a hand-drawn graph can only give an estimate, and an estimate usually fails the check in at least one equation. Substitution gives the exact value every time.

Substitution When an Equation Must Be Expanded First

Sometimes brackets have to be expanded and like terms collected before either equation can be solved for one variable in terms of the other. Always clear the brackets first, then proceed with the same four steps.

!
The sign goes with the number in front of the bracket. In a-2(b-3) the multiplier is -2, so the last term is (-2)(-3)=+6.
Example 4

Expanding before substituting

Consider the following system of equations: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{a-2(b-3)=8}\qquad\textcolor{#c27a0e}{4a+3(b-2)=13}

(a) Expand both equations, then solve the system using the method of substitution.
Expand the first: a-2b+6=8\Rightarrow a-2b=2
Expand the second: 4a+3b-6=13\Rightarrow 4a+3b=19
Step 1: a=2b+2
Step 2: 4(2b+2)+3b=19
Step 3: 8b+8+3b=19\Rightarrow 11b=11\Rightarrow b=1
Step 4: a=2(1)+2=4. So \boldsymbol{a=4,\ b=1}.
(b) Verify algebraically that your solution satisfies both of the original (unexpanded) equations.
In a-2(b-3)=8:
4-2(1-3)=4-2(-2)=4+4=8 ✓
In 4a+3(b-2)=13:
4(4)+3(1-2)=16+3(-1)=16-3=13 ✓

When both variables disappear: 0=0 or 0=k

Sometimes substituting makes both variables cancel. What is left tells you the number of solutions: the same three cases as the Lesson 1 chart.

(i) y=3x-1 and 6x-2y=2
6x-2(3x-1)=2
6x-6x+2=2
2=2, i.e. 0=0: always true.
Every x works: infinitely many solutions. 6x-2y=2 is y=3x-1 again: the same line (equal slopes, equal intercepts).
(ii) y=3x-1 and 6x-2y=8
6x-2(3x-1)=8
6x-6x+2=8
2=8, i.e. 0=6: never true.
No x works: no solution. 6x-2y=8 is y=3x-4: parallel lines (equal slopes 3, different intercepts -1 and -4).

Three Equations in Three Unknowns

Extension
ExtensionSystems of three equations go beyond the Math 10C outcomes. The idea is the same: substitute until only one unknown is left, solve, then back-substitute.
Example 5

Three equations in three unknowns

Extension

Solve the following system using substitution. \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{4p-3q-2r=-9}\qquad\textcolor{#c27a0e}{r=2p}\qquad\textcolor{#12805a}{3q-p-5=0}

Hint: the second and third equations already give r and q each in terms of p alone. Substitute both into the first equation to obtain a single equation in p.

Solve and check.
Step 1: from the third equation, 3q=p+5\Rightarrow q=\dfrac{p+5}{3}. The second already gives r=2p.
Step 2: substitute both into 4p-3q-2r=-9: 4p-3\left(\dfrac{p+5}{3}\right)-2(2p)=-9
Step 3: 4p-(p+5)-4p=-9\Rightarrow -p-5=-9\Rightarrow -p=-4\Rightarrow p=4
Step 4: r=2(4)=8 and q=\dfrac{4+5}{3}=\dfrac{9}{3}=3. So \boldsymbol{p=4,\ q=3,\ r=8}.
Check: 4(4)-3(3)-2(8)=16-9-16=-9 ✓   r=8=2(4) ✓   3(3)-4-5=9-9=0 ✓

Example 5 check: three unknowns down to one

Two expressions lift at once, and three unknowns collapse to one.

✓

Key ideas

The four steps. Isolate a variable; substitute its expression, in brackets, into the other equation; solve the one-variable equation; back-substitute. Verify in both equations.
Choose wisely. A coefficient of 1 or -1 means no fractions. Any choice gives the same solution. A fraction is normal: multiply every term by the denominator, or let a coefficient cancel it.
Exact values. Substitution gives fractions like \left(\tfrac{17}{10},\ -\tfrac{8}{5}\right) exactly; a hand-drawn graph can only estimate them, and a decimal may be rounded. Expand brackets first, then use the same four steps.
When both variables vanish. 0=0 (always true): infinitely many solutions, the same line. 0=k with k\ne0 (never true): no solution, parallel lines.
