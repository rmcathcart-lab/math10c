Solving Word Problems with Systems of Equations

Translating a situation described in words into two equations, solving the system, and interpreting the answer in context. Numbers, rectangles, money, investments and machines: the same four steps work every time.

Warm-up

How Old Am I?

Maya and her little brother Leo. Everyone ages one year per year. Right now Maya is 12 and Leo is 4.

1
How old were they 4 years ago?
Maya was 8 and Leo was 0: Leo had just been born.
2
How old will they be in 4 years? Now Maya is 3 times Leo’s age. Is she still?
Maya 16, Leo 8. No: 16=2\times8, so she is only twice his age then. The “times as old” number shrinks as they grow up.
3
In each picture, how much older is Maya? Will that ever change?
Always 8 years (8-0, 12-4, 16-8). The difference never changes, because both add the same number of years.
!
The big one. Sam’s dad says: “Right now I’m 4 times your age. In 20 years, I’ll be only twice your age.” How old is Sam? Guess first.
A 5
B 10
C 20
D Impossible
B: Sam is 10 (Dad is 40). Check: 40=4\times10 ✓. In 20 years: Dad 60, Sam 30, and 60=2\times30 ✓.
A (5): Dad 20 now; in 20 years 40 and 25, not double. C (20): Dad 80 now; in 20 years 100 and 40, which is 2.5 times. D is the big misconception: “4 times as old” does not last forever. Question 2 showed the ratio shrinks while the gap stays fixed.
+
Early finishers. Mom is 30 years older than her daughter. In 5 years she will be 3 times as old. How old is each now?
Daughter 10, Mom 40: m=k+30 and m+5=3(k+5). Check: in 5 years, 45=3\times15 ✓.

A Four-Step Strategy for Word Problems

Almost every application problem in this lesson can be attacked with the same four steps, whether the situation involves numbers, geometry, money, or rates.

Step 1
Introduce variables to represent the unknown quantities. Say what each one stands for and in what units.
Step 2
Use the given information to form a system of two equations in those variables.
Step 3
Solve the system, using graphing, substitution, or elimination.
Step 4
Answer the original question in words, and check that your solution makes sense in the context of the problem.

Watch one problem travel through the four steps: the sentences light up, the unknowns become a “let” card, the facts go into a table, and each row of the table becomes an equation.

Step 3: choosing a method (the table from Lesson 3, Section 6)

Graphing
a picture or estimate is enough, or a graphing calculator is allowed (by hand: integer solutions only)
Substitution
a variable already has coefficient 1 or -1, or is isolated, e.g. f+t=23\Rightarrow f=23-t
Elimination
the coefficients of one variable already match or are opposites, or are easy multiples

Step 4 is not optional

A system can be solved correctly and still answer the wrong question, so check the solution against the words as well as against the equations: totals must add up, counts of objects must be whole numbers, and prices, lengths and times must be positive.

Number Relationships

Some problems describe a relationship between two unknown numbers with no physical context at all. The words sum, difference, more than and twice translate directly into algebraic expressions.

sumx+y
differencex-ylarger − smaller
2 more than yy+2
twice y2y
2 less than yy-2not 2-y
!
“More than” and “less than” flip the order. “2 more than twice the smaller” starts with 2 but is written 2y+2: the 2 is added after. “7 less than n” is n-7, not 7-n.
Example 1

Two numbers, a difference and a “more than” relationship

The difference between two numbers is 11. The larger number is 2 more than twice the smaller number.

(a) Introduce variables for the larger number and the smaller number.
Let x be the larger number and let y be the smaller number.
(Both are pure numbers, so there are no units to attach.)
(b) Use the given information to form a system of two equations.
“The difference between two numbers is 11”: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{x-y=11}
“The larger is 2 more than twice the smaller”: x=2y+2, i.e. \textcolor{#c27a0e}{x-2y=2}
System: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{x-y=11}\qquad \textcolor{#c27a0e}{x-2y=2}
(c) Solve the system to find both numbers.
Both equations have the same x-coefficient, so subtract Eq. 2 from Eq. 1 to eliminate x:
(\textcolor{#1f5bd6}{x-y})-(\textcolor{#c27a0e}{x-2y})=11-2
-y+2y=9\ \Rightarrow\ y=9
Into x-y=11: x-9=11\ \Rightarrow\ x=20
(d) State the answer in words, and verify that both numbers satisfy the original conditions.
Check the equations: 20-9=11 ✓ and 2(9)+2=20 ✓
Check the words: 20 really is the larger of the two ✓
The two numbers are 20 and 9.

Perimeter and Dimension Problems

When a problem gives the perimeter of a rectangle together with a relationship between its length and width, the perimeter formula and the length–width relationship become the two equations.

Perimeter (Eq. 1): 2l+2w=P, or halve it: l+w=\tfrac{P}{2}. One length plus one width is half the fence.
Length–width relationship (Eq. 2): e.g. “the length is 3 more than twice the width” is l=2w+3.
Example 2

Perimeter and a length–width relationship

A rectangular garden has a perimeter of 54 metres. Its length is 3 metres more than twice its width. Find the dimensions of the garden.

1 Let2 Two equations3 Solve4 Answer & check
Let l be the length of the garden and w its width, both in metres.
Perimeter: 2(l+w)=54, i.e. \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{l+w=27}. Length condition: \textcolor{#c27a0e}{l=2w+3}.
Substituting the second equation into the first: (\textcolor{#c27a0e}{2w+3})+w=27\ \Rightarrow\ 3w+3=27\ \Rightarrow\ 3w=24\ \Rightarrow\ w=8, so l=2(8)+3=19.
Check the equations: l+w=19+8=27, so the perimeter is 2(27)=54 ✓; 2(8)+3=19 ✓
Check the words: both dimensions are positive, and 19 m is indeed 3 m more than twice 8 m ✓
The garden is \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{19} m long and \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{8} m wide.

Money and Denomination Problems

Here one equation counts how many items there are and the other totals their value. Keeping the two straight (one for a count, one for a value) is the key to setting up the system correctly.

Count equation (Eq. 1)
every bill counts as 1, whatever it is worth
f+t=\text{number of bills}
Value equation (Eq. 2)
each bill counts as its value in dollars
5f+20t=\text{total value}
!
Don’t mix a count with a value. Adding numbers of bills to dollars, as in f+t=310, mixes two different units. Read the units in each equation: bills + bills = bills; dollars + dollars = dollars.
Example 3

One equation counts, the other totals value

Maria has a total of $310 in five-dollar bills and twenty-dollar bills. If she has 23 bills in total, how many of each denomination does she have?

1 Let2 Two equations3 Solve4 Answer & check
Let f be the number of five-dollar bills and t the number of twenty-dollar bills (counts of bills).
Counting bills: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{f+t=23}. Totalling value, in dollars: \textcolor{#c27a0e}{5f+20t=310}.
From the first equation, f=23-t. Substituting: 5(23-t)+20t=310\ \Rightarrow\ 115-5t+20t=310\ \Rightarrow\ 115+15t=310\ \Rightarrow\ 15t=195\ \Rightarrow\ t=13, so f=23-13=10.
Check the equations: 10+13=23 ✓; 5(10)+20(13)=50+260=310 ✓
Check the words: both counts are whole, positive numbers, as counts of bills must be ✓
Maria has \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{10} five-dollar bills and \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{13} twenty-dollar bills.

Investment and Interest Problems

Investment problems work the same way, except the two equations total the principal invested and the interest earned. Interest rates given as percentages must be converted to decimals before they are used in an equation.

Principal equation (Eq. 1)
the two amounts add up to the total invested
x+y=\text{total invested}
Interest equation (Eq. 2)
rate (as a decimal) × amount, added up
8\%=\tfrac{8}{100}=0.08, so 0.08x+0.05y=\text{interest}
Interest on the calculator. 8% of $18 000: .08×18000ENTER. The overall rate: 2340÷36000ENTER gives 0.065, which is 6.5%.
Example 4

One equation totals principal, the other totals interest

Devon invested his savings of $36 000 in two accounts. One account earned 8\% interest and the other earned 5\% interest. If he earned a total of $2340 in interest for the year, how much did he invest at each rate?

1 Let2 Two equations3 Solve4 Answer & check
Let x be the amount invested at 8\% and y the amount invested at 5\%, both in dollars.
Totalling principal: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{x+y=36\,000}. Totalling interest: \textcolor{#c27a0e}{0.08x+0.05y=2340}.
From the first equation, y=36\,000-x. Substituting: 0.08x+0.05(36\,000-x)=2340\ \Rightarrow\ 0.08x+1800-0.05x=2340\ \Rightarrow\ 0.03x=540\ \Rightarrow\ x=18\,000, so y=36\,000-18\,000=18\,000.
Check the equations: 18\,000+18\,000=36\,000 ✓; 0.08(18\,000)+0.05(18\,000)=1440+900=2340 ✓
Check the words: both amounts are positive, and the overall rate 2340\div36\,000=6.5\% lies between 5\% and 8\%, as it must ✓
Devon invested \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{\$18\,000} at 8\% and \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{\$18\,000} at 5\%.

Rate and Production Problems

A final common type gives two different rates (two machines producing items at different speeds, say) together with a combined total. One equation totals the time, the other totals the output.

Time equation (Eq. 1)
hours + hours = total hours
o+n=\text{total time}
Output equation (Eq. 2)
rate × time for each machine, added up
25o+35n=\text{total output}

Each equation is a line. A point on one line satisfies only one condition; the crossing satisfies both.

Example 5

One equation totals time, the other totals output

A factory has an older machine that packs 25 boxes per hour and a newer machine that packs 35 boxes per hour. Yesterday the two machines ran for a combined total of 18 hours and packed 550 boxes. For how many hours did each machine run?

1 Let2 Two equations3 Solve4 Answer & check
Let o be the number of hours the older machine ran and n the number of hours the newer machine ran.
Totalling time, in hours: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{o+n=18}. Totalling output, in boxes: \textcolor{#c27a0e}{25o+35n=550}.
From the first equation, o=18-n. Substituting: 25(18-n)+35n=550\ \Rightarrow\ 450-25n+35n=550\ \Rightarrow\ 450+10n=550\ \Rightarrow\ 10n=100\ \Rightarrow\ n=10, so o=18-10=8.
Check the equations: 8+10=18 ✓; 25(8)+35(10)=200+350=550 ✓
Check the words: both running times are positive, and the average rate 550\div18\approx30.6 boxes per hour lies between 25 and 35 ✓
The older machine ran for \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{8} hours and the newer machine ran for \textcolor{#7b4fc4}{10} hours.
Does the average rate make sense? 550÷18ENTER gives about 30.6 boxes per hour: between the two machines’ rates, 25 and 35.

Key ideas

Four steps. 1 Let statements (what each variable stands for, with units). 2 Two equations from the facts. 3 Solve: graphing, substitution or elimination. 4 Answer the question in words and check.
Translate the words. Sum x+y, difference x-y, twice 2y, “2 more than twice y” 2y+2. Rectangles: 2l+2w=P plus the length–width relationship.
Two kinds of total. Money: one equation counts (f+t=23), one totals value (5f+20t=310). Investments: principal and interest (rates as decimals). Machines: time and output.
Check against the story. Totals add up, counts are whole numbers, prices, lengths and times are positive, and the sentence answers the question that was asked.
