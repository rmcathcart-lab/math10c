Mixture and Motion: More Applications of Linear Systems

Blending two ingredients that differ in cost or concentration, and journeys made of two legs or helped and hindered by a current or wind. Every problem today becomes a system of two equations, organized in a table first.

I can…
  • organize a mixture problem in an amount × rate = value table, and write one equation from the Amount column and one from the Value column
  • define each variable with its unit, and clear percent decimals before solving
  • use d=st to organize a journey with two legs, subtracting stopped time from the total
  • write the ground speed as b+c with a current or wind and b-c against it, then solve for both speeds
  • check the solution against both original conditions and answer in a sentence
RF9 Solve problems that involve systems of linear equations in two variables, graphically and algebraically.
Warm-up

Upstream, Downstream

Moving water and moving air can help you or fight you.

1
How fast does the kayak really go downstream?
6+2=\boldsymbol{8} km/h: the current carries the kayak along.
2
How fast going upstream?
6-2=\boldsymbol{4} km/h: the current pushes back.
3
A new kayaker clocks 10 km/h downstream and 6 km/h upstream. How fast is that current?
2 km/h. Downstream is “boat + current”, upstream is “boat - current”; the two differ by two currents: 10-6=4=2c, so c=2 (and the boat does 8 km/h in still water).
!
The big one. In still air the plane flies 300 km/h: there and back takes 4 h. Today a 60 km/h wind helps one way and fights the other. The round trip takes… Guess first.
A less than 4 h
B exactly 4 h
C more than 4 h
C: 4 h 10 min, longer than calm air. Out with the wind: 300+60=360 km/h, so 600\div360=1\tfrac23 h = 1 h 40 min. Back into the wind: 300-60=240 km/h, so 600\div240=2.5 h = 2 h 30 min.
B is the popular answer: “the wind helps as much as it hurts.” The speeds cancel, but the times don’t: the plane spends longer on the slow headwind leg, so the wind gets more time to hurt than to help. A comes from focusing on the exciting fast leg.
+
Early finishers. How strong a wind would make the round trip impossible?
A 300 km/h headwind: the plane’s ground speed would be 300-300=0, so it would hang in place.

Stand up for A, sit for B, hands on head for C, then defend your pick to a neighbour.

Mixture Applications

A mixture problem combines two ingredients (candies, chemical solutions, metals) that differ in some rate, such as price per kilogram or percent concentration. Two equations are always available:

Total amount
adds up the amount of the mixture: its mass, volume, or number of items.
Total value
adds up the value that amount carries: the total cost, or the total salt, gold, or alcohol content.

Write both equations down before solving; the system then yields to substitution or elimination exactly as in earlier lessons.

Mixture organizerAmount× Rate= Value
Ingredient Axrate A(rate A)\,x
Ingredient Byrate B(rate B)\,y
Mixturetotal amountmixture ratetotal value
The Amount column gives one equation (the two amounts add to the total amount); the Value column gives the other (the two values add to the total value). Rates are never added.
1
Define each variable with its unit (“x= mL of the 30% solution”), so that litres of solution are never confused with litres of pure acid.
2
Clear percent rates to integers before solving: multiplying every term of 0.30x+0.80y=125 by 10 gives 3x+8y=1\,250, which is easier to eliminate with.
The four steps (Lesson 4)
Step 1: Introduce variables to represent the unknown quantities. Say what each one stands for and in what units.
Step 2: Use the given information to form a system of two equations in those variables.
Step 3: Solve the system, using graphing, substitution, or elimination.
Step 4: Answer the original question in words, and check that your solution makes sense in the context of the problem.
Example 1

Blending by cost per kilogram

A café blends premium coffee beans costing $24/kg with standard coffee beans costing $14/kg. The blend weighs 60 kg in total and sells for $17/kg.

Amount (kg)× Rate ($/kg)= Value ($)
Premium beansp2424p
Standard beanss1414s
Blend601717(60)=1\,020
(a) Let p be the kg of premium beans and s the kg of standard beans. Complete the organizer, then write a system of two equations: one from the Amount column (total mass), one from the Value column (total value).
Let p be the mass of premium beans and s the mass of standard beans, in kg.
Mass: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{p+s=60}
Value: the blend is worth 17(60)=\$1\,020, so \textcolor{#c27a0e}{24p+14s=1\,020}
(b) Solve the system from part (a) to find p and s.
From the mass equation, s=60-p. Substitute into the value equation:
24p+14(60-p)=1\,020
24p+840-14p=1\,020
10p=180\Rightarrow p=18
s=60-18=42
(c) State how many kilograms of each bean are in the blend, and verify the solution in both original equations.
The blend uses 18 kg of premium beans and 42 kg of standard beans.
Mass: 18+42=60 kg ✓
Value: 24(18)+14(42)=432+588=\$1\,020, and 1\,020\div60=\$17/kg ✓
Check the value in one line. The calculator does × before +: 24×18+14×42ENTER gives 1 020; then ÷60ENTER gives the blend’s $17/kg.
Example 2

Blending by percent concentration

A lab technician needs 250 mL of a 50% saline solution. She has a stock solution that is 30% saline and another that is 80% saline.

(a) Let x be the mL of 30% solution and y the mL of 80% solution. Write a system of two equations: one for the total volume, one for the total amount of salt.
Let x be the volume of 30% solution and y the volume of 80% solution, in mL.
Volume: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{x+y=250}
Salt: the mixture holds 0.50(250)=125 mL of salt, so \textcolor{#c27a0e}{0.30x+0.80y=125}
(b) Solve the system from part (a) to find x and y.
Clear the decimals: multiply the salt equation by 10:
3x+8y=1\,250
From x+y=250, y=250-x. Substitute:
3x+8(250-x)=1\,250
3x+2\,000-8x=1\,250
-5x=-750\Rightarrow x=150
y=250-150=100
(c) State how many mL of each stock solution are required, and verify the solution.
She needs 150 mL of the 30% solution and 100 mL of the 80% solution.
Volume: 150+100=250 mL ✓
Salt: 0.30(150)+0.80(100)=45+80=125 mL
Concentration: 125\div250=0.50=50\% ✓
!
Salt, not solution. The Value column counts mL of salt; the Amount column counts mL of solution. Adding 30+80 (the rates) gives nothing useful: rates are never added.

Examples 1 and 2 check: solve, graph, check both

Each mixture system has a variable with coefficient 1, so substitution is quick: replace one variable with what it equals. The amount equation is blue and the value equation amber; the solution is where both lines meet.

Does the answer make sense? The balance point

The mixture rate is always between the two ingredient rates, and it sits closer to the ingredient you used more of. Picture the amounts as weights on a beam: the mixture rate is where the beam balances.

Think A shop mixes $8/kg cashews with $20/kg pecans and sells the mix for $11/kg. Without solving: is there more cashew or more pecan in the mix? Could the mix ever sell for $25/kg?
More cashews. $11 is much closer to $8 than to $20 (3 away versus 9 away), so the cashews outweigh the pecans, 3 to 1. Never $25: a mix can’t cost more than its most expensive ingredient.

Distance, Speed, and Time Applications

Distance, speed, and time are related by d=s\,t, which rearranges to s=\dfrac{d}{t} and t=\dfrac{d}{s}. The triangle is a memory aid: cover the quantity you want, and what is left shows how to build it from the other two.

Keep the units matched: km, km/h and hours together. A 30-minute break is 0.5 h.

Journeys in two legs

Most applications split a journey into two legs (two different speeds, or a trip out and a trip back), and the two equations come from a relationship between the two distances, the two times, or the two speeds.

A current or a wind that helps on one leg and hinders on the other is the standard case: if b is the speed in still water or still air and c is the speed of the current or wind, the ground speed is \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{b+c} going with it and \textcolor{#c27a0e}{b-c} going against it.
Motion organizerDistance= Speed× Time
Leg 1
Leg 2
Totaltotal distance—total time
Each leg’s row must satisfy d=s\times t. In the Total row, distances add and times add: speeds do not.
!
Stopped time is not driving time. Subtract any breaks from the total before writing the time equation.
Example 3

Two speeds on one trip

A trucker drove 810 km from Edmonton to Regina in 11.5 hours total, including a 1-hour fuel stop and a 30-minute rest break. While actually driving she averaged 95 km/h on the highway and 60 km/h on secondary roads.

Distance (km)= Speed (km/h)× Time (h)
Highway95h95h
Secondary roads60r60r
Total810—11.5-1.5=10
(a) Let h be the hours driven on the highway and r the hours driven on secondary roads. Complete the organizer, then write a system of two equations. (Hint: find the total driving time first by subtracting the stops.)
Stopped time is 1+0.5=1.5 h, so driving time is 11.5-1.5=10 h.
Let h be the highway driving time and r the secondary-road driving time, in hours.
Time: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{h+r=10}
Distance: \textcolor{#c27a0e}{95h+60r=810}
(b) Solve the system from part (a) to find h and r.
From the time equation, r=10-h. Substitute into the distance equation:
95h+60(10-h)=810
95h+600-60h=810
35h=210\Rightarrow h=6
r=10-6=4
(c) State how much time she spent driving on the highway, and verify the solution.
She drove 6 hours on the highway (and 4 hours on secondary roads).
Time: 6+4=10 h driving, plus 1.5 h stopped =11.5 h ✓
Distance: 95(6)=570 km and 60(4)=240 km; 570+240=810 km ✓

Example 3 check: take out the stops, then fill the organizer

The clock ran for 11.5 h, but the truck only moved for part of it. Times add and distances add; the two speeds stay in their own rows.

With the current, against the current

The boat (or plane) has its own speed b. The current (or wind) adds its speed c when it pushes the same way, and takes it away when it pushes the other way. Each trip gives a speed, \text{speed}=\tfrac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}, and each speed gives one equation.

Each trip’s speed: distance ÷ time. 96÷4ENTER gives 24 km/h downstream; 525÷2.5ENTER gives the plane’s 210 km/h into the headwind.
Example 4

Downstream and upstream

A ferry takes 4 hours to travel 96 km downstream (with the current) and 6 hours to travel the same 96 km upstream (against the current).

(a) Let b be the ferry’s speed in still water (km/h) and c the speed of the current (km/h). Using speed = distance \div time, write a system of two equations.
Let b be the ferry’s speed in still water and c the speed of the current, in km/h.
Downstream the current adds to b: \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{b+c=\tfrac{96}{4}=24}
Upstream the current subtracts from b: \textcolor{#c27a0e}{b-c=\tfrac{96}{6}=16}
(b) Solve the system from part (a) to find b and c.
The c terms are opposites, so add the equations:
2b=40\Rightarrow b=20
Substitute into b+c=24:
20+c=24\Rightarrow c=4
(c) State the speed of the current and the ferry’s speed in still water, and verify the solution.
The current runs at 4 km/h and the ferry makes 20 km/h in still water.
Downstream: 20+4=24 km/h, and 24(4)=96 km ✓
Upstream: 20-4=16 km/h, and 16(6)=96 km ✓
Example 5

Headwind and tailwind

A small plane flying against a headwind takes 2.5 hours to fly the 525 km from Calgary to Saskatoon. On the return trip, with the same wind now behind it, the plane takes only 1.75 hours to cover the same distance.

(a) Let p be the plane’s speed in still air (km/h) and w the speed of the wind (km/h). Write a system of two equations.
Let p be the plane’s speed in still air and w the speed of the wind, in km/h.
Against the headwind the ground speed is p-w: (p-w)(2.5)=525\Rightarrow \textcolor{#c27a0e}{p-w=210}
With the tailwind the ground speed is p+w: (p+w)(1.75)=525\Rightarrow \textcolor{#1f5bd6}{p+w=300}
(b) Solve the system from part (a) to find p and w.
The w terms are opposites, so add the equations:
2p=510\Rightarrow p=255
Substitute into p+w=300:
255+w=300\Rightarrow w=45
(c) State the speed of the wind and the plane’s speed in still air, and verify the solution.
The wind blows at 45 km/h and the plane flies 255 km/h in still air.
Outbound: 255-45=210 km/h, and 210(2.5)=525 km ✓
Return: 255+45=300 km/h, and 300(1.75)=525 km ✓

Examples 4 and 5 check: add the equations and the current cancels

With +c in one equation and -c in the other, elimination by adding is instant. On a number line, the still-water speed b sits exactly halfway between the two ground speeds, and the current is the distance from b to either one.

✓

Key ideas

Mixtures. Amount \times rate = value in every row. The Amount column gives one equation (amounts add to the total), the Value column the other (values add to the total value). Rates are never added.
Habits. Define each variable with its unit (mL of solution, not mL of salt). Clear decimals: 0.30x+0.80y=125 becomes 3x+8y=1\,250. The mixture rate always lies between the two rates.
Motion. d=st, s=\tfrac{d}{t}, t=\tfrac{d}{s}: cover the one you want. Two legs: distances add, times add, speeds don’t. Subtract stopped time before writing the time equation.
Current or wind. With it: b+c; against it: b-c. Find each trip’s speed with \tfrac{d}{t}, then add the equations: c cancels. Check against both trips and answer in a sentence.
