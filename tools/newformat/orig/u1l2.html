Prime Factorization at Work: GCF and LCM

Once a number is broken into primes, a lot of questions get easy. Today prime factors give us a fast, reliable route to the greatest common factor and the least common multiple — and we learn to tell which one a problem is asking for.

I can…
  • find the greatest common factor of a group of numbers, by inspection and by prime factorization
  • find the least common multiple of a group of numbers, by listing multiples and by prime factorization
  • decide whether a word problem needs the GCF or the LCM, and solve it
  • check a GCF or an LCM on the calculator
AN1 Demonstrate an understanding of factors of whole numbers by determining the prime factors, greatest common factor, least common multiple, square root and cube root.

1

Greatest Common Factor

The greatest common factor (GCF) of a group of whole numbers is the largest whole number that divides evenly into every member of the group.
The GCF of 10, 15 and 25 is 5.

Back to the warm-up: the number of take-home bags had to divide into both 24 and 32. Numbers that divide into every number in a group are its common factors; the most bags is the greatest common factor.

!
Sanity check. The GCF can never be bigger than the smallest number in the group — it has to fit into that number.
Example 1

GCF by inspection

State the greatest common factor of each group.

(a) 16, 24, and 40
16 = 24, 24 = 23 × 3, 40 = 23 × 5
only 2 is in all three, lowest power 23
GCF = 8
(b) 21 and 28
21 = 3 × 7, 28 = 22 × 7
the only common prime is 7
GCF = 7
(c) 48 and 72
48 = 24 × 3, 72 = 23 × 32
lower power of each: 23 × 3
GCF = 24

A shortcut: prime factorization

Inspection gets hard once a number has lots of factors, like in (c). Prime factors give a shortcut. From the starter:

Division ladder: 60
260230315551
Division ladder: 90
290345315551
Factor tree: 60
To find the GCF, multiply together every prime factor (matching repeats) that appears in every factorization.

60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5     90 = 2 × 3 × 3 × 5both share one 2, one 3 and one 5, so the GCF of 60 and 90 is 2 × 3 × 5 = 30

With exponents: the lowest power

The GCF uses only the primes that appear in every factorization, each to its lowest power. Here 60 = 22 × 3 × 5 and 90 = 2 × 32 × 5, so

GCF = 21 × 31 × 51 = 30

Check a GCF by dividing. Every number in the group ÷ the GCF should be a whole number: 396÷44ENTER gives 9. If the answers (here 3, 5, 7, 9) share no common factor, nothing bigger will work.

Picture it: the biggest square tile

Cover a 60 by 90 rectangle with identical square tiles, none cut. A tile only fits if its side divides both sides of the rectangle — it must be a common factor. The biggest tile that fits is the GCF.

2

Least Common Multiple

The least common multiple (LCM) of a group of numbers is the smallest number that appears in every list of multiples.

9: 9, 18, 27, 36, 45, …     12: 12, 24, 36, 48, …the first number in both lists is 36, so the LCM of 9 and 12 is 36

!
Multiplying gives a common multiple — not always the least. 6 × 8 = 48, but 6 and 8 both land on 24 first.
!
Sanity check. The LCM can never be smaller than the largest number in the group.
Example 3

LCM by listing multiples

State the least common multiple of each group.

(a) 5 and 9
5 and 9 share no common factor (except 1), so the LCM is their product
LCM = 45
(b) 6, 8, and 12
multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, …
24 is also a multiple of 6 and of 12
LCM = 24
(c) 15, 18, and 21
15 = 3 × 5, 18 = 2 × 32, 21 = 3 × 7
highest powers: 2, 32, 5, 7
LCM = 2 × 9 × 5 × 7 = 630

A shortcut: prime factorization

Trial and error gets slow once the numbers grow, like in (c). Prime factors make it manageable:

15 = 3 × 5     18 = 2 × 3 × 3     21 = 3 × 7

To build the LCM, start with all the prime factors of one number, then bring in any extra factors needed from the others (factors already counted are not repeated).
3 and 5 from 15, an extra 2 and 3 from 18, a 7 from 21:  3 × 5 × 2 × 3 × 7 = 630

With exponents: the highest power

The LCM uses every prime that appears in any of the factorizations, each to its highest power. Here 15 = 3 × 5, 18 = 2 × 32 and 21 = 3 × 7, so

LCM = 2 × 32 × 5 × 7 = 630

Check an LCM by dividing. The LCM ÷ each number should be whole: 630÷21ENTER gives 30. (630 ÷ 15 = 42 and 630 ÷ 18 = 35 too.)
Example 4

LCM by prime factorization

Use prime factorization to determine the least common multiple of each group.

(a) 18 and 45
18 = 2 × 32, 45 = 32 × 5
highest: 2, 32, 5
LCM = 2 × 9 × 5 = 90
(b) 84 and 126
84 = 22 × 3 × 7, 126 = 2 × 32 × 7
highest: 22, 32, 7
LCM = 4 × 9 × 7 = 252
(c) 20, 45, and 75
20 = 22 × 5, 45 = 32 × 5, 75 = 3 × 52
highest: 22, 32, 52
LCM = 4 × 9 × 25 = 900
Think For 60 and 90: GCF × LCM = 30 × 180. Compare that with 60 × 90. Coincidence? Use the Venn diagram to explain.
Both are 5 400. The GCF is the middle of the Venn diagram and the LCM is everything, so GCF × LCM uses the middle twice plus each side once — exactly the primes of 60 times the primes of 90.
Math History1803 · 2024 · 2245

The cicadas that keep an LCM calendar

Periodical cicadas live underground for years, then millions climb out in the same spring. Brood XIX comes out every 13 years. Brood XIII comes out every 17 years.

In 2024 they came out together, in a strip of central Illinois: the first time since 1803, when Thomas Jefferson was president.

How many years apart are the times they come out together? When is the next one?
13 and 17 are both prime, so LCM = 13 × 17 = 221 years. The next time is 2024 + 221 = 2245.

Quick reference: GCF or LCM?

GCF greatest common factor
  • the biggest number that divides into all of them
  • primes in every factorization
  • each to its lowest power
  • the middle of the Venn diagram
  • never bigger than the smallest number
60 and 90 → 30
LCM least common multiple
  • the smallest number they all divide into
  • primes from any factorization
  • each to its highest power
  • everything in the Venn diagram
  • never smaller than the largest number
60 and 90 → 180
3

GCF or LCM? Reading a Word Problem

Both tools come from the same prime factorizations, so the real work in a word problem is choosing. Ask what the answer has to do.

GCF the answer divides into each number
  • share things into identical groups, nothing left over
  • cut lengths into the longest equal pieces
  • the largest square tile that fits
warm-up: 24 hot dogs, 32 buns → 8 bags
LCM each number divides into the answer
  • events on different cycles happen together again
  • buy packages until the totals match
  • the first or smallest time both happen
big one: packs of 10 and 8 → 40

Together again: that’s an LCM

Two lights flash on different cycles. They flash together at the common multiples of the two cycles, and the first time is the LCM.

Example 5

GCF or LCM?

Decide whether each problem calls for the GCF or the LCM, then solve it.

(a) A florist has 48 roses and 60 tulips. She makes identical bouquets using all of the flowers. What is the greatest number of bouquets she can make, and what is in each one?
GCF: the bouquets must divide into both numbers.
48 = 24 × 3, 60 = 22 × 3 × 5 → 22 × 3 = 12
12 bouquets, each with 4 roses and 5 tulips
(b) Two lighthouse beacons flash together at midnight. One flashes every 12 seconds and the other every 20 seconds. How many seconds will it be until they next flash together?
LCM: both cycles must divide into the time.
12 = 22 × 3, 20 = 22 × 5 → 22 × 3 × 5 = 60
60 seconds (one minute)
✓

Key ideas

GCF: the primes in every factorization, each to its lowest power. 60 = 22 × 3 × 5, 90 = 2 × 32 × 5 → GCF = 2 × 3 × 5 = 30.
LCM: every prime from any factorization, each to its highest power. 60 and 90 → LCM = 22 × 32 × 5 = 180.
Which one? Splitting into identical groups or pieces that fit into the numbers → GCF. Cycles that line up again, totals that match → LCM.
Sanity checks: GCF ≤ the smallest number; LCM ≥ the largest. Multiplying gives a common multiple, not always the least: 10 × 8 = 80, but the LCM is 40.
