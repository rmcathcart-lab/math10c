Surface Area and Volume of Spheres and Hemispheres

A ball fills exactly two thirds of the cylinder that just holds it, and its peel covers exactly four circles. Today: the volume and surface area of a sphere, the hemisphere (half a sphere, with or without its flat floor), and working backwards from a volume to a radius.

I can…
  • explain why V_{\text{sphere}}=\tfrac43\pi r^3 (two thirds of the snug cylinder) and SA_{\text{sphere}}=4\pi r^2 (four great circles)
  • find the surface area and volume of a sphere, halving the diameter first when that is what I’m given
  • write the hemisphere formulas in terms of r and \pi: V=\tfrac23\pi r^3, curved surface 2\pi r^2, closed surface 3\pi r^2, and decide from the wording whether the flat base counts
  • get the radius from a circumference measurement, r=\dfrac{C}{2\pi}
  • work backwards from a volume to a radius with a cube root
M3 Solve problems, using SI and imperial units, that involve the surface area and volume of 3-D objects, including: right cones; right cylinders; right prisms; right pyramids; spheres.
Warm-up

The Tennis Ball Can

Three tennis balls fit snugly in a can: touching the sides, the bottom and the lid. One ball is about 6.7 cm across.

1
How tall is the inside of the can?
Three diameters: 3\times6.7\approx\boldsymbol{20.1} cm.
2
What is the radius of one ball? Of the can?
6.7\div2=\boldsymbol{3.35} cm for both: the balls touch the sides, so the can’s radius equals the ball’s.
3
Guess: what fraction of the can do the balls fill? \tfrac12? \tfrac23? \tfrac34?
\tfrac23. Can: \pi r^2(6r)=6\pi r^3. Balls: 3\times\tfrac43\pi r^3=4\pi r^3. 4\div6=\tfrac23. With numbers: balls \approx472 cm³, can \approx709 cm³. That is today’s first big idea.
!
The big one. Which is longer: the can’s height, or the distance around the can? Guess first.
A the height
B the distance around
C exactly the same
B: the distance around. Height =3d. Distance around =\pi d\approx3.14d. Since \pi>3, the circumference always wins: \pi(6.7)\approx21.0 cm vs 3(6.7)=20.1 cm. Tap Unroll the string to see it.
A is what almost everyone picks: a tall, skinny can looks much taller than it is wide. C is close (only about 1 cm apart), and it would be exactly right if \pi were 3.
+
Early finishers. How much empty space is in the can?
709-472\approx236 cm³: exactly \tfrac13 of the can, the volume of one and a half balls.

Try it: wrap a string around a ball or a pop can. Is it longer than 3 widths?

Volume of a Sphere

Mika filled a cylinder with 480 mL of water, balanced it above an empty overflow container, and lowered a solid ball straight down into the cylinder until the water stopped spilling. The ball and the cylinder had the same radius, and the cylinder’s height equalled the ball’s diameter. Across three trials the overflow container always held exactly 320 mL and exactly 160 mL always stayed in the cylinder.

The water pushed out is the water the ball displaces, so the overflow volume is the ball’s volume; 320 mL is \tfrac23 of 480 mL, and the 160 mL left behind is the other \tfrac13.

So a sphere’s volume is \tfrac23 of the volume of a cylinder with the same radius whose height equals the sphere’s diameter.
For discussion If Mika instead filled the same cylinder with 900 mL of water under the same conditions (cylinder radius = ball radius, cylinder height = ball diameter), how many millilitres would overflow, and how many would remain in the cylinder?
Overflow =\tfrac23(900)=\boldsymbol{600} mL. Remaining =\tfrac13(900)=\boldsymbol{300} mL. (Pick the 900 mL chip above to watch it.)

The snug cylinder: a formula for every radius

A cylinder that holds a sphere of radius r snugly (the sphere touching the top, the bottom and the curved wall all at once) has radius r and height 2r. Mika’s \tfrac23 relationship then turns the cylinder formula V=\pi r^2h into a formula good for every radius, with no need to repeat the experiment:

V_{\text{sphere}}=\tfrac23V_{\text{cylinder}}=\tfrac23\pi r^2h=\tfrac23\pi r^2(2r)=\dfrac43\pi r^3
Sphere formulas.
SA_{\text{sphere}}=4\pi r^2V_{\text{sphere}}=\dfrac43\pi r^3
Both use the radius. Surface area is in square units; volume is in cubic units.

Why 4\pi r^2? The orange-peel test

Trace four circles with the same radius r as an orange. Peel the orange and lay the flattened pieces of peel inside the circles: the peel fills almost exactly four circles, so the sphere’s surface is 4\times\pi r^2. (Archimedes showed the same thing another way: the sphere’s surface equals the curved surface of its snug cylinder, 2\pi r\times2r=4\pi r^2.)

A circle through the centre of a sphere (the biggest circle you can draw on it) is called a great circle. Its radius is the sphere’s radius, so its area is \pi r^2.

Math Historyc. 225 BCE · 75 BCE

The shape on Archimedes’ grave

Around 225 BCE, Archimedes of Syracuse proved that a sphere is 2/3 of its snug cylinder, in volume and in surface area (counting the top and bottom). A later writer, Plutarch, says he asked for a sphere inside a cylinder on his tomb.

In 75 BCE, over 130 years after Archimedes died, the Roman official Cicero wrote that he found the tomb in Syracuse: a small column poking out of the brambles, topped with a sphere and a cylinder.

Show that the sphere’s surface is 2/3 of the cylinder’s whole surface, ends included.
Cylinder: curved part 2\pi r\times2r=4\pi r^2, plus two circles 2\pi r^2, total 6\pi r^2. Sphere: 4\pi r^2. So 4\pi r^2\div6\pi r^2=\boldsymbol{\tfrac23}.
Example 1

A spherical tank: halve the diameter first

A spherical propane storage tank has a diameter of 5.8 m.

(a) Determine the surface area of the tank, to the nearest tenth of a square metre.
r=5.8\div2=2.9 m.
SA_{\text{sphere}}=4\pi r^2=4\pi(2.9)^2=4\pi(8.41)=33.64\pi
SA=\boldsymbol{105.7}\text{ m}^2
(b) Determine the volume of the tank, to the nearest tenth of a cubic metre.
V_{\text{sphere}}=\tfrac43\pi r^3=\tfrac43\pi(2.9)^3=\tfrac43\pi(24.389)
=32.5187\pi=\boldsymbol{102.2}\text{ m}^3
Use the π key, never 3.14. (a) 4×π×2.9x²ENTER gives 105.6831769. (b) 4÷3×π×2.9^3ENTER gives 102.1604043. (The TI-30XIIS has no x³ key: cube with ^3.)

Example 1 check: radius, not diameter

!
Halve the diameter first. Every sphere and hemisphere formula uses the radius. Putting the diameter in by mistake makes the surface area 4 times too big and the volume 8 times too big.

Hemispheres

A hemisphere is half of a sphere: the solid produced by slicing a sphere exactly through its centre, just as a semi-circle is half of a circle.

When a question asks for the surface area of a hemisphere it means the curved surface plus the flat circular base, unless the base is specifically excluded by the wording (an open bowl, say, or a dome built with no floor).
Example 2

The three hemisphere formulas, in terms of r and \pi

A hemisphere is exactly half a sphere:
V=\tfrac12\left(\tfrac43\pi r^3\right)=\boldsymbol{\tfrac23\pi r^3}
(b) Surface area of a hemisphere = curved surface area + area of circular base
= + =
Curved part =\tfrac12(4\pi r^2)=2\pi r^2; flat base =\pi r^2.
2\pi r^2+\pi r^2=\boldsymbol{3\pi r^2}
(c) Surface area of a hemisphere, if the base is not included, =
Drop the base and only the curved half-sphere is left: \boldsymbol{2\pi r^2}.

Example 3

A circumference measurement gives the radius

A soccer ball is wrapped once around its widest point with a piece of string, which is then pulled straight and measured with a ruler. The string is 70 cm long. Determine the volume of the soccer ball, to the nearest tenth of a cubic centimetre.

The string wraps the great circle, so C=2\pi r=70 cm and r=\dfrac{70}{2\pi}=\dfrac{35}{\pi}\approx11.1408 cm.
Substituting r=\dfrac{C}{2\pi} into V=\tfrac43\pi r^3 gives V=\dfrac{C^3}{6\pi^2}, which avoids rounding r first:
V=\dfrac{70^3}{6\pi^2}=\dfrac{343\,000}{59.2176\ldots}=\boldsymbol{5792.2}\text{ cm}^3
Radius first, then the volume in one line. 70÷(2×π)ENTER gives 11.14084602. Then put the whole radius in brackets: 4÷3×π×(70÷(2×π))^3ENTER gives 5792.194332. The booklet’s shortcut \dfrac{70^3}{6\pi^2} gives the same.

Example 3 check: string, radius, volume

!
Don’t round the radius. The radius is cubed, so a small rounding error grows. Carry every digit (brackets, or the shortcut \frac{C^3}{6\pi^2}) and round only the final answer.
Example 4

The same dome, with and without its floor

Sana and Tavi are each building a hemisphere-shaped storage shed, both of diameter 12 ft. Sana’s shed will include a circular floor built from the same material as the dome; Tavi’s shed will have no floor.

(a) Calculate, in terms of \pi, how much material Sana needs to build her shed.
r=\dfrac{12}{2}=6 ft, so r^2=36 ft².
The floor is built too, so Sana needs the curved surface and the base:
SA=2\pi r^2+\pi r^2=3\pi r^2=3\pi(36)=\boldsymbol{108\pi}\text{ ft}^2\ (\approx339.3\text{ ft}^2)
(b) Calculate, in terms of \pi, how much material Tavi needs to build his shed.
Same r=6 ft, but no floor, so only the curved surface counts:
SA=2\pi r^2=2\pi(36)=\boldsymbol{72\pi}\text{ ft}^2\ (\approx226.2\text{ ft}^2)
Think Which takes more material: Sana’s floor or her curved dome? How many times more?
The dome: 72\pi vs 36\pi, exactly 2 times the floor. The curved part is 2 great circles; the floor is 1.
“In terms of \pi” means leave \pi as a symbol: 108\pi is the exact answer. To see roughly how much that is, 108×πENTER gives 339.2920066.

Example 5

Working backwards from a volume to a radius

A spherical stone sculpture has a volume of 3053.6 ft³. Determine the radius of the sculpture, to the nearest tenth of a foot.

V=\tfrac43\pi r^3\ \Rightarrow\ r^3=\dfrac{3V}{4\pi}=\dfrac{3(3053.6)}{4\pi}=\dfrac{9160.8}{12.56637}\approx728.99
r=\sqrt[3]{728.99}\approx\boldsymbol{9.0}\text{ ft}
Check: \tfrac43\pi(9.0)^3=972\pi\approx3053.6 ft³ ✓
Cube root on the TI-30XIIS: type the index 3 first, then 2nd^ (ˣ√), then the number in brackets: 3ˣ√(3×3053.6÷(4×π))ENTER gives 8.999972433, so 9.0 ft.

Example 5 check: run the formula backwards

!
Undo in reverse order. The formula cubes r first and multiplies by \tfrac43\pi last, so undo the \tfrac43\pi first (\times3\div4\pi) and take the cube root last. A square root here would be wrong: the radius is cubed.
✓

Key ideas

Sphere. V_{\text{sphere}}=\tfrac43\pi r^3: two thirds of its snug cylinder (Mika’s overflow). SA_{\text{sphere}}=4\pi r^2: the peel fills four great circles.
Radius first. Halve a diameter; from a circumference, r=\frac{C}{2\pi}. Use the π key, carry every digit, round only at the end. Area in units², volume in units³.
Hemisphere. V=\tfrac23\pi r^3. Curved surface 2\pi r^2 (2 great circles); closed 3\pi r^2 (+ 1 floor). The base counts unless the wording leaves it out.
Working backwards. r^3=\frac{3V}{4\pi}, then r=\sqrt[3]{\frac{3V}{4\pi}}. Check by putting r back into \tfrac43\pi r^3.
