Prime Factorization, GCF, and LCM

Every whole number greater than 1 breaks apart uniquely into a product of primes — its prime factorization. For a big number, the division ladder keeps you organized: smallest prime first, until you reach 1.
!
Prime factors are a list of primes. 15 divides 4 290, but 15 = 3 × 5 isn’t prime — so it is not a prime factor.
Is 19 a factor of 2 574? 2574÷19ENTER → 135.47… not whole, so no.

(c) The sum of all the prime factors of 43 010 is ____.
Numerical response

GCF and LCM from the prime factorizations

GCF (greatest common factor) — multiply the primes the numbers share, each to the lowest power present.
12 = 22 × 3, 18 = 2 × 32 → GCF = 2 × 3 = 6
LCM (least common multiple) — multiply every prime in either number, each to the highest power present.
LCM = 22 × 32 = 36
!
Sanity check. The GCF can’t be bigger than the smaller number, and the LCM can’t be smaller than the larger number. If the numbers share no primes, GCF = 1 and LCM = their product.
Example 2

The LCM written as a product of primes

The least common multiple of 10 and 21 can be written as a product of prime factors in the form a\times b\times c\times d, where a,b,c,d are primes with a<b<c<d. The value of c is
A 2B 3C 5D 210
10 = 2 × 5 and 21 = 3 × 7 share no primes, so LCM = 2 × 3 × 5 × 7 = 210.
a = 2, b = 3, c = 5, d = 7 → C (c = 5)

Perfect Squares, Perfect Cubes, and the Factor Tree

A perfect square has every prime exponent even.
144 = 24 × 32 → \sqrt{144}=2^2\times3=12
A perfect cube has every prime exponent a multiple of 3.
216 = 23 × 33 → \sqrt[3]{216}=2\times3=6

Why? A square root splits the primes into 2 identical groups; a cube root into 3. A partly filled factor tree still works: whatever is missing is forced by the condition you need.

!
Look at the exponents, not the number. 72 = 23 × 32 is even, but it is not a perfect square — the exponent 3 is odd.

Check (b). The x-th root key: 32nd^3375ENTER gives a whole number, 15 — so 3 375 is a perfect cube.

Sorting: rational or irrational?

Ask: can I write it exactly as a fraction of integers? A root is rational only when the radicand is a perfect power of the right kind — and that includes fractions and decimals like \tfrac{9}{16} or 0.09.

!
A pattern isn’t a repeat. 2.020020002… has a pattern, but no fixed block repeats. Irrational.
!
Decimals under a root: \sqrt{0.09}=0.3 is rational, but \sqrt{0.9}=0.9486\ldots is not.

Remember: a repeating decimal back to a fraction

Multiply by 10, 100, 1 000… — one zero for each digit in the repeating block — so the repeating tails line up. Subtract, and the tails disappear.

Key ideas

Factor first. GCF = shared primes, lowest powers. LCM = every prime, highest powers. Prime factors are a list of primes.
Perfect powers. Square: every exponent even. Cube: every exponent a multiple of 3. Missing factors are forced by that rule.
Rational ⇔ fraction of integers ⇔ decimal terminates or repeats. Otherwise irrational (\overline{Q}).
Radicals. Pin between perfect powers; evaluate exact ones first; compare on a number line (or raise to a common power).
